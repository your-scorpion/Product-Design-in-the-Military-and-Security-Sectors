# Product Design Before the UI Stage

*Max Tsvetkov — 26 July 2020*

---

## Understanding the role

What actually falls inside a product designer's remit is a genuine question, and in this lesson I want to lay out the base competencies without touching prototyping or visual design at all.

Product design is the long process of creating and growing a *single* product, one that is itself made up of several projects. It is not knocking together a landing page, handing it to the client, and forgetting it — it is burrowing into how the product works and how its users behave. This is the mode designers operate in at companies like Yandex, Mail.ru, Kaspersky, Google, Samsung, Siemens, and so on.

Now for the intimidating vocabulary. Surveys, diary studies, user-experience mapping, CJM, usability testing, interviewing, field research, monadic tests, blind testing. Do these words mean anything to you? They are all types of research, and a serious slice of a product designer's competence. I understand that you want to draw — but modern design is not about pixels, it is about processes.

## Terminology

There is the term *UX*, and there is *usability*. In the modern reading, usability is wholly and entirely about the ease of using a product or a website, the clarity of the interface, and for the most part we are talking about digital products. It is a sub-discipline of UX. The two are not interchangeable: usability contributes weightily to UX, but it does not describe the whole of the user's experience. We can put numbers on a site's ease of use with instruments like SUS and SUM. UX also takes in how a product *feels*. A well-known logo adds confidence to using a service, on the back of trust in the brand. Land on a site carrying the Leroy Merlin logo and you trust it more than an identical no-name site — though the reverse is equally possible, depending on how your past purchases at Leroy Merlin went. Or buy a car badged BMW or Audi and you are expecting a superb driving-and-living experience, not reliability; for reliability you pick Toyota or Honda, and nobody is impressed by those.

Beyond usability there are many other sub-disciplines of UX. *Desirability* — how interesting and pleasant a product is — is exactly the gamification everyone chases. *Adoptability* is how easy a product is to *start* using; the term caught on only recently, once everyone grasped that 95 percent of mobile-app downloads end in an instant deletion. *Findability* covers the ease of locating the information you need, and a search engine will care far more about findability than about usability.

UX has no shortage of definitions. One of the popular ones comes from Jesse James Garrett, for whom user experience is built from several planes, running from strategy at the bottom up to the surface. The surface is the finished thing the user touches — the UI. Narrowly, UX is what emerges when three conditions are present at once: business, users, and technology. It is the experience a person gets while interacting with a company, a product, or a service — and usability is its subheading, the narrower science of how comfortable it is to interact with a user interface.

## Why research at all

Steve Jobs, one of the finest designers there has been, could not produce one brilliant idea after another on demand. He rarely nailed the great things on the first try; only the fourth iPhone revolutionised the market, while the first three were a very nice pocket computer and a test of the market.

So, idea number one: even the most experienced designers struggle to produce a great result on the first attempt without research. If we don't verify our ideas before building them, we are building our own *hallucinations*, or someone else's. Consider a designer's working day. Typically the designer sits over a mock-up for hours, for days, tuning it, getting sign-off, scattering buttons across menus, agreeing control behaviour inside the department — and then the end user opens the designed screen, taps a couple of buttons, and leaves after a single minute. Remember how long you spent on your first landing page? Almost certainly more than a day. And the user, arriving on your hard-won page via an ad link, read two words and left, never seeing the ideas, the sly visual solutions, the hidden functionality you had baked in. A vivid test of the point: who knows how to schedule a message in Telegram?

To rein in the creative flight and reliably deliver commissions for good money, we designers reach for a set of tools that let us make the interface better — objectively better, not merely prettier. And by tools I don't mean Photoshop, Illustrator, or a canvas with a brush; I mean tools in the sense of methodologies: ways of finding needs, of generating ideas, of subsequently structuring information. Fix the word *research* in your mind. A client walks in and says they want a mobile-app design. Do we sit straight down and start drawing? That sounds like the opening of a tragic story about how "all clients are terrible."

## The competencies of a product designer

How would I recommend you learn to do web design — the design of sites, portals, services, any web resource? Ask yourself where you draw your experience from, how you form a sense of what sites should be. Getting that experience is trivially easy: just use the internet consciously. Right now you are in contact with an object of web design. Saw a neat trick in an online shop? You remember it and use it in your own projects. Saw an ordering flow done awkwardly? You remember it and don't do that. In games there is the concept of *deconstruction* — a game designer doesn't merely play a game, they grasp the deep principles of its mechanics. It matters that we, likewise, don't merely *use* Yandex and Google but analyse how and why particular controls and blocks of a site were built the way they were.

Design, and IT more broadly, is a creative industry founded on people's creative potential. A product designer is someone who fuses several roles inside one person, according to what the business needs. Here are the roles that can live in one head.

**Industrial design** is responsible for designing physical objects and interior pieces. Most designers of other stripes regard industrial designers as nuclear physicists — the sense being that the folk in the world of *making things* do something brilliant, in a way you won't understand, and had better not touch. It isn't nearly that hard. Industrial designers make furniture, gadgets, machine tools, and lamps, turning out sketches, models, and prototypes while leaning on ergonomics, empathy, and engineering. Take Dieter Rams's radio, which inspired Jony Ive to set the style of Apple. Car design is industrial design too, the very quintessence of mastery in the field: working on a car, a designer can be visual — thinking about ergonomics and form — or an engineer. Scan job openings at car companies and you'll often find the word *design* in the title and nothing but technical engineering skills in the description. Design engineer, in other words. Closer to the visual sits CMF design, responsible for colour, materials, and finishing — also called Color and Trim, or Color and Material. Women tend to hold this post, being more sensitive to tactile stimuli.

**Motion design** is a visual story told with animation: clips, cartoons, channel idents, plenty of 3D. Film title sequences and promo reels are motion design too.

**3D**, as a discipline of its own, is the design of objects in three-dimensional space — needed for films, games, graphic design, and interiors and exteriors.

**Game design** is done by specialists who design games; the output of their work is a design document, a sort of table of game-balance mathematics with a description of the mechanics. There are usually plenty of arguments between a UX designer and a game designer — in my experience, always — because game designers are extremely reluctant to give up decision-making rights, considering themselves the authors of the game and, more or less, the Kojimas of it.

**UX/UI design** is the foundation of the product-design profession. Rather than splitting people into UI or UX designers, we combine the roles in one person, which immediately lets that person take some part in the product's strategic direction. There is still a stubborn association of UX with web design, and it is wrong: a UX designer can make an interface for cosmonauts — and it will not be a website — just as they can make a native app for a rare operating system with some unique approach to entering values. A UX designer must always know the answer to *Why?*, a researcher must always know the answer to *Why (do people behave this way)?*, and a product designer knows both — plus the answer to *How?*. From that we can define the profession: a product designer answers not only the questions about the interface but the question of how the interface will touch every business process. UX, on the whole, is about the user's experience in the context of *any* interface; a web designer covers only the web, and sometimes a bit of mobile. The thesis: a designer is not necessarily a person who draws pictures, and designers can be entirely unconnected to a product's visual side. A designer should strive to take some experience from interacting with the world around them, rework it, and return it to the world in improved form — as a product. The product need not be graphical.

> UI — how the system will look and feel emotionally.
>
> UX — how the system works: its logic and its ease.

Turn to Don Norman, who fathered the term UX from around 1990. He introduced "user experience," and for him it is everything a person senses on contact with anything at all. Interacting with the outside world lets us feel different emotions, and that shapes us. People will overpay for a positive experience — nearly every service priced above the middle is built on this. In a good Italian restaurant with its own wine production you'll be served by groomed people in expensive clothes who put on a show, telling you about a particular bottle, perhaps in Italian so you understand nothing, but with every small thing attended to. Buying cheap boxed wine and drinking it in the half-dark of your kitchen does not give you the same experience. It is cheaper, though.

This is called the *emotional response*, and cultural difference is layered over it: a product can be good in itself, yet if it doesn't fit a nation's cultural particularities they won't buy it. There is also a plane of fit with social values, and one of fit with personal preference. For a relationship between a person and a product to *begin*, all three have to line up. A design course is a product, and if you've begun a relationship with it, then it is most likely in a language you understand, matched to your level, and offering certain guarantees. Everything lined up, and you began using it.

A relationship exists — and now it must be held. This is done through regular, pleasant surprises, which stretch the lifetime of usage over a long horizon. One of the central business metrics answers for this: LTV. Note that a business and its product can carry different, even contradictory metrics. Facebook, for instance, monetises through advertising. Strip out the ads and the *product* metrics rise — the audience is delighted, no ads — but the *business* says, right, and where's the money?

It is time to bring in Don Norman's three levels of emotional design; each one shapes how we perceive the world. The first is **visceral** — how the product looks. The second is **behavioural** — how it works. The third is **reflective** — what story the product tells about me, or what story I can tell with it.

The **visceral**, or intuitive, level is unconscious. You look at the screenshots and the icon in the App Store and feel, within a fraction of a second, whether something answers back somewhere in the depths of your mind. You look at a Sberbank landing page and empathy or aversion rises in you unbidden. Or take the old wooden clock on Grandfather's mantelpiece in the village: it holds far fewer functions than the cheapest modern clock off AliExpress, a plain mantel clock, yet its visceral qualities — deep-rooted, unconscious, subjective feelings — give it far greater value in the owner's eyes. Working at this level, we can use images of children, animals, or cartoon characters to lend something a youthful face, or use colours (red as "sexy and aggressive," black as "austere premium"), shapes, or styles (Art Deco, say) that evoke particular eras.

The second level, **behavioural** — *can I interact with this?* — is usability in its pure form. For a student it is the answer to whether there's time in the evening for self-development and for taking part in lessons, and whether there's the technical means to do so. The behavioural level is the easiest to test: you can precisely quantify how satisfied you are with the lessons and what problems you hit in the platform's interface.

Because it is the level you can actually put numbers on, it is worth pausing to ask what those numbers are — a question I get constantly, and one people usually phrase as *is there a mathematical way to judge an interface, rather than the usual empirical like-it-or-not?* There is, and it splits into two axes: what the experience *feels* like, and how *fast* it is.

On the felt side sit the questionnaires, and they divide by *when* you administer them. After a specific task you use the short, sensitive ones — ASQ, SEQ, SMEQ, ER, UME — where SMEQ and SEQ are the most sensitive of the set. After the whole test or study you reach for the longer instruments: SUS, PSSUQ, QUIS, SUMI, UMUX, SUPR-Q, UEQ. Loyalty to the brand is its own axis, measured with NPS and CSI. For satisfaction specifically, SUS, CSI, and SUPR-Q do the job; of the pair of questionnaires, SUS leans toward usability while SUPR-Q leans toward UX — and SUPR-Q is the most popular metric right now, built with a focus on digital products and thoroughly validated. SUS itself is a plain ten-statement questionnaire on which the respondent rates agreement from 1 (completely disagree) to 5 (completely agree), yielding a single number from 0 to 100; typical statements are "I think the app is convenient to use" and "I found the app needlessly complex." SUM costs proportionally more in time and effort but returns proportionally more information, and UMUX-Lite is often used to forecast SUM — the direct alternatives being UEQ and PSSUQ (the latter simply a touch longer). I'd single out UMUX-Lite: easy to obtain, universal, just two questions on a 1-to-7 scale — "the product's capabilities meet my requirements" and "the product is easy to use." Among the "academically" trustworthy, QUIS stands out, though it is paid ($50–750) and, once ninety questions, is now twenty-seven; and PSSUQ, born inside IBM and free, covers four of the base usability characteristics across eighteen questions — how quickly a task can be done, how easily one learns the interface, functional adequacy, and the volume of relevant information — leaving the fifth, rapid learning for productive work, uncovered (ASQ is close in structure). A word of warning on the ancient USE and CSUQ: don't use them, because respondents lean toward agreeing rather than disagreeing, so questionnaires offering only positive answer options return skewed results. On NPS there is a real quirk worth knowing: some argue that NPS correlates directly with a company's share price, which is exactly why senior leaders care about it — and on the 0-to-10 scale, Russians reflexively give a 5, because from their school days a 5 is a *good* mark, so dropping the 5 from the scale nudges the average up almost every time. CSI, as a satisfaction measure, is assembled simply — you define the criteria that matter to the buyer and assign each a weight, gather ratings from 1 to 5 after the user passes each contact point, and compute CSI as the sum, over criteria, of the criterion's weight times its average rating. Banks love collecting NPS and CSI, phoning customers to ask.

The speed axis is trickier, because the same site will, all else equal, load at different rates on new and old devices, and the moment content *appears* can differ too. There is the raw `load` event, but it is too technical — it marks the full load of the page, and content may be waiting on a script that authorises its display, a script that may itself load at the very last moment; content can also be personalised. The vocabulary to reach for instead: **TTFB** (time to first byte — a request was made, the server answered); **LCP** (the arrival of the key content); **SSR** (whether pre-rendering exists, which affects both TTFB and UX — you don't enjoy it when text changes font and every block jumps around during load); **CLS** (which measures layout shifts during load); **RUM** (real-user monitoring); **FID** (time from the first click to the first response to that click); **FMP/FCP** (time to the first readable content); **TTI** (the first interactive interaction, at minimum a scroll); and **TBT** (the gap between FCP and TTI). A simple read is the ratio of CSR to SSR — front versus back, loosely: with LCP at 10s and TTFB at 3s you get roughly a 70/30 split. For a fast start, three suffice: LCP (how long to paint the largest element, such as a video or image — under 2.5s is good), CLS (visual stability, the absence of jumps, usually during lazy loading or async), and INP (how far the user can interact without lag while content changes, ideally under 200ms). Google's PageSpeed will give you the real-world load picture.

And the third level is the hard one — the **reflective**, conscious thinking. Here people decide what a product can say about them to the world. People buy a Rolex to broadcast their financial status; buy an Apple Watch and it says they are Apple fans, in the loop, at the crest of technology. This is the strongest level: people will happily put up with difficulty and awkward usability because they believe they'll get valuable *non-functional* advantages. The first Apple Watch was a solid mass of functional problems and strange usability, and it did not stop the company from taking the second-largest revenue in the world's watch industry within its first year on sale — and that isn't even the most striking example. To my mind the most striking is the sheer fact that 95 percent of the world's population smoked between the 1980s and the 2000s. If any reader ever tried a cigarette, they remember their first, and it was plainly not "wow, what a wonderful sensation, the best experience of my life" — especially for women. After the Second World War women gained more equal rights; before it, they rarely smoked. So this is an example of tobacco companies working the reflective level to sell: you smoke, therefore you back equality. In short, the reflective level is about a person's desire to *present* themselves somehow. Goods that lean on it are very easy to spot: at launch they are always in short supply — everyone has heard of the queues outside Apple stores, or the pre-orders for expensive Ferraris — and the instant those goods appear in every shop, the price falls, because the supply–demand balance has shifted. People experimented many times with books, simply marking them "out of stock" on a site; once the book came back, it sold at double the price and moved beautifully. When the UAE banned the film *Borat*, every record for pirate-DVD purchases of it was broken. Or take jams: put 24 on the shelf and the choice is too large, so only 3 percent buy, drowned in abundance. Playing on scarcity is almost always the reflective level. If you have no competitive advantages and can compete only on price, scarcity is the easiest way to set yourself apart.

If you *do* have advantages, you should be developing the "unfair" kind — the ones competitors find hard or impossible to copy. Patents, for example, work far better than a mere USP.

The plain conclusion: people don't buy goods and services. They buy their *attitude* toward something — a story, and a little magic. Social approval of something sells especially well.

All three levels — visceral, behavioural, reflective — you want to visualise for yourself and keep close at hand. There are many ways to structure and picture this kind of knowledge — building a CJM, creating personas, and so on — which we'll come to later. The idea is simple: you place the three principal needs at the head and build the user's relationship with the product out of them. **An example.** A person is hungry and tired and passes a kiosk. Here they'll want a snack and won't say no to a chocolate bar — visceral, the plain satisfaction of a need. They buy it, eat it, and realise they've eaten a heap of sugar and nothing more; on the behavioural level they feel a bit unwell, having yielded to a fleeting want and done their health a disservice. And given that associating oneself with a healthy lifestyle is fashionable, they'll also be hoping none of their acquaintances saw them eat that lump of sugar — the reflective level.

So who is a product designer, really? UX is a profession at the join of the analyst, the engineer, the psychologist, the manager, the executive, and the artist. I'd put it that they are a *consulting detective after many sessions of supervision* — that definition sits closest to me. The detective is needed for investigations, research, and analytics; the consultant is about common sense and working with tested patterns of user behaviour, about the ability to help management reach its stated goals, or at least to keep it from erring.

One more thing about UX. People have a single simple instinct — we want to rest and to enjoy ourselves. We work and study in order to rest. Everything we do is for the sake of rest. This is the foundation of UX: to minimise the cognitive load on our user. That, precisely, is our job as specialists.

The buyer's brain is forever hunting for ways to rest. Imagine you've won the lottery and been promised $500,000 in five years — but the company offers to buy that promise out, paying you here and now. How much should they pay? The present value is $500,000 / (1 + 0.1)⁵ = $500,000 / 1.61 ≈ $310,460. Would the winner accept, say, $400,000 today rather than $500,000 in five years? Almost certainly — and even the strictly rational floor is lower than that, because today's $310,460, placed in a bank at 10 percent a year, turns into exactly $500,000 over those five years. The brain would rather have the smaller certainty now than the larger effort later.

## Solving problems

Here is how the work goes: at the start there is always a problem the users have; we find that pain, create a solution, draw schemes in design tools or even in pencil on paper — one way or another we find the optimal solution and make a prototype. A prototype is a functional version of your idea, and it exists to be tested; without that it is simply pointless. You try the prototype, realise you were wrong, return to the design stage, and iterate — until you find the optimal solution, or until you realise the market doesn't need the product.

The most classic picture of user pain is people walking a worn dirt path instead of the paved road: a pram is awkward to push along it, and I'll say nothing of the ice in winter, still less of the powdery dust. There are two ways to solve it — pave the path, or put up fences. Confirm that the pain demands a solution and that people will pay for it, and up comes the word "wow." "Wow" is a manipulative bit of marketing — wow-product, wow-landing, wow-prize, wow-salary — a small, light, pleasant surprise, where we see something in a product we didn't expect and say, oh, neat. A phone launched without buttons — wow; a screen launched without bezels — wow. Which is why the effect is short-lived. Living, successful products keep evolving and keep issuing these little wows, and that sustains the desire to own them. Yandex Maps learned to show building entrances on the map — wow! — though 2GIS could do that two years earlier. The key idea: products evolve and are constantly topped up with needed, useful functionality, and it is exactly this that lets you build a long-term relationship between the customer and the product — which is what product designers do.

Another example: Tinder, whose defining trait is a convenient mechanic — handy, fast, simple. That much is obvious, but there are deeper moves. The usage scenario is dealing out cards, and we all know how to deal cards, so the scenario is intuitive to everyone. The system never tells its user that nobody likes him and every girl swiped left; it reports only the positive responses. A positive experience, and a steady stream of little wows.

Experience can be negative too. A gap between expectation and reality is always a negative experience, interfaces included: press "join the webinar" and get an error instead of a webinar, and that is a negative experience. The example is elementary, but it lets you start thinking in the register of the user's emotions — and that matters, because emotions are the driver of sales. Without them any business is just price-dumping, and not all of your clients (companies) will be able to compete on price. So the designer's work is the search for *additional value* that helps sell. Take Starbucks, who sell not the best coffee for a lot of money and yet have a permanent queue: Starbucks sells *on* coffee and earns *on* coffee, but what they sell is an image and a way of life. Design is meant to help sell and to create added value — and yet it is deeply anti-capitalist, and the profession is about helping people. IKEA and Zara bet on design while striving to make things *accessible*; they don't make a unique little chair or blouse for a billion, because that is fashion, not design.

By now the neural connections around user experience should have formed — every example was leading here — and we consider experience not in the context of a *person* but in the context of a *usage scenario*, the so-called user flow.

### Talking to the client

Who do we work for? Somebody. In the modern world everyone works for someone. Whatever a person is — a businessman, a freelancer, an office employee, the president of a country — every one of those roles works for a particular client. The phrase "I work for myself" is incorrect: a freelancer works for their clients, businessmen work for their buyers and their staff. That is how the economy works, and so you cannot ignore the wishes of the client, or of the client's clients. What does a client want from a designer? Usually to get a larger sum of money in exchange for some sum of money. And exactly how — that's a question for us.

Here you could end the story and sit down to draw mock-ups, but the theme of *how to talk to a client, and what difficulties arise*, is far from trivial. Einstein put it well: "If I were given a month to solve a problem, I'd spend twenty-eight days setting it." In essence we have to structure a squall of scattered information and work out what the client really needs. It happens, at the talking stage, that you discover there's nothing to draw at all — perhaps the whole product is a simple SMS with a code.

Not everything is solved with visual design. A store's customer churn may be caused not by a hideous visual style but by poor work from the sales staff — perhaps they dress oddly, or their conversation script is dismal. I've run into this often in themed shops, the sporting-goods or survivalist sort.

How do you easily make any designer fail? Give them a logo to do in one day with no revisions. Minimal brief: a strong, dynamic, confident, modern, memorable mark. The result will not match the client's expectations, regardless of the designer's skill. Creative agencies use such abstract briefs deliberately, spreading the task across a couple of dozen creatives who generate ideas, and the ideas that make it into the client presentation get paid for. But that is the world of advertising. Working on a *product*, this approach would simply kill the client's business — meaning we are not allowed to err.

So hammer three core questions into your head: **why? for whom? how?** Take the first: why does a site need a redesign? Perhaps it has aged visually, or the product line has grown and the site's logic needs updating.

**Why?** To your client, the answer is obvious: he wants to earn money and keep the business from dying over the long haul. But then understand that Nike's reason for existing is not to earn money — it is to make sport accessible to all. That is their value, the thing that raises the quality of their customers' lives, and it is precisely the added value that design brings; the inflow of money comes *through* reaching that goal. Profit is not a goal, profit is a metric. Or take banks: each has its specialisation — Sovcombank is pensioners, Home Credit is consumer loans, Alfa is businessmen, Raiffeisen is premium. The point is that a company must become number one *in* something, *somewhere*, *for* someone — find an unoccupied niche, find its blue ocean, the audience that feeds the business. Any other approach works too short-term. And note that banks' products can be functionally indistinguishable; the competition is purely on service and terms. When companies fight inside a *red* ocean, the research approach changes too: with a lot of data readily available, you don't need many interviews. Another example: for music-streaming services the main metric is how much people listen. The company wants to lift that, because listening brings profit. On Spotify the key metric is the amount of time people spend listening; from it flow others, like how often users return, which automatically raises listening time; those break down into lower-level metrics — the number of new-artist notifications, recommendations, playlists created, new songs discovered — and around all of that we wind the financial metrics. And presiding over the whole crowd of metrics are the financial ones: cash flow, liquidity, debt level, profitability by business segment.

The second question, **for whom?** — we must feel the audience we work for very finely: its habits, who the businessman's target audience actually is.

And the third, **how?** — the *manner* in which we'll close the need.

Out of the conversation with the client we should get a piece of benchmarking, a shared understanding of the project's goals, roughly like this:

| Where are we going? | Product metrics | Roadmap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raise revenue by XXX; XXXX in turnover; XX new users | Revenue, LTV, ARPU, ER, CR, Churn Rate, MAU, DAU, WAU, RR, Session Time | Iteration 1-2-3-4-∞? |

On identifying the audience — and people ask me constantly which criteria you use to define a target audience. The most basic are age, geography, income, social status, number of children, and gender, and beyond those you can reach for interests, status, and nearly anything imagination allows; the master criterion is simply the audience you are *most likely to be able to sell to*. The more modern approach is JTBD and personas — understanding what the audience actually wants, because people don't want a gym membership, they want to look good and be healthy, and games are bought not for fun but for escapism, to fill time, or to occupy the kids. Audiences come wide and narrow: tea-lovers are wide; lovers of green tea from one particular Chinese province are narrow, and a very narrow audience is expensive to target. It matters to single out the *core* — those who buy often, a lot, at high prices, the brand's advocates — then the direct audience, who hesitate but use the product, and the indirect audience, who may merely take part in the buying decision without buying themselves. The TOM (Top of Mind) metric helps here, standing for brand recognition and computed simply as the number of people aware of the brand over the whole potential audience; it is a spontaneous-knowledge survey — *what orange-juice brands do you know?* — and the user's first answer is the TOM (going instead by prompted knowledge, you supply the options).

The trap the client almost always falls into is this: ask who his audience is and he'll answer *everyone with money to spend*. As you'd guess, "all people" is the eternal answer to the question of who a client wants to sell to, and he has never heard of a "core audience." In that case, press for specifics. If the client runs an auto-parts shop, you can ask him to pull the summary data on buyers who bought anything this week in the *tyres* section — and it turns out at once that they are men aged 28–36, mostly driving BMWs, who list *phone* as their preferred contact channel.

The chief thing in working with a client is personal contact. Skype will do if you're in different cities, but meeting in the flesh is always better, and talking everything through by voice. Face to face you can ask more questions, watch how the person reacts to your proposals, sketch with them in pencil on a napkin, and get a better sense of their business and its problems. It also pays to read reviews of the client's business in advance. And in person you can tune your empathy — harder to do remotely.

One more thing about meeting in person. Fast-forward and imagine we're at the final stage, the design already drawn. Product designers usually present their work in person, offline, rather than firing a mock-up off by email. The client won't accept your ideas and concepts straight away — that's normal, he isn't an expert and will suggest (to your eye) strange design ideas, and you must *defend* yours. Only never forget that the client usually knows his business and his users better than you do, so his arguments deserve a real hearing.

Back, then, to the first meeting, and its checklist. Work beginning between two people is a partnership, to be treated seriously, so we should look pleasant — no bad breath, no flea-bitten jumper, a smile on the face. Learn as much as you can about the client's business beforehand, because arriving without even knowing whether the client is in oil, or builds rockets, or simply runs a bakery, is deeply unpleasant for him and makes the conversation hard. You can meet in a café, an office, a coworking space — plenty of options. Warm up with some off-topic talk; the client is a person too and wants to learn more about you, and may need your competencies beyond design. Charge your laptop to the brim, bring a notebook and pen, and open every needed tab in advance — your portfolio, a Google Doc for notes, the client's site. (If you're short on disk space on macOS, switch off Spotlight with `sudo mdutil -E -a` and `sudo mdutil -a -i off`.)

Now you've met. Put a warm drink in his hands — people speak more openly holding one, which is exactly why HR offers coffee at interviews. The governing approach at the first meeting is to behave like a psychologist, a doctor: first gather *all* the information, then deliver your verdict; first hear the problem, then formulate your expert assessment. Don't offer a solution on the spot — that upsets people.

Let the client tell you about himself, the business, its origins, the problems it solves, its next growth steps, and what result he wants from working with you. Perhaps he wants to move a particular metric — say, cut the number of returns — and you'll aim all your effort at that. It's a very live scenario that the client has expertly decided the way to cut returns is to make the return form more complicated and awkward. The correct behaviour here is to *research* the question and discover that returns happen because of inaccurate information on the site — and your task will be to persuade the client not to spoil the return form but to improve how content is presented and produced. Perhaps it's enough to fire the ham-fisted content manager.

There's a classic tale from a Manhattan restaurant. It began getting many complaints about slow service, and management decided to compare CCTV footage from ten years apart. They pulled the recordings, looked, and realised the average meal in 2004 ran 65 minutes; in 2014, a mere decade later, it had grown to 115. Whose fault? The restaurant noticed it was precisely the customers' compulsive phone use that stretched the meal — now the customer wants to photograph the food for Instagram, reply to everyone everywhere, and then, inevitably, complain the food has gone cold. The staff aren't to blame; the problem lies in new audience behaviour.

Or problems with booking a doctor: customers don't know which doctor they saw, or who is handling their order. How to solve it? Customers usually work with a specific manager, doctor, or mechanic, so you can put the managers' and mechanics' photos on the site — the customer will find the right specialist more easily by face. It's enough to place the photos on the clinic's or garage's site. To surface insights like this you must discuss not the specific molecules and organisms on a screen but, rather, what the user is meant to *do* on a given screen.

Along the way you'll be thinking about options and about which features the business needs. Features arise two ways: either we already have statistics and analytics, and from that data we decide the customers need a given feature; or, the reverse, we first *invent* that we need a feature (or the HiPPO — the Highest Paid Person's Opinion — invents it for us) and *then* do the analytics.

You can also forecast what happens to the business if nothing is done. Communicating with the client *on the numbers* is a very comfortable posture for a designer. First ask how much the client earns per paying user — say ₽8,000 a month, of which the business keeps 30 percent, the rest going to couriers, logistics, storage, intermediaries — so ₽8,000 × 0.3 = ₽2,400 earned per payer. Suppose people buy at a 1 percent conversion (of 100,000 visitors only 1,000 buy): then from ₽2,400 you strike two zeros and get ₽24. If the dev team costs, say, ₽1,500,000 a month, then ₽1,500,000 / ₽24 = 62,500 users a month you must drive into the service. At once it's clear what to do and what the metrics will be.

Or the client has no quantitative data at all, and then we interrogate him. He wants a property developer's personal-account interface? You must learn *why* the account is needed: how users behave before buying a flat and after, what extra services they need — or what extras the client would like to sell (finishing, five years of plumbing maintenance, a concierge, CCTV) — who buys, private individuals only or resellers, which bank the developer works with, whether there's an exclusive mortgage tie-up with a particular bank. In short, you ask many questions the client himself may never have considered, plus the general ones: what makes you better than the rest, what barriers stand before the business, what stops it growing?

But even when a problem exists and is obvious, it isn't a given that we can fix it. Consider Disneyland, known to all as a living fairy tale of endless fun. Management noticed one of its key metrics falling — and it wasn't money. Visitors had stopped returning; they came once and that was that. All because of the queues. A problem, then, to be solved. The cleverest minds in management gathered, stormed the problem, and left with brilliant ideas for making the park faster, more personalised, queue-free, modernised. And it didn't work. Why? Organisational disjunction. Changing or applying technology is the easiest thing you can do — purely a question of investment. Changing the *culture* inside a company is far harder and far slower. The root of the problem lies in people. We have two decision-making systems: the first fires a fast reaction to external stimuli, the second demands effort and must be switched on consciously. Under stress the signalling system takes over, running a person round the same loop of pattern decisions — the very ones that led to the stress. Keep this in mind: if a client says he's already worked with three designers who all let him down, that's a small alarm bell that the client simply cannot see the real problem inside his own organisation.

As you'll have gathered, a product designer is not a pixel-pusher or a painter of coloured squares — a designer does a great deal of consulting. The definition closest to me right now remains the *consulting detective*, because a designer must know the client well, must investigate him: learn everything about the competitors, the product's life cycle. Will the client maintain the site himself, or will you? Most clients never consider what becomes of your work a year on. If the client wants a blog like Tinkoff's, your first question is whether there'll be people to fill it with content regularly and watch the comments. Or a seller of cheap property wants a site like the ones luxury developers have — but the behaviour of an economy/pragmatist audience and the expectations of a "business"-class audience differ sharply, to say nothing of the buying process itself.

This is roughly why a client will often already have a KPI tracker of some kind — a spreadsheet of daily targets against actuals: a dairy target versus total dairy sales, an eye-tests target versus the actual number performed, conversions against a 70 percent goal, contact-lens targets, an average-order-value target against the real AOV. It's rarely pretty and rarely complete, but it is a gift, because it tells you which numbers the business already watches and where reality is falling short of the plan.

> **Thesis.** A professional designer immerses themselves thoroughly in the client's business, interviews real users of the products, and enters into empathy.

So the first meeting has one job: hear the client out and ask the right questions. Remember the clarifying ones — the client will rarely hand you the information you need in concentrated form, free of a heap of extraneous fact. Don't forget to lay out how *you* see the project and where you'll be heading. Be sure to establish timelines, what's achievable within budget, and, above all, what the budget is. Describe the process. Explain what the business gets specifically from your involvement — perhaps the client doesn't in fact need a designer to solve the problem, but simply needs to fire the content manager. Or, again, the client wants more orders through the site and asks you to redraw the order form, when you can grasp at the first meeting that the form is fine and the trouble is not design but search: the site was penalised and orders are scarce. (If you want to play with briefs, [goodbrief.io](http://goodbrief.io/) generates them.)

At the first meeting you capture everything, but during the conversation don't stare only at the laptop, don't text on your phone; *be* at the meeting, undistracted, showing with your whole bearing that you're maximally engaged — and ideally not merely showing it but truly engaged, because what the client says matters more than your note-taking. You can use a dictaphone, agreed with the client in advance, or capture the key ideas in quick text notes. And straight after the meeting write down everything you forgot — every photo, scrap of paper, napkin prototype, note — moving it all into one document.

After the meeting, send the client a report on it; every participant in the meeting and the sign-off chain must feel involved, since they're about to work with us.

Once it's over, digest everything, gather all photos and references into one folder, rewrite the comments — this is exactly the basis of the *brief*. A brief is a fairly complex document holding the decision-maker's contacts, the company name, marketing and strategic information: a description of the brand's goals, a detailed statement of tasks with every nuance and every comment from the correspondence, examples of competitors' work or simply the general stylistics — everything you can lean on, plus all supporting materials like logos, colours, fonts, brand books, source files. It matters to spell out what the client ultimately gets and in what form: if illustrations, then state sizes exactly, print-fitness, whether raster elements are allowed.

## Beginning the research

We've talked to the client and drawn up the brief; the next step is to find a way to do the client's business irreparable *good* and raise the company's value. This is an important stage of development that stirs heated debate in the profession. Most designers can't and won't research until prototypes or a final design exist. It's far from every company that has you researching before implementation begins — but the market is inching that way. The designer looks at competitors, gathers material to build hypotheses, transforms product hypotheses into research ones, and studies the user's pains and the environment the product is used in. Weeks will pass between receiving the brief and the first mock-up.

There's a classic moment from a design competition where a participant confesses he didn't grasp that the *goal* of design is to solve problems, not to draw. Again, the idea I'm pressing on you: design is not pictures, it is processes — and pictures are the *result* of working on processes, one possible result.

On the subject of research, then, start with the simple and obvious: you must become an expert in the field the product is made for. At companies building complex products for administrators or cosmonauts, every participant in development goes through onboarding on the product, or you plainly won't understand what's being worked on. Land in a very complex sphere — information security, say — and you take extra courses, external ones, watch YouTube lectures, immerse yourself. You aren't obliged to master a new industry at expert level, but you must roughly grasp its terminology and the essence of the profession — also needed for market analysis. Incidentally, "raising the company's value" is not a vague flourish: capitalisation is share price times the number of shares in circulation, so that when Sber's capitalisation passed ₽8 trillion it meant Sber, at that moment, was worth ₽8,000,000,000,000 — and as demand for the share rises, so does the price. That is the number, in the end, your irreparable good is meant to move.

Research used to be done only when launching a new product. Before 2000, for instance, the camera market barely changed — all cameras were alike, with unremarkable spec tweaks — and nobody researched before a new camera shipped; it was clear enough who'd buy it and why. But the economic situation has changed and consumer culture has matured. Everyone now has a camera in their smartphone, competition has become intense, and from that arose the need to understand the consumer thoroughly and to build the product *from* the audience's desires. It so happens that designers are most competent at this — we, precisely, know how to make a person feel good — so we do the research. Salespeople, managers, and marketers will help; they too know about user needs, but each from their own bell tower, and none of them thinks about UX.

By pre-project research, businessmen mean depth interviews conducted when the product doesn't yet exist. The interview is the foundation for every other kind of research — without it the others can't be done properly — and it is an *unformalised* personal conversation. There are exceptions; a need may be created by a new law about to be passed. The frequent objection to interviewing is the Ford line about the peasant who, asked what he needs, would ask for a faster horse. Ford never actually said it, but the illustration is clear and apt — and there *is* a real Ford quote, from his diaries: "My secret of success lies in the ability to grasp another person's point of view and to see things both from his and from my own."

The difference is in approaches. We *search for a need*, observing people's inductive and compensatory behaviour during research. The oldest, artisanal way a product comes to be is the chain need → product → market: there was a need for a hammer to drive nails, a product was created, and it created the market for building tools. During the Industrial Revolution the world took another path, product → need → market. Programmers have their own, product → market → need. We, meanwhile, walk the path need + product → market. It's no wonder, then, that many of the world's top design leaders champion interviewing — though there's also the genre classic of top managers sitting in an office declaring they've worked twenty years and know it all. Working with *them* is a topic for a separate lecture — and the way I've come to move such decision-makers is to appeal to the science, because "wrong" questions really do produce wrong answers, and there's a body of work to prove it. Fritz Heider described causal attribution — respondents interpret an external situation through their own experience and view when they lack information, the way a homemaker interprets a relative's conduct at a job she's never seen. Ulric Neisser showed that people cannot objectively register the influence of advertising and other factors on their choices, and will even aggressively deny that influence, unable to see their own motives: they bought the Snickers not because of the ad but "just because chocolate." And Edward Thorndike described the halo effect, by which a general impression smooths over particulars — beautiful people are taken for cleverer, tall people for stronger, and so on. Hand a stubborn manager those three names and the conversation changes.

Interviewing carries a large margin of error, because the sample is small — from five people up. The people must be *representative*. Representativeness: take the population of Eastern Europe as the general population, and to learn its average attitude to China you draw a few people at random from every region of every country and ask. The assumption is that their answers represent the whole. If five people from Murmansk say they love China, does that mean Murmansk loves China? Not really — so there is a chance of error, resolved by the number of respondents and the right selection. I rely on a Gaussian sample: 30 respondents per cell. The second problem is absenteeism — a given group's unwillingness to take part — which we resolve with additional, mostly *quantitative* research to validate the hypothesis. And when that quantitative validation must run at federal scale, over an enormous audience, the answer is to find CATI centres — computer-assisted telephone interviewing — which keep trained interviewers, the equipment to store survey data, and software to analyse and export to SPSS and Excel.

> Know it — do it and propose it.
> Don't know it — ask and research.

## Recruiting

For an interview, and for any research at all, you must assemble a *relevant* sample — perhaps a deliberately biased one with a set of characteristics. Physics has the notion of *homogeneity*, uniformity of composition: take a group of people with a single profile and research only within that profile.

And how do you know you've assembled a relevant, homogeneous, representative sample? You grabbed five schoolfriends — is that a relevant sample? Obviously not. Socio-demographics are not the chief set of parameters when selecting respondents; the chief one is *awareness*, or the degree of the product's presence in a person's life. The classic example: people who own a bank card were invited and asked which criteria they'd used to choose it — and the answer was *none, the card came with the salary project*. We don't need such people; they must be filtered out at recruiting. What we'd have wanted is the person who constantly travels to Europe and chooses a card by whether it's Mastercard or Visa, because the cash-withdrawal fees differ.

When testing new functionality in a working service, it matters to hunt both experienced users and novices. That's a hygiene rule.

And here we touch recruiting proper: how to assemble that sample, how to find people. There are two ways — through an agency, or rolling up your sleeves and searching yourself. Agencies work well; you prepare the question list in advance and send it over — outfits like UXSSR, RusOpros, ADS. Recruiting yourself, mind the *walkers* — people who earn a living going from study to study, lying about their product experience to get onto your interview. You sieve them with decoy questions like "are you an active user of our direct competitor, PutiApps?" — while PutiApps doesn't exist; these are the so-called honeypots. Recruiting is hard. The temptation will be to just ask friends, colleagues, relatives, but if they aren't representative, that's a blunder. Your respondent must also be an *expert in the problem* they have, or they're a poor respondent. If you can bring in specially trained people to recruit, I'd recommend it. Agencies are easy to find: [ESOMAR](https://www.esomar.org) is the international association of researchers — pick your country and you get a list of agencies that recruit there — while the more popular ones on the Russian market are [uxalliance](http://www.uxalliance.com/) and [uxfellows](https://www.uxfellows.com/), and you can ask agencies for discounts, haggling is fair. Another useful link is [qrca](https://www.qrca.org/), where you can find yourself a colleague on a foreign market.

The temptation will also be to offload your work onto the marketers. But marketers research the market from the standpoint of an *already-made* product: here is popcorn, and they look at how else to sell it, how to shift the public's attitude to it — how demand changes if you drench it in strawberry syrup or shape it like little telephones, how to hook new cohorts on popcorn. Crudely, the marketer's job is to flog a product by telling the story that the product exists on the market, which is why marketers are excellent at extracting big budgets and, given their way, would spend the whole company on advertising — but the commercial director won't allow it. Product research is the *design of new products before development begins*, so we research when we want to change or create something. The design team makes useful, desirable products grounded in user research; marketers and analysts, meanwhile, research whether the potential-user market is large enough and whether income will cover costs. When the product is in development, the marketers prepare the launch strategy with you, based on the value they found — value that can be functional, financial, social, or psychological, and that should strengthen one of the following: corporate resilience, operational resilience, strategic resilience, financial resilience, or economic resilience. Want to meet SDG/ESG criteria for tax breaks? That lifts financial resilience. Move into digitalising the business? That strengthens strategic resilience. So, to launch a major feature you must estimate the market's size, estimate the product's size, and think through pricing — which we do together with the marketer.

If you need respondents across several countries at once, you can dig yourself deep. Searching solo, [craigslist.org](https://moscow.craigslist.org/) helps a great deal — US UX designers post ads there looking for respondents. You can post on a freelance exchange (even Yandex used to), or post recruiting ads on social media, or simply go through acquaintances.

## Working with an agency

You can hand the research to agencies — they want it. They'll do it all and send a report with the takeaways, but… you yourself won't level up, won't sink into the topic. The report will be made to the *agency's* template, not as you'd have wanted or pictured it. Another argument for doing research yourself: agencies often hand the work to junior specialists to cut costs, which hits quality hard — you deal with a seasoned, professionally ingratiating account person, while a beginner does the actual work.

American and Russian agencies differ sharply. Post-Soviet agencies work to the brief, following the questions and scenarios you gave them (if you gave them). American agencies ignore the spec and do what they like, citing their super-expertise, and do it very slowly and expensively — then hand you a recommendation on what to improve in your product. You wanted to learn how people use the taskbar in Windows, and you're told the Start menu isn't needed. It comes out closer to consulting than to commissioned work, and they take offence if you try to steer them back to the original task. That's a concrete warning about what to expect specifically from American agencies, so spell out the goal of the agency's work in the contract if you decide to hand research over entirely. This is my experience; others' may differ.

There will be hard-to-find people — a fine-grained disease topic for a pharma project, or a farm in a particular district with a particular specialisation, or a usability test with several categories of stakeholder needing four or five people in each, urgently. Then agencies rescue you on the *best-efforts* method: you order twenty respondents and they offer back the smaller number they can find in the time. A bad turn is also possible, where the agency takes money to find twenty, finds ten, and doesn't refund — arguing the payment was for *time*, not result. Those are horror stories about foreign agencies; Russian ones search until they find, or don't get paid.

However you find respondents, they may carry one of the following problems.

The **timid** fear giving an honest negative answer and always try to say the socially desirable thing; you must repeat many times that we are *specifically* hunting for problems and it matters to surface any difficulty, however small. The **talkers** love straying into long musings — closed questions exist precisely for them, to bring them back into context. The **slow** are in no hurry, speak very slowly, think a great deal, and stretch time out; with them it matters to quicken your own pace and nod actively. The **upset** worry keenly about their mistakes during testing — to this category belong the people in the coming section on ethics — and it matters to support them, reminding them we're testing the product, not the person's skills. The **unsure** re-question their every action, forever seeking approval; don't rise to the provocation, and ask *why do you think you did something wrong? what raised the doubt?* The **expert** is our favourite type in B2B, telling you at once how it should be done — advice of the "bigger button here, logo centred, larger font" variety — so clarify *why* he considers that a good solution. And the **silent** answer in monosyllables, thinly and uninformatively, so you must ask as many clarifying questions as you can.

## Ethics

Now a little on the moral side of working with a respondent. Interview questions can reactivate traumatic experience in some people, which is very live for fintech and medicine. Say a person unable to walk lives in a village, and it's a serious problem for them that there's no grocery delivery. We ask about it; they answer, dwell on it, take in all the difficulty, and slide into depression. Or we ask about saving for a child's education in a family with a combined income of ₽15,000. The examples I've given are grave and obvious, but you must also think about the less obvious — the absence of certain things at home, or of any travel experience — and you never know in advance which question will be sensitive. That is exactly why the in-person meeting matters more: on a phone interview it's harder to catch a person's reaction to a question. The researcher's ethical nuances are laid out at [esomar.org](https://www.esomar.org), and the discipline overall is called the *sensitive field*.

Working with the premature loss of relatives, understand that this is a very high-risk group: loss of a loved one plus prolonged grief disorder equals the risk of breaking a person's life. It is resolved by prior screening — it matters to select respondents without complicating circumstances. If you do run into it, there are options. The first is a *state assessment*: you ask the respondent to rate on some scale how it went, a technique borrowed from psychologists. The second is the *extended goodbye*: you ask a few additional, relaxing questions. Say you'd been discussing how a person's data can leak online and drastically affect their life — hackers, blackmail, and so on — and the person has dwelt on it and formed a gestalt; at the interview's end you say there is, on the whole, no such risk, we're simply researching the question, looking to improve our product. It's an un-typifiable situation, and the closing will differ every time.

## Planning and processing interviews

So you've run the interviews and have a mass of data. Now what? You'll want to dump every idea straight into the backlog — not the cleverest move; you need to spend time analysing the results. Run three interviews a day for a week and that's fifteen, and you'll be forgetting some of what respondents told you. For processing I once again recommend Miro or any other online whiteboard — you won't be able to write everything down anyway, and real-time clattering on the keyboard irritates the respondent. The second option is working through audio, which is slow but can be outsourced for money at [zapisano.org](https://zapisano.org/) — and analysing a one-hour interview *with* clustering, even without transcription, will take two hours. By the numbers again: an interview runs 1.5 hours, plus about 20 minutes' break and at least 30 minutes beforehand to check the equipment; analysis takes 2 hours and up (1–1.5 with a transcript, 2–3 without); clustering depends on the task. Recording via phone runs a high risk that a call or notification interrupts it, or that the app caps the recording length, so use an external microphone, or switch to aeroplane mode — and go carefully with VoWi-Fi, quality over thrift.

Working with transcripts is simple enough: open the text and copy the useful bits onto stickies on the whiteboard. On average, one interview should yield 50 to 100 stickies. You group them — and that isn't all; next you *pack* the data into a CJM, a value-proposition canvas (pains, jobs, gains), JTBD, a Business Model Canvas. Choose the framework in advance; it lets you move toward the goal more consciously — not just prattle on, but lead the conversation with a plan. A natural question at this point is *when* you stop analysing by hand and move to mathematics, and the standard rule of thumb wandering the market is a decent guide: at 1k data points, heuristics; at 10k, ordinary models; at 100k, neural networks.

This is how we form the information base — and the most important base sits in *your head*, your neural connections. You all have experience buying in online shops, working with educational portals, working with mobile apps; analyse that knowledge. Perhaps you're wholly unfamiliar with the theme — oil, space, information security — and then you need a consultation with professionals in it; it may concern building rockets, cars, defence, and then you go and learn, though I'll say plainly that without consultants you still won't be able to work. Either way: gather the information, then sort it onto shelves — with mind maps, flowcharts, CJMs, JTBD, personas, any way of structuring and storing information. I'll repeat once more: Miro — master this tool without fail, it's the best on the market and international; its alternative is [mural.co](https://mural.co/).

Research may turn up that the market doesn't need the product, that the client's idea is loss-making by definition. Our job is not to fear telling the client. Some designers shrug this off — *I did my work; that the product doesn't earn is the client's fault, he can't do his job (earning), and I did mine.* No: the designer is at fault. In industrial design, if a designer makes a product whose design flaws kill people, the designer bears criminal liability — not the manager who sketched a wheel without an airbag on a scrap of paper, but the designer who obeyed him. In truth the responsibility in such a case falls not on one but on three — those who conceived, checked, and approved — and one of them is the designer. If the idea is doomed, our job is to find growth points for the client's business, not to help him go bust. Take Post-it notes: people were merely trying to make a super-strong glue and got a very bad one; the author of the bad glue said it was a glue for bookmarks — and now we have Post-its, design thinking, and all the rest. The second turn of events is when the idea is *not* doomed but a more promising direction exists — also important to tell the client. Everyone knows Viagra is a remedy for hypertension, angina, and other heart conditions; it's famous for its interesting side effects.

During the research we surfaced and formed personas. And now a large block — competitive analysis.

## Competitive analysis

A competitor is *any* way a user can solve their problem. Say you provide medical services, a private clinic. Who's your competitor? Other private clinics — but also state clinics, online doctor services, Google with its heap of medical articles, non-classical practitioners in private practice, gyms, pharmacies, mobile apps with health-monitoring and data-based advice. Another example: you open a shawarma kiosk on the city's central street with no other food point around. What price do you set? It seems you can jack it sky-high — no competitors, after all. But there are vending machines, grocery shops, food delivery. There will always be *indirect* competitors.

Direct competitors are companies whose main product is practically indistinguishable on any parameter — Delimobil and Yandex.Drive, or two rival brands of sour cream. Indirect competitors (competitors by JTBD) target your audience, but the product itself differs in price, features, positioning — Adobe Stock and image search in Yandex, say: you find a photo in Stock, then drop it into Yandex and find it without the watermark. The products aren't competitors, yet they solve one job. A vivid example of indirect competition: the US market had a giant, Blockbuster Inc. — DVD rental and the like, an utterly polished operation and business model. Then Netflix appeared, a tiny player, with an entirely dissimilar model — mailing DVDs and keeping only a website instead of rental points. There were problems: discs broke in transit, extra offices had to be opened across the country. At one point Netflix wanted to sell itself to Blockbuster for $50m and was refused — they didn't believe the model. A year later the internet sped up and Blockbuster was forced to build an online service; a couple of years after that they had to cut prices to loss-making levels to pull people into their offline stores, purely to take them from Netflix. Their economics didn't add up for several years, after which they raised prices again, decided to close in 2010, and were gone by 2014. A further kind is *general* competition, or competitors by acquisition channel, whom you fight for a click in search ads or SEO — industry journals, some of which pull organic traffic away from the companies actually working in the industry and selling their products: with SEMrush, the customer clicks not our article but Search Engine Journal's, and from such an article he can't become a buyer — he peels off onto another road.

As you can see, you have a multitude of competitors — hundreds. Competitive analysis picks out the strong and weak spots of them all and makes the product competitive. There's no sense launching an exact copy of an existing service; there must be a twist. And here the research methods rescue us — online surveys, usability research, a Feature Table — all run across the *whole* life cycle of the product, from its birth to its final month.

A good way to structure the business model is Osterwalder's canvas. Unlike the ordinary Lean Canvas, it foregrounds *relationships* with the customer, especially long-term ones, and the *distribution channel* — where the goods will physically be sold, which is about offline goods: pickup points, couriers. Try to analyse no more than ten competitors per segment in a single study. Note that the sections "key partners," "resources," "activities," "cost structure," "revenue streams," "channels," and "customer relationships" belong wholly to the *business model*, not the product — lawyers for legal compliance in the countries of presence, for instance. An IT product is merely a layer between business processes and consumers.

If you'd rather build the business *around* the product, turn to Product-Led Growth. In it there are three levels of business growth — user acquisition, monetisation, and retention — and the product itself answers for acquisition through virality, marketing, and sales. Obviously this is the SaaS service model, because you have to let people try the product and keep time-to-value low, and it suits only red oceans. Compared through the sales funnel, the classic AARRR — Acquisition → Activation → Retention → Referral → Revenue — is about acquiring users through promotions, marketing, and virality, where growth comes not from the marketing department but from the user's *adoration* of the product and their turning into a brand advocate. The sales-led approach, by contrast, is more the complex corporate story, where the process begins with finding the decision-maker, then a demo, a trial period, purchase, and support.

The MOAT framework lets you make a product successful even without unique features. All products fall into four types: dominant products (cheap and good), differentiators (a niche), disruptors (cheap and bad), and losers (expensive and bad). Examples of disruptors are Google Docs or Canva — inferior to Microsoft Word and Photoshop, but free or cheap.

Having no competitors is, first, a very rare case, like the arrival of a new technology such as blockchain. If you truly have none, then perhaps building the product is too expensive and slow, or there's no user problem people will pay to solve, or the market isn't ready for the solution. There are many articles now about people who launched a product and burned out, with a breakdown of why the product didn't land — very useful reading. Almost all startups die for one plain reason: their product simply isn't needed by the market.

How to look for demand? Ask. You can ask Google first (desk research) — and you must know how to phrase the query: you *could* type "search for parking spots in Moscow," but "how to find a parking spot" is more correct. The second way to ask is to talk to people about how they solve the parking problem *now* — and perhaps they don't want yet another app with free spots marked, but a change in the transport structure as a whole.

## Differentiating from competitors

Study the competitors' interfaces: if everyone's app has search, yours must too. Surface both their weak spots and their strong ones. Nine of ten competitors have a poor product filter and one has a good one? Take the good part, copy it, improve it, skim the very cream.

There's the notion of the *three pillars of uniqueness*, which lets you set yourself apart. If we're repeating competitors — an online shop, and all online shops are more or less alike — we can use different *visual languages*, or an atypical *tone of voice* with the customer. Or take media: there are heaps of online magazines that write brilliantly and at length, and it's hard to create a new publication with something unique — you'd have to compete with a mass of great teams, which raises the question, why compete at all? Let's make an *aggregator* of the best content and compete not on content but on *technology* — there's less competition in the technology environment. Google loves doing exactly this: it produces no content but aggregates data from every source.

## Handing off to development

The most important thing to grasp in this block is that the designer's core job is to *connect* different knowledge from different departments and to track the final result. First you work with the business side, then with the development side. Companies usually hold a great many very different people, with different motivations and different notions of one another's work. To compensate for the wrong notions and expectations about the various roles, work methodologies were created — and they, precisely, let you deliver features to customers on a conveyor, predictably and at constant quality, with quality support, which is itself a quality criterion for SaaS.

Besides managers, analysts, testers, and developers, you'll meet the *Agile coach* — a separate role, or one someone on the dev team takes on. This person must watch the process and the team's inspiration; leave a team to grind on a product unattended and over time they slip into depression and lose interest, so you have to monitor their mood, gather feedback, and keep their interest warm. That's the Agile coach's charge, and he often tries to sustain the *startup spirit*. A startup is a test of a business model — but, as we already know, you must test not only startups but features at large projects too.

A word on business. After launch, a classic business has more outgoings than income; it holds an initial financial resource and needs borrowed funds to grow — capital we invest that later pays back as owners' return. A startup, by contrast, is the *expectation of fast, explosive* revenue growth relative to costs. Need to make more money for the Hilton? It invests in building a new hotel that turns a profit years later. Airbnb needn't build anything — it launches its app on new territory. A hotel without hotels. And the standard rule: a low price means more sales, lower costs, but less profitability; a high price means less traffic, a dearer customer, but higher profit.

This is also where planning gets genuinely hard on a *long* deal cycle, the kind that raises the question of how to run both management accounting and cohort analysis at once. The plan — a sales budget plus an extrapolation forecast — is a purely financial model showing the company in one slice, money, alongside a management balance sheet; once it exists, it's an approved document from which KPIs and norms are computed, and you should regularly be given data on shipments, purchases, stock, and receivables. The simplest formula for the cost of goods and services produced is (time + materials + units) / number of products, where *time* is the cost of labour, *materials* the external resources (advertising, contractors, outside spend), and *units* the resources bought from other departments (reports requested, design ordered). On a long cycle the trap is that you can't get the final picture quickly — by the time the data arrives, half the team has scattered — so you judge by *lead* indicators, the proxy metrics: not clicks and site visits but MQL (marketing-qualified leads); not the number of calls and demos but new MRR (monthly recurring revenue); not letters attempting to win a customer back but retained customers and upsold MRR; not a reconciliation act but an agreed contract. The client's decision-making time matters less than you'd think — you simply give strategy more room, and strategy has a goal that can change. You always have predictable variables: the team's size, skills, and experience; expected company growth; cash flows; metrics; products; jurisdiction. A cheap mass product leans on marketing; an expensive product for the few leans on sales — a business model you can play different scenarios within.

There is a classic development approach called the *waterfall*, which means clear planning and staged development: first analysis, then implementation, and you cannot start implementation without finished analysis. States and large holdings love it — they have the money and time for each stage. One stage ends, the next begins, and only so. It's predictability, with its own spec at each stage, and it suits building something *typical* — modifying an aircraft, say, everything the same but a new engine version. Waterfall builds nuclear plants, icebreakers, standard panel housing, connecting a subscriber to the internet — a cascade model without iterations that works decently under staged advance payment to a contractor, when the project isn't complex and, above all, is very predictable. The process is bedded in; no surprises should arise.

There is a more flexible methodology, Agile, which we use when the task is stated ambiguously. Agile is often taken for chaos, and sometimes it *is* chaos — everyone running from work-stage to work-stage, constantly redoing everything. But there is *directed* chaos, and to give the chaos a goal we introduce the term **MVP**: study the customers' needs and quickly launch a minimal version of the product to satisfy them. Among the less popular models: TDD (test-driven development), where tests are written before code — you first write a test for the feature's functionality and run it with a guaranteed *negative* result, since the feature's code doesn't exist yet, then the developer writes the code and reruns the test; the approach is meant to help write code without errors, though developers dislike writing tests in such quantity. Another is SDL (Microsoft's Security Development Lifecycle), about reducing zero-day vulnerabilities in boxed products — developers must take many information-security courses, requirements are gathered through the lens of reducing the chance of a break-in or the unsanctioned use of features, every other stage marches in step with security, and after release developers must assign "security vulnerability" bugs a high priority.

An MVP is a product of *high quality* and *low grade* with minimal functionality. Knocking a feature together on your knee is not an MVP, it's a botch. The idea sits in the lean-startup approach: the realisation of the *core* value for the consumer and nothing more, built in a week or a month. What's meant by high quality is the middle letter — **V**aluable: the feature must be made well enough that it won't need urgent fixes right after launch.

A telling example is the Yo-mobile, an eco-friendly Russian hybrid — a fine idea. How to build it? Make all the parts and assemble it! Sounds right. But think a moment. We must focus on the *goal*: we want to create eco-transport, so we go and make the minimal working version first. Can we make a car in a month? No. Can we make a scooter? Yes! And you'll say — why make a two-wheeler when we're making a car, a big and interesting project? Here's why: scooters took off and the Yo-mobile did not. The electric scooter found its niche as eco-transport, and it needn't be developed further as a *kind* of transport — it closes the pain. Then you can build a *second* startup for the charging-and-storage infrastructure. A large company with resources and expertise, like BMW, can create an electric car straight away — they're masters of it and have the means. In the Yo-mobile's case, where there weren't enough resources (mostly in the area of competencies), you should walk the path from small to large: learn to touch the market very cheaply and carefully, and produce to demand.

How do you know you should make the scooter and not the car — how do you know what users need, and how do you dare choose the scooter? CustDev: interviewing potential users. First identify the audience, answering, depending on the business type (B2B/B2C), *who is your customer?* — which often overlaps with building personas. Then move to the MVP. It's a mistake to skip CustDev and pin your hopes on analytics from the MVP: you'll never learn, from 300–1,000 bought users, whether their behaviour is the result of seasonality or external motivation, or of flaws in your product. CustDev was popularised by Steve Blank in *The Four Steps to the Epiphany*, and it isn't merely interviewing but an approach in which the company's most important asset is its customers, not its product. And if the idea is a startup and you don't know how to raise investment, the first steps are these: reread this entire blog; then go into accelerator programmes, which are really an ordinary product-management course focused on specific startups — investment in the form of knowledge, and perhaps a little funding. A *grant* is a non-repayable subsidy, sometimes for research; you needn't return the money but must report on its use — bureaucracy — and in return some public benefit and scientific groundwork are expected; grants are a decent start for a startup, but don't forget users and profit. There are also business platforms with perks (Skolkovo — tax and customs breaks, internal grants), though if you're aimed at European or American markets, be careful with jurisdiction. The usual first step is an accelerator to prepare you to pitch investors; if you already have something product-like that works, you can approach venture funds; accelerators often hold a demo day for pitching. You can write to investors directly on social media, meet them at conferences and communities, or ask your business angel to introduce you — the best route being a mutual acquaintance whom someone has already invested in and who recommends you. Investors want a minimum of 10× on their money, and even that isn't the end goal, merely the reason for interest: at the top of the funnel you should have a hundred-plus investors — it's an ordinary B2B sale of a stake in your company — and of 200 investors at the entrance, perhaps 2–3 will invest.

And now the main event: **Agile**. This approach is for working with an out-of-date spec under tight deadlines. Requirements can change on the fly, and then waterfall stops looking attractive. A bug appears that isn't a regression? Fix it now. If the spec is supplemented mid-work — say, off the back of our research — only Agile lets you reach release with the expected result. First, commit the Agile Manifesto to memory as a conceptual approach: a working product matters more than detailed documentation about it; each team member and the interactions between them matter more than processes and tools; work *with* the customer matters more than agreeing every trifle in the contract; and working *through* changes matters more than doggedly following the roadmap. These statements are more than ten years old.

**Scrum**, now, as a method. The team must hold a *Product Owner* — the one who sees the whole product picture, sets priorities, can motivate, and watches deadlines: a bold, empowered, all-understanding person. A *Scrum Master* — the one who initiates every change and watches the project's course, teaching, mentoring, removing obstacles, trying to shield the team from the Product Owner and from little-known villains. A *Production Team* — three to nine people who do all the building, can self-organise, have long worked together, bear responsibility for process and result, and are broadly specialised and cross-functional. And the *Backlog* — a prioritised list of requirements, constantly replenished and edited, where the team estimates tasks not in hours but in a conditional "small / medium / large," or in points on the Fibonacci sequence (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21).

Say a task comes in: when the "caller" phones warmed-up leads, he may discover a lead isn't actually warm and wants to note that on the profile. The feature's value: +$700,000 saved once we switch off the sources that send cold leads. We prioritise it:

| Question | Score |
| --- | --- |
| Is the process blocked if the task isn't done? | 0 |
| What's the scale of the problem — will more than half the users be affected? | 1 |
| If done now, will it help the process? | 1 |
| If not done, are the losses large? | 1 |
| Is it a one-off task? | 1 |
| Does the task affect the solution directly or indirectly? | 1 |
| **Total** | **5** |

*Backlog grooming* is a meeting of Scrum-team representatives where the product backlog's details are discussed and the next sprint planning is prepared. Sprints and their planning, retrospectives, and meetings are an important part of the process. The retrospective is where the team improves its processes. Especially important are the stand-ups, at which each member answers three core questions: what did you do yesterday toward the sprint goal, what will you do today toward it, and what problems do you have in reaching it? A sprint is one to two weeks in which the team gets through part of the backlog — this is the essence of Scrum, when the team has agreed a fixed number of tasks for one sprint and no new ones are added. The team works autonomously and transparently; everyone knows who's on what, with diagrams and boards to help. At the sprint's end the team demonstrates the result for release, followed by a discussion of what was done well and what could be done better. The main thing is not to make the process an end in itself: Agile is about a *result* after every iteration. (It doesn't suit landing pages or corporate sites especially well.)

In brief, that is the whole of Scrum: a development approach oriented on full control of the process and fast sprints, which shortens time-to-market. There are interesting interpretations — Nexus, for large projects with many dev teams, or the more modern Large-Scale Scrum (LeSS), where a big team splits into three small, full-fledged teams that remain one team, so that many features ship rather than one. These systems have a weak spot: they don't account for the team's *motivation*, which is the Agile coach's charge. And if the team is new, no one knowing anyone, the motivation question turns acute — a new team will work inefficiently regardless, however seasoned the Scrum Master or Agile coach, and the businessman won't sit beside the programmers; it doesn't interest him. There's a solution: team dynamics after Bruce Tuckman. In three to six months the team should move into growth; if it doesn't, the Scrum Master is so-so. In a good, high-performance groove a team will hold for three to four years — though teams can, of course, live their whole lives at the starting point, as in state organisations, where efficiency will always be below average.

And how to plan all this. For risk management we use a SAFe (Scaled Agile Framework) style, laying planning some way ahead and managing risk. Planning happens once a quarter, when everyone gathers and builds a plan five sprints out. Why five? Because we plan tasks for four sprints, but one weekly sprint may slip for external reasons. And you write out the risks — what problems could arise in reaching the sprint goal — then vote on them: you go to the iOS team, ask whether they can implement your design by next sprint, and they rate it 1 to 5, where 5 is *certainly* and 1 is *no confidence at all*, and we correct our plans accordingly. Naturally risks arise that the team won't finish a task in time, and *risk roaming* is applied — the product lead gathers all risks and groups them: *resolved* (no risk, the team says it'll do it all), *owned* (someone took responsibility — not enough people, the director said he'll hire), *accepted* (well, nothing to be done, an external risk; if it happens, the sprint goal won't be met), and *mitigated* (a risk still unclear).

Agile is a *philosophy* — but it includes approaches built on Agile principles. Scrum is an *operational framework* that lets you spin up development by a process quickly; yet if you hold to the philosophy of Agile — and don't turn it into ritual — the process must be changed iteratively. Kanban, meanwhile, is not part of Agile and is applied without it; use Scrum + Kanban and you have Scrumban, taking the regular meetings from Scrum and the control of an individual task's speed from Kanban.

These are the rules within which you apply your creative methods. And that, incidentally, is where a *Growth Team* comes in for anyone whose product is already stable, with a good market share and heavy legacy: such a product needs to be maintained separately and to have its growth points hunted separately, by different teams. A Growth Team has its own PM, marketer, universal developer, and data analyst — and can be split functionally further still, one team doing only onboarding, another only notifications. Because the work is never really finished: a living product keeps issuing its small wows, and someone has to keep finding them.
