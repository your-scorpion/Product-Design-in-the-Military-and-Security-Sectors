# Working in International Markets

*Maxim Tsvetkov · February 2, 2019 · [original (RU)](https://your-scorpion.ru/international-work/)*

This article is about stereotypes. Naturally, every person, case, and business is unique and has its own particularities. An Italian may behave like a German, and a German may act like a Belgian. Stereotypes never show the full picture. Yes, Norwegians eat a lot of fish, Swedes put shrimp on their hot dogs, and Germans wear tall socks with sandals and shorts on hikes and demand their change down to the last penny — never missing a chance for sarcasm. Italians think the French smell bad, and Spaniards think the same of the British. Finns drink a lot; Swedes deeply value personal space but care little for their personal data. Still, you need to know the general median trend. There are objective technical nuances too: across nearly all of Europe and Latin America, a comma separates the decimal from the whole number, while in Asia and the States it's a period — which is why we use UTF-8 everywhere. The Chinese, meanwhile, are far more tolerant of social inequality than Americans, and more inclined toward risk. But these are trifles. What I want to talk about are the more significant — and less Googleable — aspects of working with different nations.

Stereotypes in business are, if anything, harmful. Professional background is the key factor in determining someone's negotiating style. Everything from their education to their work experience shapes how they approach a deal. Lawyers, for instance, with their deep understanding of conflict and litigation risk, will pay especially close attention to the warranty clauses in any agreement.

There's a classic figure: if you enter a market that already has three to five major players, your odds of success are slim. If you can, it's better to go after less competitive foreign markets — build a great product in one market, then scale. Windows of opportunity are defined by place and time; your product might be relevant in Australia today and in the Czech Republic tomorrow. The IT business isn't tied to a location, and no country's market is self-sufficient (except the US). National borders are not borders for business — plenty of startups from other countries also want to work, and do work, in the markets of Eastern Europe.

The first thing to grasp: the global market is won through hacks and flexible approaches, not by competing on money. You cannot out-spend Microsoft.

The second: a company doesn't count as global if it can only boast sales in its local market — it doesn't matter whether that's Russia or Germany. A company is considered international when roughly 70% of its revenue comes from international markets; in Eastern Europe, that's mostly gaming companies. The goal is achievable: the average Russian spends $700–800 a year on online purchases, while in the US, the UK, and Korea that figure is close to $4,000.

## Differences

In Eastern Europe, the market took shape with supply outstripping demand, so there's a belief that a good product and flexible pricing are enough. That isn't even close to an informed opinion. On the international market, it doesn't work.

The first step is translating your site into the target languages and optimizing for mobile. The translation is done by a native-speaker editor plus a professional translator on your side. Editors vary — newspaper editors usually won't do, because of their stylistic habits. The professional translator is there to handle the small things, like rendering Russian quotations as ordinary phrases in the target language. It's easier to build the interface in English from the start; you'll need more than a translation of the interface text — you'll need full adaptation. There are two main types of error: context errors, and text that overflows the space allotted to it in the interface.

When localizing into 10–15 languages, you can't avoid contractors — and they use subcontractors. What you end up with is a kind of multi-language vendor relying on many subcontractors, who may have their own contractors in turn: freelancers and in-house staff. With that much delegation, controlling the quality of the work is always hard. At the end of it, you assemble a block kit — one large file containing the localization of the site and the product.

So now you have a site, and clients have started writing to you (actually they haven't, but more on that later). You need to answer inquiries in the language the client speaks, and a meaningful part of your team will start living on Pacific time. Usually that's 15:00 to 24:00 Moscow time. But working for FAANG can start at noon and end at 3 a.m. With an interactive chat on the site, you can grab a B2C client's phone number and reply on WhatsApp from some British number, or even on Viber. Clients won't leave you their country code, though — you'll have to determine the country yourself.

In that on-site chat, don't open with the standard "how can I help you" — people dislike it. Better to write something local and tied to your business. Say you sell ready-made river routes; you could ask in the chat, "Which river will you be rafting at the next competition?" with answer options. Naturally, you'll want to prioritize such questions by country. In B2B you won't sell expensive goods through a site chat and a messenger — B2B is a long sale, and you'll need to provide trial versions of the product, run demos, sometimes at your own office in the client's country.

You'll need your own office in another country, or at least your own person there. Sitting in one country and selling to the whole world is hard. People from every country are very different. The physiology is identical, but cultural differences are deeply individual. Drop a "landing party" — a small team — into the new country and let them build the MVP of the launch. Rein in your sense of humor: fewer ethical jokes. If you're already a big business, you need your own presence in the other country — a real office with employees, contact with counterparties regardless of their scale (SMB or Enterprise). But until you're that big, just find a part-time employee from the other country, and brace yourself for a lot of micromanagement. Your language level needs to be upper-intermediate at minimum — that's plenty. Perfect English matters less than understanding the cultural particularities of your local team member and aligning on terminology. And thinking in the target language, since thinking in a given language shapes a native behavioral model. Managing international employees is hard; they often do something other than what you expect, so it's vital to communicate business goals very clearly.

In India it's customary not to touch people much. But touch is a sign of great respect. Staring intently — practically scanning someone with your eyes — is a sign that you like them. And yes, without a smile.

To understand your own culture, try answering for yourself which of each pair describes you better:

1. Affectivity (gratifying needs) versus affective neutrality (restraining impulses);
2. Self-orientation versus collectivity-orientation;
3. Universalism (applying general standards) versus particularism (taking specific relationships into account);
4. Ascription (judging others by who they are) versus achievement (judging them by what they do);
5. Specificity (limiting relations with others to particular spheres) versus diffuseness (no preset limits on the nature of the relationship).

On top of that, layer the global division into different types of society:

1. The evaluation of human nature (evil — mixed — good);
2. Humanity's relationship to the surrounding natural environment (subjugation — harmony — mastery);
3. Time orientation (toward the past — the present — the future);
4. Activity orientation (being — doing);
5. Relationships between people (lineality, i.e. hierarchically ordered positions — collaterality, i.e. group relationships — individualism).

## Tests and Design

What do most agencies do when they try to make an international product? They translate the landing page into the target language and pour traffic at it. That sometimes works back home, but in saturated markets the conversion rate will be through the floor. There's the concept of market volume — the entire market as a whole — and it usually looks rather appetizing. But it all comes down to market capacity: in the area where you launch your store, there are another 20 competitors, and you can't reach every client. You can use the Nielsen panel, but definitely not to set the price of an offline product. Beyond capacity, elasticity and market dynamics matter. The market may already be near saturation. The business models of competitors in foreign markets matter too — even those who've already left the market.

First: look at the particularities of the local markets. If you have a landing page, put a "Buy on Amazon" button on it. Amazon is an excellent test for B2C; in the US market it's the main store for physical goods. Amazon handles the logistics of all goods itself, but because of that you won't have access to customer contacts. And all your discounts and promotions will be set in advance, in line with Amazon's discount schedule. Reviews are the most important thing — many Americans often use Amazon instead of Google to search.

Second is SEO: in the West there's only one search engine, Google, so there's more competition for top positions. The risks of using cloaking are higher. Many IT companies write articles, already have a lot of organic traffic, and use voice search — and all of it on mobile (up to 80%). So when it comes to promotion, all eyes on ROI (which must not be negative).

My experience scaling products from Eastern Europe to the international market has shown that the classic test of a business idea is very expensive. I know of cases where people spent even a million dollars on a test. They hire a VP of sales for $300,000, with no experience of the company's product, and send them off to generate leads. But local mentality and language are far from all you need for a successful sales launch. It's a kind of milestone everyone passes through.

You need to test more cheaply. Crowdfunding is a test of product demand. App-store reviews are also a source of information — you should answer every comment and try to surface problems. A questionnaire is still a cheap way to gather information, but don't forget the particularities of language. The word "complex" in a certain context can be read as a "complex" (integrated) product rather than something complicated to use. Or surprises, like Brazilians who use the internet only from McDonald's Wi-Fi. Or those same Brazilians from paid traffic, who actively delete the product on day one. Also, in Israel people write right to left — so how will they give you five stars? Will they count from the left or the right? Put the numbers inside the stars. In Israel it's important to pay attention to an investor's reliability, since it's a startup market.

In an international product you can't put text on an image (I mean banners) — they're hard to edit; it's easier to rely on icons and photos. You shouldn't drop text entirely, though — without text people don't feel the interface; text reads as a mandatory decoration. So you'll need a flexible system for working with marketing materials.

## Europe

In the West they're wary of working with Eastern Europe, but there is a positive brand attached to it: good quality for little money. You've built the product in English, and if you're aiming at Europe, then EFIGS (French, Italian, German, Spanish) is your path forward. In Western Europe it's very much the custom to communicate at length, to come in person and negotiate. Constant roadshows. At the same price, they'll prefer to order from Romania or Poland over Russia.

Germany is the startup capital of Europe. With Germans everything is planned out — the exact meeting start time and the exact agenda, all in English — whereas for the French, being late to a meeting is perfectly normal. And they really do eat baguettes every day. At the same time, the French greatly value being addressed respectfully, and they don't welcome English for historical reasons, preferring French. And sometimes Verlan. If you let them know you're not from England, they may well switch to English. This matters when you need a service from an agency in France. It's also worth accounting for the AZERTY keyboard layout, under which an interface tuned for the standard WASD performs very sadly. This particularity applies to France and French-speaking Belgium.

Germany has its own quirks — for example, a law with requirements for a site's contact page, or that mobile phones are called *handy*, or the meticulous proofreading of every document before signing (which affects how you run interviews). Like many Europeans, Germans prefer to keep their work-life balance closer to "life," so on weekends they won't spend their time on you — they'll prefer a hobby or family. Otherwise, from an interview standpoint, Germans are a very good audience. There are very few topics you can't discuss — even income, health, intimacy, and age — and they'll readily and bluntly discuss any subject once an NDA is signed. There's a language barrier, since there are many German dialects, but in big cities English is fine. Because everything must work by the rules from a German's point of view, they'll even cross at the zebra crossing without looking left or right. Germany is boring — everything closes by 8 p.m. and nothing works on Sundays, which is why Germans so love spending time in nature. Some restaurants, cinemas, clubs, museums, and amusement parks stay open. They love bicycles too, but it's easy to catch a fine. In winter they open the windows for ten minutes three or four times a day to air out the room.

Italians love to dramatize: if you didn't give them feedback, it means you're unhappy with the work and already firing the person. For Germans, by contrast, withholding feedback is as good as praise. No criticism = everything was done well. But for many cultures — again, Italians or the French — withholding feedback expresses dissatisfaction with the result. The French, meanwhile, love to criticize emotionally and rarely give positive feedback, and you have to account for this in interviews — separating real pain points from yet another emotional surge. The Dutch will always state their opinion of your work directly, because they criticize the work, not the person. Giving a Dutch person positive feedback destroys trust. In Asia, criticism of the work equals criticism of the person. Hence the rule: you can meaningfully influence a business decision midway through the approval process in Russia, Germany, or the Netherlands, but certainly not in Japan. In Japan you have to start influencing a decision at the very earliest stage. And in Italy you can influence a decision simply over an after-lunch espresso — an excellent time to talk.

Subordination also varies country to country. For Germans, disagreeing with the boss is perfectly normal, as it is for Austrians. By contrast, for the Chinese such behavior is categorically forbidden — you mustn't even think about questioning a manager's decision. If the boss presents a development plan, then Italians, French, Spaniards, and Germans will almost certainly start asking "why — what for — where did these conclusions come from?" Americans, Canadians, Australians, Brits, and Swedes will focus more on the plans than on understanding why the plan is the way it is.

The French won't turn down a drink with colleagues — they want to get to know the person, to learn more about them. It's the same with Russians, Hindustanis, Brazilians, and Japanese. If someone won't have fun with me, then they don't trust me. Germans, meanwhile, draw a clear line between work and personal time. In China, if the boss says the whole team is going out drinking, everyone goes drinking — by order. This affects how you assign tasks: where a Frenchman can be asked informally, Germans need a formal, official request, because if a task isn't written down, it isn't important. And your colleague from Finland might not even say hello if they run into you in a shop. But as a host, they'll be sure to offer you coffee (and nothing else).

Remember that Europe is many different cultures and languages. Bulgarians and Greeks, for example, nod up and down when they disagree.

The English deserve a separate mention. They try to be very polite and indirect, and to your bad idea they'll respond: *that's an interesting line of thinking!* — all while knowing perfectly well that you propose bad solutions. And the English really dislike direct sales through banners — you have to flirt with them through clever creatives. But the UK is a market that's appetizing in money terms and without major language problems (peculiar, but still English), one accustomed to outsourcing from India. Many companies in London actively hire Indians and Pakistanis. I deliberately use the Russian word *indus* for Indians — but that word, which literally means "Hindu," offends Muslims from the subcontinent. The more correct term is "Hindustani," yet for this article I'll keep saying "Indian." A general rule for interviews: high-income people prefer to speak only positively and avoid personal topics, while people below the middle class are more communicative when it comes to complaints. But they too have little desire to discuss finances.

Finland as a country is quite friendly toward Russia, but it has many strong programmers and designers of its own. Finland, Norway, and Denmark are used to working with the Baltic states: Latvia, Lithuania, Estonia. So you need not only English but the local languages. And Estonia has a very friendly tax system, which is great, and people 35+ know Russian.

And Sweden — good English and not far from Russia. If your budget is limited, it's better to start with the small markets. But despite the English, only Britain and Ireland are suited for English-language promotion online; in other countries the audience will Google in their local languages. And even when they do use English, it's a peculiar English. Localizing an app into English for Russia, the UK, and the States is three different localizations. In Switzerland they speak three languages: German, French, Italian. English is enough for everyday matters, but to work in a particular canton you must know that canton's key languages. Sunday is a day of quiet — nothing works in the cities, and after 8 p.m. you can't even flush the toilet, so as not to disturb the neighbors. And a driver isn't allowed to eat while driving — it's illegal. Switzerland isn't in the EU, so even Europeans have to fuss with visas. In Italy, August is the month of vacations — all work activity slows down.

When doing research, you have to clearly understand a country's cultural layer. It's far from acceptable everywhere to offer only two genders to choose from in a survey — you must add an "other" option. Better to take a representative of the target country and run the survey past him or her as a test.

There will be a lot of stories with legislation. The Google tax, mandatory payment processing through a foreign partner, getting caught by foreign VAT. In Russia, cash-register equipment. Also, legislation in Russia changes often — it changes with good intentions, but it still scares off investors. In any case, you'll need a foreign-currency account, a transit account, the correct transliteration of your first and last name, billing in USD accounting for VAT — Austria 20%, Belgium 21%, and so on. A reseller can ease the pain of paperwork and tax payment by taking that function on.

Politeness. Normal: "Hello, how are you?" — and wait for the answer. Answer the same question back. Don't address people by name in a public chat. Meeting a woman you know well: one kiss in Brazil, two in Italy. Especially for parents — without it, it's an insult. In general, all of South America prefers one kiss, while European Spanish-speakers prefer two (I mean the sound, not actual lip contact). But one thing unites them — the pot must be stored inside the oven.

The IT industry in Europe is applied — that is, not about innovation. For innovation, go to the States or to China.

## The United States

All the world's markets work the US, including China and India. The rate of a Russian developer or designer is higher compared to those countries (in Russia it's $30 an hour; Indians do it for $15 an hour). But the quality of work in Russia is higher.

The main feature: Americans love to just go and do — double the price of a service, or hand everyone promo codes, and see what happens — whereas to Germans, with their innate meticulousness, this approach seems wild. In the Valley, the time for a startup to go from idea to business is a year to a year and a half. In the rest of the US that span is two to three years; in many other countries it's five to ten. Startups in Europe are slow, but they understand this, and that's how the business is set up.

But there are things common to both the US and European markets: if a client sees an invoice from a private individual, they may back out of the purchase. So the receipt needs a company logo — better yet, connect Stripe. For a young startup, opening a legal entity in the US is quite costly, so everyone solves the problem their own way.

It's worth mentioning here that the States have a strong entrepreneurial culture. Where in Germany or England everyone hides any failures from their résumé, in the States people are proud of their list of failures. If Germans want to launch a startup, they'll assess the risks a million times, conclude the risks are high, and go back to boring corporate work. In the States, risk is not a reason not to try.

Another feature of the market: Americans love to exploit what's already been built, which is why you'll find very old, '90s-level websites at big American companies. And it's not due to a lack of money (obviously) — Americans simply don't like making a product for the sake of making a product. Take PayPal: its interface has barely changed since the service's birth; whether clients like it or not doesn't much matter. The interface does its job and there's no worthy alternative, so the business will keep working. That's why "we'll make it better and more convenient for you" is not an argument. You have to solve one of two problems: earn more, or spend less.

A problem can be found or created. Found: a company is losing money, and you'll help them save it. Created: your competitor was defrauded by criminals for $1,000,000 — buy our solution and you'll be protected. Or: a competitor is moving into a new niche and will overtake you tomorrow, but we already hold positions in that niche — buy our startup.

You have to come bearing a solution intelligently, too. It annoys Americans when one hired person in New York does the pre-sale and all further communication happens with someone from Eastern Europe in broken English. Remote B2B sales for the US are very rare, so you either go and sell yourself, or you find a partner who'll be on fire for your product, give themselves to the project 100%, and push it everywhere. You have to create the feeling that the company is American — not a distributed group of friends from Eastern Europe with one representative in the US. The basis of that feeling is internal correspondence in good English, working on US time, and developers' ability to travel to the client for integration. Which means the developers must be ready to meet snakes in the California mountains, or to rescue their little dog from coyotes at Griffith Observatory. And to watch nothing in sports except football, baseball, hockey, and basketball.

Coming bearing alcohol, however, is dangerous. The person might be a recovering alcoholic (AA meetings are very popular in the US). Bringing a matryoshka as a gift is OK, but an expensive gift can be read as a bribe.

It's important to preserve UX: Americans are used to giving their card details and getting a two-week trial of a product in return (especially in B2B, which is never free in America). Offer a three-month trial and you'll look suspicious. If, at the start of the sales funnel, the client doesn't clearly see a warning that their card details aren't needed, then by default it's assumed that at some point card details will be required. In B2C they often take a dollar instead of a free trial. Nobody will hand over their phone number just like that either — they prefer to use email. If you need to enter an SSN, then only the last four digits. Understanding such local nuances takes strong Customer Development, during which you'll have to actively fight the American's urge to drift into politics and avoid discussing money.

And the time zones. Almost the whole world wants to be adapted to the West Coast time zone. In the States everything is fast, the food portions are huge, milk is sold by the gallon. Because Americans drive for groceries once or twice a month and store everything in big freezers in their basements. Buying two years' worth of canned beans with coupons is normal. The staple bread is white toast bread (not in California), while European bread is expensive and rare. Even an electric kettle won't be in every home — people prefer the microwave. There are reasons for this: first, in Europe the outlets are 220/240V and water boils faster than on the American 120V; and second, Americans prefer coffee and have a separate coffee maker. As do the Spanish.

Everything around the US is also a market with its own particularities. Canada (Montreal) is officially French, but you need English too. Two languages at once. For New Year nothing is given as a gift, but for Christmas (which is December 25) you can give small gifts — but BEFORE December 25. On the 26th the holiday is already over.

A separate word about bloggers. It's very important to verify a blogger's honesty: they may assure you that nearly all their audience is from the US, when in fact their followers are Indians from India. Confirmed the blogger's audience is from the US? Congratulations — they'll be very expensive; you could take a blogger with a European audience instead and diversify the risk. Fortunately, many are willing to work on a CPA model.

In the States and Canada it's customary to speak very loudly. If you're sitting with a friend at a bar surrounded by a few groups of three or four people, you probably won't be able to hear your friend. Arabs are less loud. But in California everyone tries to communicate very politely and avoid conflict. Say "f\*\*k off" in the States and the odds of a fight are very high. In Australia, by contrast, people tell each other off twenty times a day with no consequences.

A contract in the States and a contract in Europe are very different contracts. Many believe it's precisely because of the particularities of contract work that Europe has work-life balance. In Europe, hardly any employer will agree to terminate a contract early. The Netherlands adore working on a contract basis.

In Eastern Europe people love using Facebook for both work and personal posts at once. In the US this is unacceptable — Facebook for an American is photos of pets and children, while for building business contacts it's LinkedIn. Messengers are an even more personal channel than social networks. Until you've been directly invited to communicate via WhatsApp, it's better not to push into that channel. First you establish contact on LinkedIn, then the correspondence moves to other channels.

To work with Latin America you need localization into Spanish, which half the world speaks. But in Brazil, in that same region, Portuguese is used, and these users are the most able to pay; in the rest of Latin America, Spanish is in use. Addressing a Brazilian in Spanish is offensive. They love the sun. It's easier to learn Brazilian Portuguese — it's closer to Angola and to Spanish — but it differs from original Portuguese. Brazilian Portuguese has many words identical to Russian, many nasal sounds, and more closely resembles Catalan and Galician. For example, the word *puxar* means "pull" in Brazilian Portuguese and "push" in original Portuguese (or vice versa — too lazy to fact-check). Brazil is an audience able to pay that may well be unable to write and read in its own native language while still being well-off — farmers, for instance. Farmers don't pay taxes and are very rich; they meet old age in Portugal.

So, Brazil can boast a good economy; the rest of Latin America — not so much. But in Brazil there's no work at all from New Year's to March — everyone's on holiday. Don't take out your phone on the street — it's dangerous. Need to make a call or order a taxi through an app? Step inside, it's safer there — and if it's a *cracolândia*-type area (an open-air crack market), you mustn't show your phone at all. All of Latin America, really, isn't especially safe. The locals have developed an internal sense of how to tell a dangerous place from a safe one. In Argentina the culture is more European, similar to Italy, while Venezuela and Colombia are more Latin in temperament. And Argentina has excellent developers, cool IT, lots of Italians, some Russians too — and you have to be very careful with the word "black" in reference to people. They love football so much that during an important match they cancel classes at school and the whole class watches the game. If your birthday is the 24th, or you're 24 years old, brace yourself for Brazilian jokes about your orientation.

On business trips to Brazil, be careful. In the jungle a huge number of dangerous creatures await you, mostly small ones. An anaconda doesn't much feed on people and will be frightened of a human, but a bug can infect you with terrible diseases. And in Rio de Janeiro, you can easily be killed for trying to hit on someone's girlfriend.

Promote a site to Spain, and a lot of people will come of their own accord. Also, Miami is effectively a Latin Republican state, so they may come along too. In South (Latin) America, users are willing to pay in games for the chance to play as a team, but to a greater degree they're willing to watch ads. By device, Latin America is mostly Android (most of the population isn't very wealthy). I once had a case where the statistics showed a lot of `es-US` in `navigator.language`. And at the same time, there were many app crashes on Android during search. It turned out the app didn't support Spanish characters after translation via Google Translate, and the Android users were mostly Spanish-speaking.

And if you're offered a business trip, it's only to Costa Rica. It's not called the Switzerland of Latin America for nothing, and Colombians need a visa to enter. Cuba is currently under strong American influence — they adore baseball.

When recruiting respondents, you have to pay special attention to local holidays, and try not to schedule interviews on Monday or Friday — there'll be a lot of last-minute cancellations. Americans value time with family and hobbies, and would rather sit at a bar with friends than in a usability lab. But if they do come to the interview, they'll give high scores, even if the work process was awful.

In Brazil it's customary to have a lot of children. Everyone swears, the state pays for maternity leave, and contraceptives are handed out to everyone for free. Lots of fast food, and free martial arts. They love coining new words (unlike Portugal), and humor. The guys have a good and cheap technical education, so many leave to work in the States. After that, the main problem for a Brazilian techie is how to move a mountain of money from the States to Brazil. Local IT doesn't pay especially big money — a senior is around $2–3k, mostly fintech, and the work is done mostly by locals. But the stars in fintech can earn a respectable $10k a month without leaving for the States. The only thing that keeps people in the local IT market is the culture, since the States mean working yourself to the bone, and Brazilians are very laid-back people. Many work for US banks, earning huge sums. Many Brazilians are obsessed with the American dream and try to leave for the States. And in old age — for Portugal. IT managers in Brazil are often poorly versed in IT. In Portugal it's also customary to work long hours, but with constant breaks. Brazilian and Portuguese cuisine are quite similar.

In Brazil it's very expensive to import cars; many locals choose GM and Honda made inside the country. Yet poor people go hungry and there isn't enough work, even though Brazil is an enormous farm. Huge companies grow food on vast territories, while ordinary people often don't have their own land to grow soybeans or sugarcane. There's a strong attachment to skin color, since Brazil is a mix of different nations: as everywhere, the whiter you are, the more leeway you get — up to and including freely carrying marijuana in enormous quantities.

Paraguay produces electricity for nearly all its neighbors, has stunning waterfalls, and all goods there are much cheaper than in, say, Brazil. Paraguay is also associated, for everyone, with counterfeits. Chile is very different from the rest of South America; Mexico is second in terms of differences. Different food, different drinks. In Mexico there's Coca-Cola everywhere, but with natural sugar. Mexicans even consider themselves the most Latin of all Latin America. Which brings on plenty of jokes from other Latinos, like the ones about jumping the border fences as a national sport. Mexicans also adore *albur* — that is, ribald jokes built on wordplay with double meanings. And in restaurants they leave a tip, at least 10%, always. People from central Mexico City are called Chilangos. By the way, in US schools it's specifically Mexican Spanish that's taught. In China, by contrast, tipping is a rare thing.

But speaking of borders, the Darién Gap between Colombia and Panama is hell. Better to take a plane to Mexico.

Brazil, Uruguay, and Peru are very similar. Brazil has a very strong community of Japanese, Italians, and Jews. It also has many migrants from Iran, Italy, and Venezuela. The crime level depends directly on the size of the city. São Paulo is definitely not safe — millions of poor people — but it's the safest in Brazil once you drive away from the city. Minas Gerais — the best food. All the best carnivals and resorts are in the north, and at carnivals everyone makes friends and starts relationships. In Colombia, too, the level of safety leaves much to be desired. But getting hold of narcotics there is difficult, contrary to the reputation. Across all of Latin America, Uruguay is considered the most stable economy — there people are ready to pay and have the income to do it. Uruguay has a good standard of living; the same can be said of Costa Rica.

Mexico and Spain love colorful, bright résumés. In Spain they listen to reggaeton, eat very salty food, bathe in bureaucracy, regard introversion as rudeness, dine late in the evening with the whole family, and never make remarks to anyone.

How to translate a product: fit the UI, the currency, then hand it to a native speaker for proofreading. Macros for inserting domain names and currencies are lifesavers. In design, use bright colors, emoji, a female voice for narration (matriarchy). The infinitive and the imperative coincide in English but not in the Romance languages or Russian, so a call to action is better translated from Russian into Spanish than from English into Spanish. Russian is grammatically closer than English.

## Asia

The 21st century is the century of Asia, and for IT that means Hong Kong and Singapore. Population: 4.3 billion people, or 60% of the Earth's population. In the future this will shift in Africa's favor, but for now it's so. China and India alone — the two most populous countries in the world — are home to 37% of the planet's inhabitants. There are two interesting markets at once in the APAC region: the mature market of Australia and the fast-growing market of Southeast Asia, which includes two of the four BRICS countries, as well as the home of Samsung, LG, and Hyundai — South Korea. The Southeast Asian market is mobile-oriented, which makes it very similar to the Chinese one. Technology penetration here is high. Almost everyone has a smartphone, which sometimes replaces bank cards. The main thing is not to make a mistake with dollars: Australia and America have different dollars, with a 30% difference. Ecuador's national currency is also the dollar.

The first important feature is European individualism versus Asian collectivism. The former think more about their personal life and family (Brits, Americans, Germans); the latter more about their role in a large group (Asia). This is indirectly confirmed by the strict hierarchy of "the boss is more important than the subordinate," where the subordinate has no right to argue with the manager, even if the manager is very wrong. A complete ban on exiting the hierarchy within the collective. Father → son, boss → subordinate, older brother → younger brother. So it's deathly important to reach the boss right away, and to handle the unwillingness to take responsibility for anything. This is also the source of a very prejudiced attitude toward frequently changing jobs. In the following table I've tried to correlate country with the importance of subordination, based on my experience:

| Country | Authority of the boss |

| India | Very important |
| Japan | Very important |
| Canada | Weakly expressed |
| Israel | Quite weakly expressed |
| Germany | Important, on the whole |
| Brazil | Important, on the whole |
| Netherlands | Absent |
| Saudi Arabia | Quite important |
| China | Very important |
| Italy | Important, on the whole |

The second feature: if you're not a local, you're nobody. So choosing a partner to do business in China is just about the most important decision there is. Tech hubs from Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen will chase after a sound business — there's plenty to choose from. Choose the city based on the partner companies you'll be able to work with within the tech hub. Each region of China has its own particularities; Shanghai, for example, is very fast and business-focused. Chongqing is the largest megalopolis of central China, third after Beijing and Shanghai, with a great deal of manufacturing. If they ask for a design by Saturday, it should be ready by Thursday or Friday — then you can make edits by Saturday and get a decent result. It sounds hard, but given the strong financials of Smeshariki — the Russian animated series, which after entering the Chinese market immediately grew to 40 million — the game is worth the candle.

In Asia it's customary to work in one place for ten years, or until the end of your life, sometimes by the 996 principle. For an Asian, proposing a European service to management carries serious reputational risk. It can put an end to their entire career, because in Asia generally the scariest thing for a specialist in the business environment is to make a mistake. To "lose face." Especially in Vietnam. A boss, for example, can't arrive at work in a car of a lower class than his subordinate's. At the same time there's a drive to measure up to the Western person. It works the other way too: gifts that publicly emphasize a person's merits are very much liked by the Chinese. It's especially important to pay attention to the packaging. But you mustn't exceed 200,000 yuan (and sometimes less), or it's considered a bribe. Gift ideas for the Chinese: tea, alcohol, dried fruit, jewelry. Chinese girls generally love jewelry, while the guys often get model haircuts and pronounced eyebrows. You can find lots of gift ideas on Xiaohongshu under the tag #小众高级. By contrast, Korean girls don't wear jewelry. It's customary to politely refuse a gift two or three times before accepting it.

Similar cultural traits can stem from completely different historical sources: Chinese and American cultures, for example, are generally characterized by a low level of risk aversion. In China this may be linked to the collective nature of agricultural labor, which required a system of "solidarity" to manage certain risks. In America, low risk aversion may be driven by an ethic of self-belief and the second chance. France, on the other hand, is a risk-averse society. The French rely on planning to avoid risks and have developed a system of "risk prevention," embodied in a complex social-security system. Thus their culture is expressed in a very prominent social institution.

India has a cult of the social elevator, and it's a value of the whole community that you can work with. The regions differ not only in language: northern India is more about tomatoes and millet, the south about coconuts and tamarinds. The north is Hindi, where English isn't as popular. The south is safer and more educated. There are also wholly isolated territories, like North Sentinel Island, where dangerous tribes have lived for thousands of years already. Language matters to account for too: in the Kerala region one of the popular dialects is Tamil. You have to observe the community and begin to feel its values (passive learning) or do custdev (active learning, but the methodology should be aimed not at users' pain points but at their values). Study it intelligently — in the countries of Asia, the Middle East, and Latin America, people love to inflate their ratings and not show criticism.

Language is no less important. There are more than 50 languages in Southeast Asia, and those are just the main ones. Counting dialects, Indonesia alone has more than 300 languages. No wonder — Indonesia is a very densely populated country made up of 17,000 islands that together are wider than the States. Be ready to eat *cabai*, the hot chili sauce. If you don't eat it, something's wrong with you. And the main export of goods is to the States. But there are only five densely populated islands, and there are dangerous islands that even the locals don't venture onto. You need to decide how you'll localize your app — into which languages and for which segment. English will do for the Philippines, because a large share of the population is English-speaking (Tagalog), but this option won't work in Indonesia, where it's Bahasa. Indonesia has an enormous number of regional dialects on top of the official language; you mustn't give or take anything with your left hand, and if you did — apologize. You mustn't touch a person's head, you mustn't pass anything over a head, even a child's. Business is conducted through group consultation; the boss will always ask his subordinates' opinion. In Korea you must serve and take things with two hands. You'll also find the jeepney only in the Philippines. In Korea, too, English is poorly known; with the youth the language situation is a bit better, but language barriers are still present. Also, in Korea a programmer is not a prestigious profession. As in many other countries with a lot of electronics manufacturing. And then there's WeChat for everyone in China — without it you can't even get onto the internet everywhere; it has replaced even business cards. But if a business card is offered, you must take it with both hands and great respect. The savings culture in China is simple: they love freebies and saving, but at the same time they're ready to pay for quality, and they trust word of mouth. Of course, in Singapore the culture is a bit more European — there's no corruption, they speak Singlish, they're not afraid to lose face and can say "no" right away, without a year-long negotiation. And most importantly, if your product takes hold in Singapore, that immediately grants you a credit of trust across the whole Asian region. At the very least the road to the Asian tigers (South Korea, Singapore, Hong Kong, and Taiwan) is definitely open.

It's important to behave at the table. Even the length of the chopsticks matters: if the food is placed in the center of the table, you need long chopsticks and must hold them right at the base. In Indonesia, the Philippines, India, and Bangladesh, chopsticks aren't much used — more forks and spoons. In Korea, metal chopsticks are used instead of wooden ones. The Japanese use only chopsticks, of various materials. In China there are different chopsticks for different purposes — for example, Haidilao hot pot requires long wooden chopsticks because the food is hot. It is categorically forbidden to stick chopsticks into a rice dish — that's how the dead are seen off. If, during lunch with the Chinese, someone adds food to your plate, it's a sign of great respect. A reminder: the one who invites always pays. No arguments.

In China it's better to start writing about work in March, because after the manager layoffs in February, new ones appear in March, and right after the Party congress you can build business plans. The main conferences are in May–June; then in November many Chinese travel on business trips (since they'll be laid off in February and need to get their travel in at the employer's expense), and a hellish pileup of work is scheduled for January. Not all Chinese work in one place their whole life.

China very much loves to close deals in a restaurant with a lot of alcohol. In that respect there's a similarity with Russia. Stock up on your health — you'll need it. And on endurance. Negotiations can drag on for hours with a constant exchange of the same arguments. It's part of the culture, walking in circles. Though without shuttle diplomacy. But you have to pay separate attention to context, as in Korea and Japan. If you didn't get a reply letter from a Chinese person to a service request, you have to understand from context whether the needed work will be done or not. Never give the Chinese chrysanthemums, clocks, scarves, pearls, or umbrellas — these are all associated with misfortune and death. And any sharp objects as a gift are a symbol of severing the relationship. Conversely, in low-context cultures the emphasis is on direct communication; negotiators prefer to communicate directly; information is conveyed explicitly; meaning is laid plainly into the message, expressed in words rather than hints.

Don't drill into a Chinese boss with your eyes, as is customary in the Western world. It's considered disrespect. And in Africa and Latin America, even aggression. Also, the Chinese don't like taking personal responsibility, so it's important to create the illusion of group responsibility. Don't directly demand payment for a conference — you need to find support for paying for it among other Chinese: the guys from another department went to a conference, they covered a conference last year, a colleague already got a conference ticket.

For IT companies it's important to attend the RISE conference in Hong Kong. And you must never criticize a Chinese person in front of their colleagues — that's a guaranteed loss of face and, accordingly, of the employee or partner. The Chinese won't allow themselves to say "the work was done badly." They'll praise, they'll dodge, and if everything's been done well, the communication will be fairly neutral. You have to ask the Chinese to speak directly. Delivery of wholesale goods from China to Moscow can take up to three months; direct rail is usually stuck in queues. When you meet a Chinese person, no bows — just a nod or a handshake. Save the bows for Thailand, Japan, and South Korea. A firm handshake is a sign of aggression.

From an interview standpoint, in Japan respondents try to smooth over their experience with the product and give a more socially expected answer. Rapport is established far from the first session, and with the Japanese it's very much needed. So instead of an interview, a good practice is to involve respondents in design-thinking sessions. Be sure to say a hundred times that it's not the respondent being tested but the product, since the well-known fear of losing face really does exist — everyone is afraid of being caught in a mistake. If a respondent can't handle a task, they won't admit it. Better to schedule interviews on weekends; no one will take time off work for you. But if you've agreed on an interview, the respondent will almost certainly come. As for prices — very expensive.

Website design in Asia is specific, and the products have poor implementation; it's a question not merely of translation but of the psychology of perception. Take two opposite cultures, Sweden and Japan: their sites will be very different — [McDonald's JP](https://www.mcdonalds.co.jp/) versus [McDonald's SE](https://www.mcdonalds.com/se/sv-se.html) (rigid hierarchy, not very colorful). The model of content consumption differs too: China is fast consumption; in India they don't like downloading content from the network, since the internet is expensive. Even on PlayStation, the blue cross means "confirm" and the red circle means "cancel," but in Japan this behavior is inverted. For African countries it's very important to use the target audience from the local population in photos, and they have a complex gradation by skin tone. Communications in South Africa, Kenya, and Nigeria go mainly through WhatsApp. The Middle East and North Africa (MENA) love to decorate their avatars, to make everything visually unique. If you have a navigator app, you need options to change the little car on the map. The standard European approach — take an app and translate it into Chinese — won't fly in China. You need to adapt the visual content too — for example, remove all the bones from the game graphics. A typical Chinese consumer has TikTok, Baidu, and WeChat on their home screen; everything else is grouped into folders. And they'll only agree to pay into Chinese banks. So instead of your own app, it's better to make an app inside WeChat.

And this is the place to talk about app stores, because launching a mobile app in Asia is a story of its own — not all of Southeast Asia, of course, but China, Japan, and Korea, which I know firsthand. The defining feature is the ban on Google Play in China and the general volatility of the Chinese market. In exchange, the Chinese have a couple of hundred local stores you can negotiate with. The main ones are 360 Mobile Assistant, YingYongBao (Tencent), Wandoujia (Pea Pod), Baidu Mobile Assistant, and PP Assistant. Income is usually split 50/50 between the developer and the store. You hand the stores an APK localized for China, agree on categories and ratings, and discuss what perks you can get from the store in exchange for the right to publish first. It's customary to hire a local partner to bring an app to the Chinese market, but it's very important to control their work — in the course of translation, the word *valenki* (felt boots) can turn into "booties," and then the visuals have to be redrawn too. Offline advertising with QR codes also goes over well in China, while buying traffic is not a very easy process. The App Store does exist in China, and holds just 27% of the market; Apple has solved most of the publishing problems there. On the surface it's all fairly simple — translate the texts and connect UnionPay for payment. You can take the trouble to embed into the ecosystem of the local big players (I've only had the chance to work with WeChat). The downside of ecosystems is that your app is then required to be exclusive to one of the three leading platforms.

The languages differ. In China and Singapore it's Simplified Chinese; in Taiwan and Hong Kong, Traditional Chinese. Chinese who live outside China don't learn the characters. The Chinese writing system needs far fewer symbols per word than a European one — the word "grocery" fits into two characters. But at the same time, all Asian languages demand more space than Latin ones: the characters are taller and need more air around them, because the symbol's design is complex. Where in the Latin script we're used to working with fonts either serif or sans-serif, Chinese characters have four styles of writing, each with its own particularities. The characters weigh more, too: in Latin one symbol equals one byte, in Cyrillic two bytes, and a single Chinese character may well take up all four. I used a simplified version of the font, and it still came to about 20 MB.

Posting the app is not enough — you have to advertise it. The general rules of marketing work for the Middle Kingdom as well: all sorts of 50%, 20%, 5% discounts, unique content. But the cultural norms differ greatly, especially colors and fonts, and those have to be studied. Social networks are a problem, since Facebook and Twitter are blocked in China, and a VPN is too expensive a luxury for most residents. So authorizing through the standard social networks won't work, but there are local players that let you authorize through their service: WeChat and Weibo. And it's worth keeping communication particularities in mind: when it's 10 a.m. in Moscow, it's already evening in China, so letters should be sent accounting for Asian colleagues' working hours, and preferably in their native language. For all the suffering listed above, you can gain access to a market with an ARPU higher than in the US (exception: children's games aren't monetized). It's worth clarifying what exactly you mean by Asian markets, though. Koreans love musical content, Turks greatly value apps for children's development, and the Chinese adore showing off their status. The difference between Korea and China is colossal, even if the personas are similar. The easiest to adapt to is the Korean audience; the hardest is China. But on the whole, in Southeast Asia players are calmer about pay-to-win compared with Western Europe and North America. And across all of Asia, mobiles have replaced consoles for long-term use — a story-driven gaming session can last hours.

If you're building a service from Russia specifically for China, a few things deserve attention from the very start. First, make sure you have a person who can speak Chinese and is actually a user of Chinese services — online translators don't know the standard names of interface elements, and double negatives almost never translate correctly. Their dates are written year-month-day, they have no surnames, and given names consist of one or two characters. The Chinese aren't good at English. Second, access time from Chinese territory to Russia is several seconds, so you'll have to acquire a server in Hong Kong. Third, the Great Firewall doesn't just block traffic but analyzes it, and can suddenly block anything at all; VPNs are blocked and exist only among Chinese who have contacts in Europe and America. Also, since Google is blocked, Google Fonts and many CDNs are unavailable, and extensions for Google Chrome — the most popular browser in China — are blocked too. The good news is the App Store is fine. Fourth, there are a great many viruses in China, so people try to download as few unfamiliar things as possible. And fifth, if you poke around the Chinese part of the internet, their sites are packed with content — long pages, lots of menus, everything expanded and on display at once.

So, to make an app inside WeChat you need a design. It'll be hard to Google (or Baidu) the top Chinese designers, so just do good work and test it on the Chinese. As for what "good" means for the Chinese — look at Meituan and Qihoo 360, and how nimbly they work. The development is simple and clear — just send the developers [this link](https://developers.weixin.qq.com/miniprogram/en/dev/). Except you have to keep the servers in China. Registering a domain, though, takes some fuss: you'll have to obtain a number of licenses (business operation in China, ICP, PWF). And in China the responsibility falls not on the company but on a specific person, and that person will have to be taken to China and photographed.

If you've decided to hire Chinese people as respondents, then first and foremost, schedule interviews on weekends. People don't take time off work. The moderator must be a native speaker, and small souvenir gifts are in order.

On the whole, China's entire e-commerce market is divided between Taobao and JD.com — Alibaba and Tencent, respectively, Jack Ma and Pony Ma. China has very little NFC; everything's via QR — that is, China differs from Europe. If some product isn't in China, it's probably not needed there, or it's in WeChat. A brand name should be comfortable for the Chinese to pronounce: McDuck sounds like 麦当劳 — *màidāngláo*, and Burger King is 汉堡王 — *Hànbǎowáng*. The Chinese are wealthy; for them there's no problem hiring 100 programmers in a week — there's a surplus of resources in the culture. And that's how a Chinese person's mind works — lots of resources, we're big, we're the biggest of all.

Color and China is a very hot topic. The easiest way to understand the meanings of colors is to go to the opera, since the costume colors reflect a character's nature. Red is responsible for loyalty and courage, black for integrity, white for cruelty. Also, mainland China doesn't like the color pink for anything business-related — you definitely can't, for instance, give pink sweets or wear a pink T-shirt. But in Hong Kong there are many people in pink clothing. Hong Kong is already ceding ground to mainland China, though, so the king of shopping is now Hainan. Hong Kong differs in many ways — they celebrate Christmas, for example.

The same with numbers. The number 8, for instance, is associated in Chinese culture with prosperity. If a dish consists of eight ingredients, it's a dish for luck. And in Japan, if you go to the toilet at 4:44 a.m. you can see a ghost, since the number 4 is death.

By the UN classification, Asia is five subregions:

- **East Asia:** the People's Republic of China, the Republic of China (Taiwan), the DPRK and the Republic of Korea, Mongolia, Japan;
- **West Asia:** Azerbaijan, Armenia, Bahrain, Georgia, Israel, Jordan, Iraq, Yemen, Qatar, Cyprus, Kuwait, Lebanon, the UAE, Oman, the Palestinian territories, Saudi Arabia, Syria, Turkey;
- **Southeast Asia:** Brunei, East Timor, Vietnam, Indonesia, Cambodia, Laos, Malaysia, Myanmar, Singapore, Thailand, the Philippines;
- **South Asia:** Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Iran, the Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri Lanka;
- **Central Asia:** Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan.

Six countries in Africa are developing very fast, one of them Rwanda. The most developed are Nigeria, Kenya, Senegal, and South Africa. But Nigeria is unsafe, with a lot of scams — though it does have cheap IT people (Andela). A Nigerian from the Igbo culture relies heavily on hierarchy and practicality in negotiations. Building warm relations is important, knowing the culture and citing historical precedents is important, chatting and laughing after the negotiations is important, telling the Nigerian what achievements await them after working with you is important. Many know English. In Kenya, a large amount of manufacturing is owned by people of Indian descent, and they have their own fenced-off communities in the country. So when starting your business in Africa, it's far from certain you'll be working with Africans. If a person introduces themselves as being from South Africa, there's a chance they're actually from Zimbabwe. On the internet, the younger the person (and in Africa the youth dominate), the greater the chance of having internet. In the cities there's internet almost everywhere, but only on phones. In Ethiopia or Sudan it's more likely to be one phone per family. The phones aren't from the high price category — TECNO or other Chinese phones. WhatsApp is very popular, as are other social networks. South Africa even has its own IT. If you walk into a room where people are sitting and talking, then in South Africa etiquette requires you to interrupt everyone and say hello. And don't rush.

North Africa: Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia — quite French-speaking. So-called Françafrique; they're fairly poor, though Senegal is more or less. East Africa is English-speaking — former British colonies. Indians completely cover the trader niche in East Africa. In the North it's the Lebanese.

In business, it's customary to divide Africa into five blocks: central, western, southern, eastern, and northern. East Africa is the most developed for business, and the South is the most backward. Investment mainly flows into South Africa, Angola, Congo, Egypt, Libya, Niger, Guinea, and the Ivory Coast.

Flights to Oceania are quite difficult: you fly to Japan and wait for the storms to stop. And there's the particularity of the Indian mentality, including the desire to copy everything they've implemented on external contract.

Let's not forget the CIS part of Asia, either. In Almaty the weather is very warm and sunny, while the winter is very cold. The average number of children in an Asian family is six. The first child is historically given to the grandparents to raise, since they're still young enough. The police aren't much trusted. Hashish grows in every flower bed.

In India the cost of a lead is comparable to Russia. The lead itself is cheaper but buys less willingly. The entire paying audience is English-speaking. Mobile is very popular; telemarketing works. Employees may vanish into the mist after the first paycheck and never return. LinkedIn profiles and achievements are usually fake, written to fit market requirements. Tasks have to be repeated many times and controlled very heavily, but give them creative freedom. Don't expect any planning from them — it's all in the moment. The IT people are in Bangalore, a good place for hiring. So if you're looking for the head of a small office, in India that's a salary of €1,000, while in Germany for a German it's €10,000. But in Germany you can find an Indian manager for €5,000.

Across the CIS, each country had its own specialization. Kazakhstan — oil and gas, industrial enterprises. Uzbekistan — agriculture and cotton. Tajikistan — hydroelectric stations. Turkmenistan — gas.

### Arab Countries

Although the Arab countries belong to Asia, we all understand that the MENA countries and China are as different as it's possible to be. So we'll consider the Mediterranean separately. Offline advertising is very important, even if you're an online service. Billboards, advertising on TV and in print. In advertising: use photos of women very carefully (face and hands only), no politics, no intimacy, no smoking, no alcohol. Dress women in the abaya, boys in the kandi/kandura. And the nuances of how the kandura is worn help you tell which country a particular Arab is from. The abaya doesn't have to be black, but black is the cheapest, and an abaya is usually much more expensive than a kandura.

If you launched your ad during prayer, no one will see it, and the first working day is Sunday (the exception is Dubai). There's Ramadan, which also strongly affects buying activity. At the end of Ramadan there's a peak of purchases. And they have their own calendar — the Hijri calendar.

Ramadan is an Islamic tradition and the ninth month of the calendar, when you can't eat anything from dawn to dusk, but after sunset you can avail yourself of the iftar buffet. The dates are tied to the lunar calendar; at the close of the season the holiday of Eid al-Adha (Kurban Bayram) is held. Relative to the Gregorian calendar, Ramadan shifts back by roughly 10–15 days each year. When Ramadan falls in the winter period, it's postponed more often because of the clouds, but nonetheless the eighth month of the calendar, Shaban, can't last more than 30 days. So Ramadan can begin even without the moon. To begin Ramadan, a group of people must go up into various mountains and watch for the moon over the course of the night. Saw it — Ramadan can begin. In the Middle East, Ramadan usually starts at the same time in all countries, except Oman.

In MENA countries they can easily ignore a meeting agreed on in advance — punctuality is low. If they did come to the meeting, they immediately expect numbers from you. "We want to buy mobile-app development from you, how much money?" — and these questions come right up to the spec stage. Often the clients aren't Arabs but Hindustanis. But if they are Arabs, then they're very, very keen on women.

Support — in Arabic; the text on the App Store icon — in Arabic; product reviews and SMM from local players. But English is needed too. In your service's search results, priority goes to those cities and countries with which there's no geopolitical enmity. By now it's clear, I think, that you have to be able to set up communication in Arabic. The language is interesting — it has no spaces and it's RTL, so adapting the interface begins with a horizontal inversion. By analogy with Persian and Hebrew.

In the Arab countries (Algeria, Bahrain, Djibouti, Egypt, the Islamic Republic of Iran, Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, Libya, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Sudan, Tunisia, the United Arab Emirates, Yemen) Facebook is very popular. Promoting a business, communication, finding a wife, shopping, and much more. No wonder the Facebook tracker sits in second place in the regional top.

Saudi Arabia is the capital of Islam — Mecca and Medina, the two main holy cities of Islam, are located there. Which means the country is quite conservative, and the umrah is performed often. There's a strict ban on alcohol — you can even be jailed. At best, a fine, arrest, and instant deportation. Pork is also banned, everyone knows that. But fish without scales is banned too, so it's better not to offer the rare salads of eel, crocodile, or shellfish. Looking at Dubai's example, they're taking very cautious, small steps toward a more Western culture — buying footballers, investing in e-sports — but they also try to keep a balance. And at the same time, the UAE also heeds Saudi Arabia's opinion in geopolitics. As does almost the entire Islamic world. It's Saudi Arabia, for example, that looks at the moon and decides when Ramadan should begin.

The companies are located in Riyadh, Dammam, Jeddah, and Yanbu. But different cities have different prayer times, which complicates scheduling meetings between branches. In Medina it's mostly tourism. Of the entertainments familiar to a Westerner, only cinemas can be named.

As for languages, the guys from Morocco speak a quite specific Arabic, rather aggressive in sound. They can understand practically all Arabic dialects, but few understand them because of the abundance of Spanish and French words. The Arabic of Egypt and Saudi Arabia is very similar, while the guys from Iran can only understand Arabic, not speak it. The word کتاب, *kitāb* (book), for example, is pronounced the same in any popular Arabic dialect. You can even predict the "noisiness" of a flight this way: Turkey and Egypt are very noisy, while Iran and Russia are quiet.
