# The Foundations of East Asian Symbolography

*Maksim Tcvetkov · Product Design and Security Lead*

---

East Asian characters are a beautiful example of visual expression. The languages of Asia hold a wealth of graphemes and strokes, but no alphabet. Historically, the characters trace back to burning bones and stones in a fire and reading the future from the marks that cracked across them.

There is a lovelier version of the same story, rooted in divination on turtle shells and animal bones. The practice was specific: hollows and holes were bored into a shell, the diviner spoke a request to the spirits, and a red-hot iron rod was pressed to the surface. The shell cracked, and from the shape of the cracks the diviner foretold events — then recorded the prophecy on that same shell. This is how the sacred writings that survive to our day were made.

Those writings held pictograms, and from pictograms the Chinese characters grew (*pico* means *to draw*). Ideograms, in turn, are built on pictograms. At first the characters were used to inscribe laws onto enormous iron cauldrons set up in the city. But a cauldron is hard to cart to another town, so writing moved to bamboo — and so the earliest books in China appeared. The slats were written right to left, top to bottom, then bound together with cords and carried to different parts of the country. The cords rotted away, and to this day excavations turn up these slats, their sequence painfully hard to reconstruct, because the order of the slats has been lost.

## The Visual Form

A character has three aspects: an outer shell, a semantic content, and a phonetic sound. In our own day a character is a written sign, not the equal of a picture of a particular object; pinning a character to an image is merely one way to memorize the drawing itself. Many characters are made of several signs — a phonetic part and the radical-keys that fix the meaning. By "signs" I mean graphemes, which are themselves composed of strokes. A character carrying a key accrues additional strokes as it shifts: 皮 (pí) → 波 (bō) → 婆 (pó). The key sets the sense; the phonetic sets the sound.

Loosely, characters fall into three kinds. **Ideograms** are ideas, units of meaning: 麻 denotes a fibre crop and is built from a broad roof 广 over 林, a grove — because the harvest was stored under the roof. **Phonograms** pair a key with a phonetic; in 妈 ("mother") there sits 马 ("horse"), and the two are close in sound. These make up nearly 80% of all characters in Chinese. **Symbolograms** work as pure signs: 上 *up*, 下 *down*. To these one can add a fourth, **borrowed** characters: 来, for instance, means *to come* and reads *lái*, but 来 once stood for barley. People said *lái* for *come*, yet had no character for it — so the character's meaning shifted to fill the gap.

![A signboard reading 明满学校 — "Mingman School."](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/Untitled-1-1.jpg)

The semantic content and the drawing of a character do not, in themselves, announce its pronunciation. In modern Chinese the correlation between a character and its reading runs, by various estimates, from 20% to 60%; in practice it is closer to 20%. Now and then the drawing does betray the sound — 荷 (hè), rendered in technical documentation as *load* and not widely known among translators, shares its shape with 何, read the same (hé) but in a different tone, which translators will read correctly. A character is pronounced as a single syllable, and each may carry up to five tones — and, with them, up to five meanings — while different provinces pronounce things differently. Take 啊: depending on the tone — *a, ā, á, ǎ, à* — it voices a different emotion, the fourth tone most often reserved for cursing. The everyday words 买 and 卖 mean *buy* and *sell*; they read alike but in different tones, *mǎi* and *mài*. In southern China the pronunciation is Cantonese; the north has altogether different dialects, while Beijing's speech sits closer to early Chinese. Somewhere like Suzhou you can meet six to eight tones. Seven major dialect groups are usually distinguished: Mandarin (Putonghua), Gan, Hakka, Min, Wu, Xiang, and Cantonese. In the 1950s, Mandarin was designated the official dialect for all of China.

![](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Group-118.jpg)

Older characters were built from a far greater number of strokes, and committing them to memory was hard. So one aim of the writing reform carried out by the Chinese government in the 1960s was to simplify the characters by cutting their strokes — which made it possible to teach reading and writing to vast numbers of people and to streamline the characters visually: 飛 → 飞, 雞 → 鸡, 學 → 学, *開 → 开*. For the most part it was the keys that were simplified; only around 600 characters were heavily reworked. The classical forms live on in Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Macao — and among masters of calligraphy.

And yet the Chinese deeply love the visual expression that a dense thicket of strokes affords. When sinologists tell you a character tops out at thirty or forty strokes and that this is the most possible, you can be sure that in some province the owner of a small eatery has already painted a signboard whose name runs to eighty or ninety.

Emoji, too, carry meanings other than the European ones. 👌, for example, means *thirty* — in life and online alike.

Linguistics has a term, *prosody*: the rhythm and melody of speech.

The pen, it is said, is one of the character's chief enemies. In books a character's strokes vary in thickness — but that variation came from writing with nib and brush, and for understanding and recognizing a character, thickness is unnecessary. Stroke weight is a purely aesthetic component. See for yourself, below, how native speakers write for their countrymen:

![A handwritten note.](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/photo_2018-12-09_20-16-00.jpg)
![事事顺利,贵人相助 — "may all things go smoothly; may you be aided by benefactors."](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/ff.jpg)

Far more important is to watch the *length* of the strokes, which bears heavily on meaning. Consider 七 *seven* against 匕 *ladle*. Or the classic mistake: 土, here glossed as *a country bumpkin*, against 士 *warrior* — the first differs from the second in the relative length of its horizontal strokes. And here the difference is harder still to spot: 妺 against 妹. One more thing to keep in mind: the drawing of a character itself can vary with the style of writing — Zhuanshu (seal), Lishu (clerical), Xingshu (running), Caoshu (cursive), Kaishu (regular).

Such unfortunate distortions of meaning are avoided by drawing a character's strokes in the correct order. This matters, because the hand behaves differently depending on the slant of the pen and the direction of its movement. There are three basic rules of stroke order:

1. top to bottom, left to right;
2. horizontals first, then verticals, then strokes that fall to the left, then those that fall to the right — — | \ / — provided the horizontal and vertical do not cross;
3. for enclosures such as 冂 冋 回, the outside first, then the inside, then the closing stroke.

A fuller algorithm exists as well:

1. Horizontal strokes come first, then verticals and falling strokes.
2. The left-falling stroke is written before the right-falling one.
3. The character is written top to bottom.
4. Then left to right.
5. The outer contours of the character come first, then the strokes inside.
6. The stroke that closes the contour along the bottom is written last.
7. The central vertical is drawn first, then the strokes to its sides.

Most words in modern Chinese consist of two syllable-characters, with the stress on the last. See the example below, from a Chinese café:

## Entering Meaning

To record the sound of characters, the Pinyin system is used — a transliteration into the Latin script. It is precisely by this romanization that characters are typed on an English keyboard, with the desired one then chosen from the list offered.

*Pinyin* literally means *to join sounds*, and is usually rendered as "transcription," "phonetics," or "pronunciation" of Chinese. It is the most successful instance of romanizing standard Chinese — a phonetic notation and transliteration into the Latin alphabet — in use across all of China, and it has become the principal means of computerizing the language. Because Chinese is tonal, Pinyin provides corresponding marks, placed over the vowel finals to indicate each of the tones:

![Pinyin tone marks placed over vowel finals.](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/4xO7b7UtJKA.jpg)

Typing on a phone, the Chinese use both the standard QWERTY layout and the nine-key layout (T9) they grew used to in the pre-smartphone era. For those who are not strong on Pinyin — the system is fairly recent, and the older generation may well not know it — there is manual input: simply drawing the character with a finger, or voice input. You can also enter a sequence of strokes, and in Hong Kong you will meet the Sucheng/Cangjie keyboard layout.

There is also a numeric "Chinese," more of a written, youth register, where digits stand in for words by sound — the 2 (二, *èr*), for instance, becomes associated with 爱 (*ài*, *love*), as in the famous 520, which reads like "I love you."

![Numeric Chinese in the wild.](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/Untitled-1.jpg)

Obsolete input methods can still be found. Wubi, for one, is a faster way to type but hard to master, and it is now difficult to find software with decent Wubi support — so even devotees of the method are migrating to Pinyin, which is precisely the state's goal: standardization. In Taiwan, Cangjie and Boshiamy are popular; in Japan, Kanji input.

## Technical Considerations

Thanks to Adobe and Google, we have at least one good, free typeface that takes a coherent view of the CJK languages — Chinese, Japanese, and Korean: [Source Han Sans](https://github.com/adobe-fonts/source-han-sans). Japan uses Han ideography (*hanzi*, or *kanji*), so a single typeface can serve several languages. For mobile, you might look at Droid Sans Fallback. But Chinese forms suit Japanese poorly, which is why the Japanese created simplified, stylized versions of certain characters — Hiragana and Katakana — now used alongside the Chinese ones.

The choice of typefaces is not wide, because Chinese fonts are hard to make: you must draw thousands of characters for the traditional glyphs. Fonts with that many characters weigh upward of 30 MB — look at [Noto CFF OTF](https://www.google.com/get/noto/help/cjk/), which in its full build runs to 34 MB. No surprise that most Chinese sites rely on the system font rather than serving the user a typeface, or else use fonts under 1 MB that hold around 3,000 characters. But even that is too heavy a file for a simple web page, so you can forget about `@font-face`. The upshot is that there are few workable families to begin with, and good ones are a mere handful. The most commonly used are PMingLiU (serif), Microsoft JhengHei (sans-serif), Microsoft YaHei (微软雅黑), SimHei (黑体), SimSun (宋体) — the font for Simplified Chinese — and Google Noto Sans CJK. At Tencent I was advised to use the Microsoft YaHei variants FZLanTingHei, FZLanTingKanHei, FZLanTingYuan, and FZZhengHei; the last of these I used for the final mockups.

When you want something more expressive than these workhorses, there is a richer, more artistic seam to mine. Among the more interesting faces are Alibaba PuHuiTi, the Founder family 方正字庫 (FZNSTFW), and [SimHei](https://docs.microsoft.com/ru-ru/typography/font-list/simhei); and the site [chinesefont.org](https://chinesefont.org) is a good place to wander for striking specimens.

Apple recommends the following families for Pages:

- *Simplified Chinese:* PingFang SC
- *Traditional Chinese (Taiwan):* PingFang TC
- *Traditional Chinese (Hong Kong and Macao):* PingFang HK
- *Korean:* Apple SD Gothic Neo
- *Japanese:* Hiragino Sans (sans-serif) or Hiragino Mincho (serif)

It is worth saying separately that Chinese fonts must also be bought, and they cost more than European ones, because drawing 3,000 characters is dearer than drawing the 270 glyphs of a European typeface. If a font's name carries the prefix FZ, it was developed by [Founder](http://www.foundertype.com/); similar abbreviations in a name point to the maker. Even the maximal version of a European family — support for a hundred languages plus extra symbols — comes to only 840 characters. And Asian families are never designed solo; this is the work of a team of dozens of designers.

There is also a division into serif and sans-serif faces, by analogy with the Latin. In sans-serif fonts the stroke weight is roughly even, while serif faces are more artistic; sans-serifs are more often used for short texts and headings.

![Serif versus sans-serif Chinese type.](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/Artboard.png)

There is a term, 春联 (chūnlián) — the red strips of paper bearing wishes for the Chinese New Year, a festival already more than 4,000 years old, celebrated in late January or early February. One of its notable features is the pasting of these red strips around the entrance to the home: to the left and right of the door, with smaller strips above and on the door itself, always on a red ground in gold or black characters. And you must not swap the left and right sides.

![春联 (chūnlián) — New Year couplets pasted around a doorway.](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/2020-01-14-10.33.39.jpg)
![](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/вф.jpg)

In bad fonts — and in good ones too — extra strokes may be drawn onto characters. For this reason Apple sometimes uses images rather than text for headings on the Chinese version of its site. Some families lack the rendering even for all the keys. Different regions of East Asia may use different glyphs for the same meaning, and beyond that there are different styles — Xingshu (行書) and Caoshu (草書) among them. Do you feel the heap of comprehension problems facing a certain part of the population? The good news: Unicode supports every character needed, independent of UTF-8, UTF-16, or UTF-32 — more than 80,000 of them, as you can confirm by wandering through [this reference](http://www.loc.gov/marc/specifications/specchareacc.html). In practice, though, about 3,500 characters are used, which is enough to print magazines and newspapers.

The minimum size for a character is held to be 8×8 px, the size to which [SimSun](https://docs.microsoft.com/en-us/typography/font-list/simsun) is adapted — yet even native speakers struggle to make out characters that small and read by context rather than picking out each one. Better to aim for the 12–16 px range; anything under 16 px makes for hard reading. In one project I used 18 px and had no trouble. Line spacing is best kept between 120% and 145% of the type size. And bear in mind that characters are harder to read from a distance than Latin text:

![Characters are harder to read at a distance than Latin text.](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/ghjgj.jpg)

All the small marks, punctuation included, occupy exactly the same em square as an ordinary character — for instance ，。？」. This is an attempt to make the font monospaced, and it is wrong: punctuation should not take up so large an em square. Just look at how it reads: 「运动员」. No other inter-character spacing needs adding, because in Chinese it is not the word but the character that holds value. In Old Chinese a single word was always written with a single character, and there was no punctuation at all; reading such texts worked like this — you read, and read, and read, and suddenly the picture forms in your head. Each character carries meaning, often many meanings, so you cannot guess its sense from the look of the character or its radical. Take 水 (shuǐ), which is *water* as a standalone character, but as a component is written 氵 — as in this left-side placement, 洋 (yáng), *ocean*.

![水 "water" as a standalone character and as the 氵 radical.](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/gfhf-2.jpg)

Chinese websites unsettle a European with their lack of generous whitespace: the characters are bulky in themselves and are not separated by spaces, and there are no capital letters. But the text does align left to right, top to bottom, as in European languages — at least in most cases. Now and then you will run into top-to-bottom, right-to-left writing, mainly in highly formal documents, or in artistic settings from Taiwan and Hong Kong, or for poetry; vertical writing brings additional adjustments to punctuation, such as a period centred in the character's cell.

When you build a site, it is very useful, in declaring a font, to list the family names first in English and then in Chinese, since the font file may be named differently in the system. And, by analogy with Arial / Helvetica, you should specify fonts for both Windows and macOS.

## Reaching These Markets

Getting the characters right is half the work; getting the product in front of the people who read them is the other half — and it runs on a different toolkit entirely, one worth knowing before you ship.

If you are aiming an app at a Japanese store without reading the language yourself, services carry you a long way. Google Trends comes first, then [goodkeyword](https://goodkeyword.net/) for discovery, and [weblio.jp](https://www.weblio.jp/) for synonyms; to break phrases down into individual keywords, [romajidesu.com/translator](http://www.romajidesu.com/translator) does the segmenting.

Bringing a product to mainland China is a heavier lift, and the path is well worn. You create an account on Baidu, rebuild the site in Chinese and host it inside China, and obtain the G and ICP licences before running paid contextual advertising. You commission reviews on Zhihu and seed them through KOLs — key opinion leaders — and you keep the brand's conversation alive on WeChat, Weibo, and Xiaohongshu.

This is the core of what you need to know to minimize the chance of stumbling when you work for Asian companies. I very much hope you will avoid mistakes when building products for Asian markets, and that you will fold every new piece of knowledge from this article into your interface design. Otherwise you may hear, aimed your way, the dismissive *guā lǎowài* (瓜老外) — a foolish, European-looking foreigner who poorly understands the language and culture of China. Want to design interfaces for China? 了解一下！
