# Logic and Clients

> Translated and adapted from the Russian original by Maxim Tsvetkov.


## What logic is for

Logic is an instrument for knowing the truth, and truth is the single correct choice among those available. The search for solutions across the whole of life is a foundational part of our thinking. It is not the task of logic to find a way of mapping a person's mental algorithms, which is why phrases like "female logic," "a different logic," or "the logic of this group of people" do not fit: for a professional, logic cannot come in varieties — there is only one. And that single logic seeks an answer to the question of how we ought to think in order to arrive at reliable knowledge about the object under study.

The point of studying logic is the ability to argue. The base law for successful argumentation is the law of identity, which holds that any thought must be clear, precise, and simple. The law forbids using the same word in different senses or pouring the same sense into different words, forbids creating ambiguity, and forbids drifting off the topic.

## The law of identity

Take the sentence: "Because of poor preparation at the conference, the designer repeatedly could not change the slides." The breach of the law of identity is obvious — whose poor preparation, the designer's or the conference's? — and from that breach unclear statements are born. In symbolic form the law looks like a → a, where *a* is any concept, statement, or whole line of reasoning. Violating the law of identity is a dreadful error for designers and copywriters, the kind for which art directors fire interns.

But the law can be broken deliberately, in order to confuse an interlocutor and prove a false thought to them, and that is the moment a sophism is born. A sophism, then, is an outwardly correct proof of a false thought achieved by deliberately breaking the laws of logic.

## When the client's experience outweighs yours

I have written before that a client rejects your arguments whenever their personal life experience is given priority over your professional experience. Experience teaches us that the world we live in is marked by a certain stability and repeatability, so citing successful design decisions similar to your own can sometimes help to override the client's experience. And if you cannot argue the point but know your solution is correct, nothing remains but to appeal to intuition — which is the same logic, simply handing you a finished answer without visible argumentation.

## Building an argument

In other cases, proving your case means confusing the interlocutor through long reasoning. At the end of any chain of reasoning there must always be a conclusion that serves as the transition from statements A1, A2, A3 to inference B1. For example: if you have apples, you can eat them; if you have no apples, you cannot eat them; if your apples are rotten, the rotten part must be cut out, and then they can be eaten — therefore your apples can be eaten, but on some you must first cut away the rotten parts. The first three statements are premises and the last is the conclusion. To any other line of reasoning you can reply on the principle that the presence of a cause does not refute the effect.

## Paradox versus contradiction

The unpleasant phenomenon of paradox deserves separate mention: two opposing assertions, each backed by a perfectly convincing proof. Consider the statement that my iPhone is newer than another iPhone that is newer than mine — that is a contradiction, and contradiction and paradox are different things. In such a situation it is always important to steer the conversation toward paradox and then exit it by the methods described above, without letting an outright contradiction arise.

## The trap of authority

Very often, too, art directors love to prove their point from a position of force: "make the logo bigger, because I said so." From a logical standpoint, proof through authority is a fallacy. Appeal to personality is inadmissible; everything must be argued. Einstein was sometimes wrong, and Hitler regularly voiced sensible ideas — which is exactly why proof through authority is poor. Perhaps that is why our schools have no subject called logic: a child who commands logic can put a teacher and their authority in a foolish position rather often. A line from Leonardo da Vinci fits here. He knew well, he wrote, that because he was not well-read certain proud men would think themselves entitled to censure him as a man without a bookish education — foolish people, who did not understand that, as Marius answered the Roman patricians, he could answer them: you who have adorned yourselves with the labours of others will not grant me the right to my own. The conclusion: experience is set against authority as a new truth is set against the traditionally dominant errors. Better to use arguments — and history tolerates no subjunctive mood.

## Formal and dialectical logic

Be ready to meet people who have finished a philology faculty; they wield not only formal logic but dialectical logic quite skilfully. The difference shows in symbols. *A = B or A ≠ B* is formal logic, the numeric kind. *A = B and simultaneously A ≠ B* is dialectical logic. A more humanities-flavoured example: a human is both an animal and not an animal; a human embryo is at once a human and not a human; an adolescent is at once an adult and a non-adult, and at the same time a child and not a child. This is the dialectical type, and at this point lawyers tend to be dumbfounded, since they are drilled in formal logic, where a thing can be either A or B and the rest is demagoguery. The way out of dialectical tangles is Sherlock's favourite method — deduction.

## Deduction and proof

Logic is a science not only for comparing finished lines of reasoning but for creating arguments, and that is called deductive reasoning. "My acquaintances often put on socks, therefore putting on socks is a property of all people" is a poor example to analyse. But phrase it the other way — "all people put on socks, therefore my acquaintances must put them on too" — and you have deduction. Sherlock Holmes was not lying: deduction is reasoning from the general to the particular.

Now translate that into a real case. You have a project to present to a client, so prepare a thesis, arguments, and a demonstration. The thesis is the concept requiring proof; the arguments are the quality judgments used in proving it; the demonstration is the logical link between thesis and arguments. Proof is either direct or indirect. Direct proof moves from the arguments to confirmation of the thesis, and it is the type used in court practice, in science, in polemics, and widely in statistical reports, documents, and rulings. The client can of course always say no — but that is a refusal of your proposal, not of you, so always ask "why?", and you will have a continuation of the dialogue with new theses and arguments. Indirect proof, by contrast, establishes the truth of a proposed judgment by proving the falsity of the judgment that excludes it. It is justified when there are no arguments for direct proof — a plain establishment of the falsity of the judgment that contradicts the thesis, plus a measure of intuition.

## Understanding what the client actually needs

A thesis can be anything, but it is best if it is your client's *need*. We need few things — mostly food, a home, family, safety — and a need arises when its object exists in a single copy: a wife, a son, one's health. A person in need is in a knowingly losing position, so showing that you are in need is bad for you; an indirect sign of need is excessive sociability, the need to appear clever. If you display need, people will work with you out of pity. But if you show the client that *they* need you and your services, the cooperation will be long and perhaps even fruitful. Your client makes decisions on an emotional level, not by the numbers alone; the numbers are merely the justification for an emotional decision. Influence the interlocutor's emotions by agreeing with them.

This is also why you must not rush off headlong to execute a client's instructions. Think first about the result the client wants. If the client asks you to make a mascot character, that does not at all mean they need a character; most likely they need a way to make the company more recognisable, and *that* is your real task, with the character only one solution among many — and not the best one. Keep asking yourself questions: how will the company be useful, why should anyone remember it, is there any desire to associate oneself with its image? Asking questions is the best way to understand a client's tasks and solve them.

It follows that responsibility for the overall visual style is shared, not the designer's alone. If a designer draws a site in skeuomorphism and the client then demands it be redone in flat, the designer would be at fault only where there is no manager — because agreeing the general visual style always happens in the early stages of cooperation, whether the designer, the manager, or the art director does it being a matter for each particular shop. Whether the client likes skeuomorphism or flat is the client's call, not the designer's; the client must like what the contractor delivers, which is a basic requirement of the work, for otherwise why would the client deal with such a company at all? Before starting, ask the client to assess your studio's portfolio and let them choose a style that suits, because if none of your people draws in the required style there is no point beginning — there is no sense in an avant-garde artist attempting photorealism. The quality of design for a particular audience can be measured with a survey such as SUPR-Q, which includes questions on aesthetics.

## Running the conversation

Always open with a generalised reminder of why you have gathered and what results the conversation needs to reach by its end. Don't be shy about joking — self-irony is a sign of an intelligent person and clears the air — and it is better to think up the joke in advance. Close the meeting by discussing the next step.

## A little probability

Without some grounding in probability theory you cannot make weighted decisions. Take the classic example: a die is rolled twice; what is the probability that the first roll shows at least 2 and the second at least 3? Lacking other inputs, assume all six faces are equally likely, and take the total probability as one. With two rolls there are 6 × 6 = 36 outcomes, so draw a square of six rows and six columns — that grid is your event space. The first roll satisfies "at least 2" in five ways ({2, 3, 4, 5, 6}) and the second satisfies "at least 3" in four ways ({3, 4, 5, 6}), giving 5 × 4 = 20 favourable cells out of 36, or 5/9. But this is a chance, not a 100 % forecast.

Go further. What is the probability that on either of the two rolls no more than 2 comes up? Shade every cell where a 1 or a 2 appears on at least one die, and you get 20 shaded cells against 16 empty ones, so the probability is 20/36, which reduces to 5/9 — more than half, and again only a chance.

Another example. A father promises his son a gift if the son wins at least two games of tennis in a row, playing against the father and against the club champion, the champion obviously playing better than the father. Two orders of play are offered: father–champion–father, or champion–father–champion. Before thinking it through, the temptation is to pick the order where the word "champion" appears fewer times — but reread the condition and notice the word *in a row*. Let the probability of beating the father be *a* and of beating the champion be *b*, so that b < a ≤ 1. The probability of losing to the father both times is p = (1 − a)(1 − a) = 1 − 2a + a², and the probability of beating him at least once is 1 − p = 2a − a² = a(2 − a). In the first order the son's chance of winning works out to b · a(2 − a); in the second, to a · b(2 − b). Since b < a, we have (2 − a) < (2 − b), and it becomes perfectly clear that the second order is significantly more promising for the son. You can reach the same place arithmetically rather than algebraically: rate the difficulty of beating the father as 0 and the champion as 1, then compare 0–1–0 against 1–0–1. In the first you must win against the champion and at least once against the father; in the second you must win against the father and at least once against the champion — and the second is the better bet.

And one more. One doctor says the patient is fully healthy, and statistically this doctor is right 90 % of the time; a second doctor says the same and has the same record. What is the probability that after both visits the patient really is healthy? You could answer like the joke — fifty-fifty, healthy or not — but the more interesting answer is 1 − 0.1² = 99 %, since the chance of both doctors being wrong is 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.01.

The same compounding logic governs everyday analytics. Suppose you have a button, you run an A/B test and the clicks rise by 70 %, then you run a second test changing the colour and text and clicks rise by another 40 %. You do not add the percentages; you compound them. The first test takes the original 100 % to 1.7, and the second multiplies again: 1.4 × 1.7 = 2.38, a 138 % rise in the button's conversion relative to the original — and remember that sales matter more than conversion, with views and wish-lists serving as proxy metrics for sales.

When the numbers themselves need crunching across a month or a forecast — say a new search-promotion service is launched, the older services sell less, yet the average income rises and you must compute monthly income and project to year-end — the work runs through one-dimensional (linear) arrays, one array meaning one ordinal index. In Google Sheets that is the `ArrayFormula` function: `=arrayformula(A1:A*B1:B)` captures all the values across the columns, and `=arrayformula(sum(A1:A4*B1:B4))` sums the products, entered with Command + Shift + Enter. For two-dimensional arrays you reach for `=row(H7)` and `=column(H7)` to return the row and column.

## The Kano model

Worth adding here is the spring concept devised by Noriaki Kano. A feature has two parameters — functionality and satisfaction — and falls into one of five categories: Must-be (M), One-Dimensional (O), Attractive (A), Indifferent (I), and Reverse (R). Deliver only the must-be work and the client will be dissatisfied; overdeliver, doing more than expected, and the client will almost certainly return to that contractor. At a retrospective, discuss which features can move the client's attitude and impression from the red line to the orange and then to the green.

A caveat sits alongside this, though: do not count on delighted clients to spread the word, at least in the premium segment, where word-of-mouth practically does not work. Good service does not sell itself to a small audience, and referral schemes do not work either — and if one suddenly does, that is the first sign of commoditisation.

## Lessons from e-grocery

Take the e-grocery industry as a worked example. Is the quality of groceries delivered to the home an attractive characteristic or a must-be one? For quality to stay stable you must give the front-line staff a stake in it — for instance, a constant line of contact between courier and client, with the client seeing the courier's photo and phone number, and the same for the in-store pickers; and if the people did well, the client can tip them in the app. Why is this hard? Answer the question: a fresh tomato is *what*, exactly — green, soft, large? In a classic shop the buyer chooses the item they like and the rotten or expired goods get written off, whereas in e-grocery the client tasks a picker with selecting the ripest premium-segment bananas, and the picker has never eaten a premium-segment banana and cannot fulfil the order. The second problem is geography: everyone starts with the million-plus cities and works down to the half-million ones, but Moscow and St Petersburg are only about 20 % of Russia's grocery turnover, around 30 % with their surroundings, leaving the other 70 % spread across the whole country — and cities differ in behaviour, some million-plus cities having weak penetration of electronic services. There is also a dissonance between the offline and online experience: the corner-shop format always means not the best price and a thin range, the alternative being a hypermarket you have to drive to but where the goods are cheap, and which is large enough that you will do some walking. The e-grocery analogy is slow delivery from a wide range versus fast delivery from the limited stock of a local warehouse — but here people value not time (this is not the restaurant model) so much as predictability and consistency. After this tour of e-grocery, try writing out fifty development tasks and giving each one a Kano characteristic.

## Knowing who you're designing for

Describing a target audience as a plain bulleted list is the common approach, but a persona deserves something more complete. For any persona, set out a photo or illustration capturing the image; a name; a quote or life motto; demographics (sex, age, marital status, role, industry, city, country); personality traits (extravert or introvert, and for games necessarily achiever, killer, explorer, or socialiser, plus Jung's archetypes); a short biography with life goals, motivations, and pains; and the technologies they use (the internet, software, social networks, iOS or Android, whether they watch TV). Reports of the "Who plays mobile games?" kind are worth a look, too, for the many ways they visualise audiences and personas.

I like to add a layer of metaprograms. Reference is whether a person trusts their own or others' opinion (internal, mixed, or external), surfaced by asking how they knew they had done the work well. Motivation runs from striving-toward to avoidance, with a mixed middle. There is the focus of comparison — looking for differences or for similarities; the size of generalisation — staying general or diving into detail; the direction of thinking — from general to particular or the reverse; modality; and the underlying motives of power, affiliation, achievement, or safety. The right questions tease these out: to find motivation, ask what would make staff exceed the plan; to find an orientation toward process or result, ask them to describe their most interesting work project or the process of work at their last job. If the persona is a specialist, you need specific criteria — for the self-employed, the tax regime, profession, and type of activity; the nature of employment (a side job or full-time); their methods of finding clients, the number of clients, and the sources of orders. I also like to add a slice through the Rossiter–Percy grid, a little table of high and low motivation against high and low involvement, where negative motivation is everyday spending such as paying a doctor, a plumber, or a courier and positive motivation is satisfying personal wants — a new smartphone, a trip to the mountains, a party in Dubai — while high involvement is the basic needs (groceries, safety, health) and low involvement is the expensive craft coffee and breakfast on a summer terrace.

## Leadership, analysis, and the business around the work

It helps to know what business analysis even is, and what a business analyst does. The work runs through defining the business requirements; defining the approach to working with requirements; understanding who the stakeholders are; capturing the functional requirements; analysing, prioritising, and documenting them; communicating them to the interested parties (new requirements for the call centre, say); and managing the validation of requirements. Requirements descend in order — business requirements, then user requirements, then functional and non-functional requirements — and an error in the business requirements means everything downstream carries the error too. When data is scarce, the COCOMO calculator at softwarecost.org helps: a tool for estimating development time, originally from the Pentagon, after a clever fellow in the 1980s noticed you could look at the line count of code and gauge how long a product would take to build. That is the Constructive Cost Model, with COCOMO II and Function Point Analysis as alternatives.

On leadership models, the old, sound PAEI framework by Ichak Adizes answers the question well. P is the Producer (the team lead) who makes the product, writes the code, and reaches results. A is the Administrator (the project manager) responsible for process, systematisation, control, and formalisation. E is the Entrepreneur (the product manager) who sets the goal and direction and is about growth and exploration. I is the Integrator who fuses it all into horizontal links, engaging and harmonising the team. A leader can be dissonant or resonant. The dissonant leader cannot show empathy, stands apart from the team, does not grasp burnout, gives mostly negativity, and does not understand others' opinions — typically the demagogue or the clueless, toxically optimistic leader. Such a person may follow an authoritarian model, the one who alone knows what to do, treating people as instruments, which is exactly the right thing in a crisis (a nod to the quarantine). Or an ambitious model, telling no one anything while expecting staff to guess, being excellent specialists but poor teachers who recheck their people's work into the night — a style fit only for highly qualified, highly motivated specialists. The resonant leader works from the opposite end, understanding the team's emotions: the democratic kind who manages jointly with colleagues, the comradely kind who is about communication, the coaching kind, and the idealistic kind who motivates by painting a beautiful image of the future. The standard chain over a company's life stages runs authoritarian → democratic → comradely.

There is also the practical matter of contracting with foreigners. For the union states, ordinary civil-law (GPH) contracts do the job; a platform like solar-staff.com helps, though it takes 10 % commission plus 20 % VAT, while the self-employed are the most cost-effective at just +6 %.

## A closing puzzle

I hope this note helps you structure your approach to arguing with clients, colleagues, and subordinates. As a parting morsel, a logic puzzle: there is an iPhone 4, an iPhone 5, and an iPhone 6 — which is older? Perhaps the device produced earlier is the oldest, and its version number ought to be lower than its seniors'. But there is also the notion of being "senior by rank," and a 25-year-old lieutenant fresh from the military faculty may well outrank a 35-year-old warrant officer. So the iPhone 4 is the senior one, while at the same time version 6 is higher than version 4. Where is the truth?
