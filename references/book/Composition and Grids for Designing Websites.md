# Composition and Grids for Designing Websites
### From the academic principles of illustration to the modular grid on screen

*Maksim Tcvetkov · 2 October 2015*

Let's talk about composition, and about color within composition. For an illustrator, composition can't have fixed, unshakable laws, but there are certain nuances worth knowing all the same. I'll say up front that I won't be laying out dogma, drawing diagrams, or piling up examples. What follows are the important observations from the academic school that are worth keeping in mind throughout the whole process of building an illustration or a design. These aren't binding laws, though — book illustration has its own rules of composition, decorative-applied art has its own, painting has its own. I'll try to describe only the core means for achieving expressiveness in your work.

The most basic thing to arm yourself with is **rationality**. Composition is usually built around a recognizable shape: there's the rule of thirds, the triangular composition, the rectangle, the rhombus, the circle. Any form in a composition has to be tied to its function and worked out constructively. Every part of the composition has to be subordinate to the other elements that make up the overall shape, with perspective accounted for. There will naturally be primary and secondary elements, but the composition should be at least 90% resolved. That 90% of the work should go toward structuring the viewer's attention; the remaining 10% can be left open for the story to develop.

**Contrast.** In composition, contrast can be split along a number of lines: big–small, long–short, wide–narrow, many–few, static–moving, horizontal–vertical, volumetric–flat, story/atmosphere, and so on — and alongside all that, don't forget the seven color contrasts: dark/light, warm/cold, complementary colors, simultaneous contrast, contrast of saturation, and area of color. Contrast doesn't have to live only in color or form, either — it can be a contrast of outlooks, of societies. You could, of course, take the easy route and make a composition symmetrical instead of contrasting, but such a designer or artist isn't worth much. When some object or character is lit with a stronger tonal contrast than everything around it, our eye locks onto that object.

![Contrast in composition](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Group-33151.png)

The simplest example of formal contrast is enlarging the point size. That trick calls for blocks of small, set text nearby. Half of all designers rescue a shaky layout with a massive headline.

**Contrast** of complementary colors runs like this:

▪ yellow–violet
▪ orange–blue
▪ red–green

All these pairs always contain yellow, red, and blue (think back to third grade, mixing gouache). Since a mix of yellow, red, and blue always gives gray, logic says a mix of complementary colors should give gray too. But whichever color you use, remember that every individual color on a canvas carries its own associative qualities. Red, blue, and yellow give the strongest contrast; violet, orange, and green are significantly weaker; and white and black are the strongest of all in their contrast — which is why monochrome illustrations can grab attention every bit as well as color ones.

Contrasts of material are possible too — smooth against rough, hard against soft, glossy against matte. You can convey a tactile sensation, feel out the material and render it correctly. Texture, for instance, can be contrasted on the principle of expressive, delicate patterns set against small elements that blur into a uniform mass. Look at Tintoretto's "Portrait of a Man" — all the attention concentrates on the man's head, and the clothing barely differs from the background. Go further still and think of portraits set against landscapes, and it's easy to see how the landscape loses badly to the portrait in color, rhythm, and finish.

![Contrast of material and finish](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/h.jpg)

Contrast of light and dark, big and small: a mid-toned background can read as either light or dark, depending on whether the figure placed on it is black or white. A large dark shape will always seem more striking if there's something small and white beside it. And contrasts of important and secondary are extremely common even in the most basic student still-life studies.

**Accents and rhythm.** I don't think rhythm needs much explaining — it's a ship rocking on the waves, a lamp blinking monotonously, a sledgehammer striking a heated blade. But how do you actually use rhythm in composition? Start from the fact that rhythm is everywhere in our lives: the turn of the seasons, the days of the week, intricate ornaments, the beating of a heart, the rhythm of tables arranged in an interior, the rhythm of brickwork. Rhythm is order. It's meant to divide and unite the elements of a composition at once, while holding a certain structure. A simple example is alternating dark and light patches to create contrast and make figures recognizable. If you look at rhythm as a principle in composition, you can start from plain lines — enough of them, and they inevitably create the illusion of depth.

![Lines creating rhythm and depth](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Frame-432.png)

Rhythm, accordingly, can be used to suggest perspective, which is if not the key part of a composition then certainly a very important one. It shows up even in small things: the direction of the strokes in an illustration can help convey one intention or another. If you're drawing something airy and hovering, you shouldn't use horizontal strokes. Strokes running downward along a form show rigidity; lines running through a form show softness; ellipse-like strokes following the direction of a form serve the form itself. Rhythm doesn't have to mean repeating identical elements — it can be motion, what's called "progressive rhythm."

An example of rhythm is columns. Books use two columns, magazines four, and newspapers a good deal more. The main rule: horizontally rhythmic blocks shouldn't repeat on neighboring or nearby rows — columns tend instead to grow from top to bottom.

![Columns as rhythm](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Group-46.png)

**Wholeness of composition.** There's a word for this — constructiveness. It isn't for nothing that artists talk about constructive drawing, about building the form, as opposed to drawing through light and shadow (the Stroganov school's approach versus the Glazunov school's). Wholeness is when everything in a composition is fused into a single, indivisible whole, as in a constructive drawing. You can check your own composition's wholeness against a few criteria: no part of the drawing can be removed without damage; the parts of the composition can't be swapped around; new elements only make it worse. Consider the dancers in a theatrical production's crowd scenes — they're dressed identically, which strengthens the sense that their movements belong together. The lead character in such productions is usually dressed in a costume of a different color.

**Lines.** One of the heaviest-weighing factors in working through a composition. The main object is usually placed where all the lines intersect. Vertical lines (towers, columns, trees) create an atmosphere of grandeur, solemnity, and calm. Where vertical lines dominate, you need to take steps to restore balance — adding horizontal lines.

Diagonal lines carry more energy and motion. The falling shadow of a leaf or a cat, for instance, will look livelier than the shadow of an angular house. And no discussion of lines is complete without the "line of beauty," shaped like the outline of a woman's back (the S-curve) — though really, any curved line gives a smooth transition of direction.

![The S-curve line of beauty](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2012/10/46565656-1.jpg)

**Space.** Space can't be depicted on its own — it depends directly on the objects in the illustration. Objects sitting on the ground, for instance, usually need more space above them, while a hanging object needs more space below. A person's head needs more space in front of the face, and it's best not to place the horizon in the middle of the working area. Space matters enormously in composition; without it, there's no composition at all. The scale of a space in a picture is a kind of deception — an artist always lies a little. A landscape drawn simply along the horizontal will look visually larger than the same landscape flanked by trees, houses, or other obstacles at the sides. And strictly horizontal lines lend a sense of reliability and calm to a space.

![Composition and spatial arrangement](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2012/10/comp_composition_your-scorpion-2.jpg)

**Angle of view.** A measure of depth in which reverse perspective, isometry, and frontal and direct perspective can all successfully coexist. Angle can add dynamism to an illustration in much the same way color patches can add chromatic expression. Diagonals and other slanted lines bring motion and add expressiveness to a form. There are certain recommendations, for architecture especially: keep the viewing angle within 30–40 degrees in both the horizontal and vertical planes. Angle lets you deepen the sense of space, and auxiliary elements often serve exactly that purpose.

**The rule of thirds.** A perfectly symmetrical composition suits an epic or a solemn moment, but the device shouldn't be overused, or the sense of naturalness disappears. A composition usually turns out better when it's built off-center, since that steers you away from the two chief problems of composition: centering and crowding the edges of the frame.

**The golden ratio.** Very briefly, and at its most basic: a ratio of 2 to 1, or 62% and 38%. There's a simple formula, a/b = (a+b)/a. The main objects should sit at the point of the golden ratio. Take a line where the central point C is the golden ratio found by that formula; lay the "formula ruler" against the horizontal and vertical sides of the canvas, and where the two points C intersect is your golden ratio. Simple, not entirely rigorous, but it works nonetheless — especially in photography. And for websites, a panel should carry some harmonious ratio too: 1200 − 742 = 458.

![The golden ratio applied to layout](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2012/10/Absolutely-Global-Independence-Relative-Layout.png)

Plenty of technical people take a photo of their grandmother, apply the golden ratio to it, and — unsatisfied with the result — start claiming the golden ratio is nonsense and doesn't really exist. What they're forgetting is that finding the exact mathematical proportions is no guarantee, on its own, of a beautiful result. Science doesn't compensate for a lack of talent; it only describes it.

But none of that is really what matters. What matters is the new thing the illustrator creates — everything outstanding is new, and everything new is an important quality of composition. There's also the law of subordination worth keeping in mind here: the simultaneous accounting of color, light, rhythm, volume, proportion, dynamics and stasis, and the many other nuances that go into a quality illustration — using every means of composition at once to produce a finished piece of work. I think that's a fair place to wrap up this introductory piece. And, yes — never forget the proportions of your canvas's sides, and always keep the light source in mind.

**Verticals, horizontals, and curved lines.** Combining vertical with horizontal makes a very strong impression. Long horizontal stretches of sea suggest calm, while tall vertical forms suggest grandeur. Lines can be diagonal too. Rounded lines always seem gentler and calmer; diagonals are always more dynamic and aggressive.

**Direction.** A diagonal running from lower-left to upper-right feels very swift, and that quality is used to convey light, free movement — heavily used in design. In any mail client the icon for sending a message is a right-pointing arrow (send), while the back step in browsers is a left-pointing arrow. The hands of a clock move the same way.

![Directional lines in design](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2012/10/comp_line1.jpg)

**Unity of mass.** Simple forms have been the core visual tool since the Bauhaus. A square always looks shorter than it is, which is why squares are conventionally stretched taller to compensate optically. It's the reverse with a circle — a perfectly round circle looks stretched wider than it is. A circle in the upper part of a canvas will always suggest something light and free, while a circle in the lower part will feel like something heavy. In just the same way, a horizontal line looks thicker than a vertical one — the eye always magnifies anything horizontal and registers vertical elements more weakly. Against a black background these properties read even more strongly. All of this feeds into building a sense of proportion in a composition: the more aggressive a shape (a square, a triangle), the more open space it needs around it to keep the composition balanced.

There's a very simple way to build a good composition: use the International Typographic Style, also known as the Swiss style — everything neatly in its place, well and correctly aligned, a perfectionist's paradise, wall-to-wall Helvetica. That typeface is about as neutral as it gets, which is exactly why it suits fast, functional execution so well. The foundation of the Swiss style rests on the rule of thirds and fifths: simply divide the sheet into three columns and three rows, and you get a harmonious grid. You can divide the sheet into five in the same way, and you can alternate — three by five, or five by three.

![The Swiss/International Typographic grid](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/◎-STROKED.png)

### Checklist for reviewing a composition

A finished composition is worth checking against a short list of questions turned into working habits. Start with the guiding lines — the main axis of the piece needs to be genuinely supported by the elements around it, not merely implied. Rhythm comes next: dark and light patches should alternate, and the heads in a crowd scene should be placed correctly rather than left to chance. Focus matters just as much — the piece needs a clear main subject, with the characters' gazes and the direction of the lighting pointed correctly toward it, and the tonal values accenting attention where it belongs. Variety deserves its own check too: folds in clothing shouldn't repeat too uniformly, and trees in the background shouldn't all look alike — variety of detail is always a plus, in design as much as in illustration. Economy is the opposite discipline: if a figure is wearing too many ornaments, the clothing and the background behind her should be pared back to something simple; where the lit areas are overloaded with detail, strip information out of the shadows to compensate. Repetition is worth checking on its own terms too — whether certain details find an echo elsewhere in the piece. And finally, balance, and the contrast of light against shadow, brought together under one heading: unity.

Someone reasonably asked, in the comments on an earlier version of this piece, why any of this should matter to a designer rather than an illustrator or a fine artist — surely this kind of knowledge belongs to painters. By that same logic, though, a designer wouldn't need to lay out a page, since there's a layout specialist for that; wouldn't need to analyze data, since there's an analyst; wouldn't need to think through usability, since there's a UX researcher; wouldn't need to write clearly, since there's a copywriter; wouldn't need to check their own mockups for mistakes, since there's an art director and a client for that; wouldn't need to understand type, since there's a type specialist; wouldn't need to hand off files to development, since there's a technical designer; wouldn't need to draw, since there's an illustrator; wouldn't need to touch up photos, since there's a photographer, a retoucher, and stock libraries; wouldn't need to come up with new ideas, since there's a creative director; wouldn't need to touch 3D, since there's a whole industry of specialists for that; and wouldn't need to talk to the client, since there's a manager. All that would be left for the designer to do is drop a finished picture and some text into the spot the art director already marked on the layout — and a specialist reduced to that isn't worth very much.

## Grids on the web

A website's composition has its roots in the modular grid. Modular grids aren't only about print and websites — whole cities are laid out on modular grids. Anapa, for instance, was rebuilt from scratch on a strict orthogonal grid.

Every advance in applying the computer to typography, editing, and layout was originally driven by the needs of print publishing, aimed at saving money. In magazines and newspapers, designers are constrained by the publication's format; on top of that, the thickness and weight of the eventual book matter too, along with the particulars of distribution and the economics involved.

Before building a grid, you first need to find an interesting composition. Start with a simple pencil sketch — and it should be a sketch of the composition, not of the grid itself. The composition has to guide the viewer's attention well, through the density of information, the pace at which it's absorbed, and so on.

Grids come in different typologies. There's no single agreed classification, but you can distinguish a few distinct characters of grid: collage grids, column grids, modular grids, combined grids, and every possible mix of the four.

**Collage grids** have no gutters and no empty rows — there's no end of variations on this kind of grid. The horizontal divider here is simply the type's baseline. A vivid example is a grid with a zero gutter, or rather one where certain columns function as the gutter themselves — a kind of postmodernism. A grid like that calls for very contrasting typefaces, grotesque paired with old-style serif, and text set in counterphase. In the examples below you can see how empty columns can play the role of a gutter — it opens up a great deal of air and lets you play very flexibly with micro-modules.

![Collage grid example one](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Screenshot_13.png)

![Collage grid example two](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Screenshot_13-1.png)

Grids like this show off a site's modularity beautifully, which works well when there isn't a great deal of content. You can use a progressive system too, making each new column larger or smaller than the last by some algorithm — as with the golden ratio.

Next come **column grids**. These have several vertical dividers (gutters), with columns either equal or varied in width. Gutters are simply the empty areas between columns, and it's exactly these that set how much "air" a page has. Make the gutters too wide — as wide as a column itself — and the grid effectively becomes a collage grid.

Column grids often lean on force lines, which give a layout dynamism; a content block rests against a force line, and this is one way of steering the viewer's attention. In the example below we end up with four large columns and blue points of attention that concentrate focus on the content.

![Column grid with force lines](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Screenshot_13-2.png)

This approach lets you flexibly vary how much detail the content carries — showing more on the first screen, say, and merging several columns into one as the user scrolls down. Larger columns are built up out of smaller ones.

The third kind is the **modular grid**, which necessarily has both a vertical and a horizontal divider. The simplest example is any news site or online shop, where every card is a module. Thanks to the horizontal dividers, you get far more flexibility to play with dynamics and rhythm.

![Modular grid example](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Frame-13.png)

All the grids described so far share the same character in their dynamics — they're even, symmetrical constructions. But you can build progressive grids with asymmetry too, giving yourself room to play with the proportions of the modules and with rhythm, highlighting the most important modules. The simplest way to build such a grid is combination: merging columns into larger blocks, which lets you place content flexibly without being aggressive about it.

The next example is overlay. A progressive grid is built as a combination of several simple, even grids. The most vivid example of such a grid is Karl Gerstner's, built as a set of variations on a 2-3-4-5-6-column grid, developed for the Swiss magazine *Capital*. Grids of this kind are needed when a single grid type can't handle a product's demands — such a grid calls for careful study, and the designer has to put in the work to understand the original grid-maker's intent before starting to design the site itself. One grid for ads and articles, say, and a second for instructional material.

![Karl Gerstner-style overlay grid](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/8787.png)

There are also grids built on geometric or mathematical proportions — the most common being the Fibonacci sequence, the golden ratio, and Le Corbusier's Modulor.

And there's the grid built from the composition of an illustration or photograph: we take the dynamics of the lines and the proportions from the illustration placed in the design and repeat them in the grid. Every guide line echoes a line from the illustration's composition, extending past its edges to form guides for aligning the other elements. A sense of untidiness and chance is quite appropriate here, since sites like this are often built as works of art in their own right.

![Grid derived from an illustration's composition](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Frame-14.png)

And finally, algorithmic grids, whose core idea is that some algorithm — logical, mathematical, data-science-driven — sits underneath. The result is unpredictable, and what we output is itself an expression of that algorithm.

Inside a grid sit what are called modules. Practically every popular screen resolution is a multiple of 4px or 8px, and divides evenly by 5px or 10px — the exception being 1366px. So the smallest module you can take as a base is, say, a 5px-by-5px square. One caution worth flagging right away: the height of the type itself shouldn't be tied to that minimum module — only the baseline should be. All the leading in the text has to be a multiple of the micro-module, so that the baseline lands exactly on the small module's boundary.

![Baseline aligned to the micro-module](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Slice-1.png)

Columns, gutters, margins, photos, illustrations, the leading of the whole text block, and icon boxes are all aligned to the modules. But icon thickness and size, type sizes, divider thickness, and decorative elements don't have to follow this rule.

A grid with a module system has to adapt across different screen resolutions:

![Adapting a modular grid across resolutions](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Frame-14-1.png)

Every block can be stretched differently depending on the task:

- You can stretch just one specific block selectively — in our case, the image.
- Or stretch only the empty areas, leaving the content untouched.
- Or simply stretch all the content proportionally.
- Scale the content proportionally, in whole or in part.
- Change the card's form factor entirely.

![Different stretching behaviors for a card](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/photo_2018-07-01_14-27-36.jpg)

Inside a block, you first work out the behavior of the stack — the stretched and compressed margins, the padding from the card's edges, the gaps between rows — and only then, using the grid, do you bring all those values into line.

![Working out a card's internal spacing](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2017/08/shot_170803_102401.png)

But if we're working at production speed and need a standard grid that developers will recognize immediately, we simply merge the concepts of Grid (the module) and Layout (the columns) to match the development frameworks in use. Layout gets used as the modular grid, and an 8pt grid handles convenience and alignment, with type and icons aligning to a 4dp grid. Standard resolutions of 960, 1080, 1140, 1440, and 1560, with 60px or 120px columns, give predictably good results. You can depart from the standards, too — I used to often build 1200px with 24 columns, a 30px cell, and a 20px gutter instead of 960 with 16 or 12 columns, based on service-usage statistics.

Take Sketch as an example. Your input parameters should all be multiples of 8pt:

1. Artboard resolution
2. Total Layout Width
3. Gutter / column width

So, to work on an 8pt step, the grid has to be a multiple of 8: 320 (mobile), 768 (tablet portrait), 1024 (tablet landscape), 1360 (desktop), 1920 (desktop HD). Width (column width) equals 8, and Gutter (the space between columns) is a multiple of 16. For the calculations, a service like [gridcalculator.dk](http://gridcalculator.dk/) is a better bet than doing it by hand.

![Grid calculator output](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/Снимок-экрана-2017-04-24-в-9.32.14.png)

If you're working with Bootstrap or Foundation, the grid is already chosen and configured for you: the standard 12-column grid with equal-width columns, a safe default. Bootstrap's grid sets up that 12-column scheme, which on screens 1200px wide and up gives 30px between columns, with 15px of side padding on the content, left and right. All told: 1170px of content plus 15px of padding on each side. Equal-width columns help you manage large, complex sites and save developers a lot of grief on fast-moving production work. The number of columns determines how the layout changes with column width and gutter spacing — worth understanding well when adapting a grid across different platforms.

![Bootstrap-style equal-width column grid](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/ssfs.png)

That all sounds abstract, so let me give an example from actual practice: a desktop portal at 1920px. That resolution needs to divide by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, and 12, so the column and gutter sizes are easy to work out. Say the gutters are set at 40px — then 1920 / 12 − 40 = 120, the column width. That's building the grid from the larger number down to the smaller.

You can go the other direction too, mobile-first: say we decide the gutters between columns will be a universal 20px (which conveniently covers both the 5px step inherited from desktop, since it divides by 5 and 10, and the 4px step for mobile), while the columns themselves need to be three times as wide under certain conditions. That gives a grid of (20px × 12) + (60px × 12) = 960px. With this approach you can work out column size by multiplying by 2, 3, or 4. It's strongly advisable to fix the gutter size between columns — both Bootstrap and Foundation do this out of the box. Column width is set as a percentage, while gutters are fixed in pixels or rem; this matters so that content doesn't merge together at some point as the gutters shrink.

If you'd rather not experiment with grids yourself, take one of these values and you won't go wrong: 12 columns at 60px/40px, 12 columns at 48px/48px, 12 columns at 60px/40px, 16 columns at 45px/30px, or 9+ columns at 80px/30px.

8px is used everywhere because it's the only natural number whose remainders, summed across division by every natural number smaller than it, equal the number itself: 0 + 0 + 2 + 0 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 8. On top of that, 8 is the number of vertices on a cube and the number of faces on an octahedron, which suits a grid meant for VR nicely. It works like a children's construction set: if every block is the same size, that's a poor set; but if every block shares a grid, the way Lego does, that's an excellent one.

There's also a good technique for vertical rhythm: set the Gutter Height to half the line-height value. If the line-height is 24, the GH should be 12 — and the text will then fit neatly into the vertical rhythm. Technically, in CSS, line-height can be set in pixels, percentages, or a unitless ratio (24px, 150%, 1.5).

![Vertical rhythm via gutter height and line-height](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Group-2.png)

Every grid works with the Box Model — border, margin, padding, and the dimensions of the elements themselves. Border is the thickness of the stroke around an element's edges; in design tools this parameter usually doesn't affect the element's overall dimensions, the way a shadow doesn't. Padding is the space between an element's edges and its child elements. Margin is the space between an element's edges and the objects next to it.

*One practical footnote, since it comes up constantly when checking a composition: mirroring an illustration is one of the most useful tricks for catching what your eye has stopped noticing, since a flipped image reveals imbalances a tired eye glosses over in the original. Photoshop doesn't assign a hotkey to this by default, so in professional studios it's common practice to bind horizontal canvas flipping to F2 yourself, in the keyboard-shortcut settings, and keep it within easy reach for exactly this kind of check.*
