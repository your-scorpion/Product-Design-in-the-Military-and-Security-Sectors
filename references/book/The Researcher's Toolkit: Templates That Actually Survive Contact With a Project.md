# The Researcher's Toolkit: Templates That Actually Survive Contact With a Project

*By Maxim Tsvetkov*

Every study, no matter how ambitious, runs on the same four rails: define the goals and tasks, prepare and recruit, run the research, then analyse and synthesise. And like any honest article on the subject, this one has to begin with the tedious part — understanding the problem. Skip it and you'll reach for the wrong method, the results will quietly rot in a drawer, and the blind spot between what you *wanted* to learn and what you *set out* to learn will steer the whole product into the wrong position on the market.

A research goal is an outcome — the thing you walk away knowing — even when the work is syndicated. *Understand why the target audience won't touch the portal, or won't upgrade to the paid plan.* Why does the top slice of TikTok's audience never show up on Netflix? Why does nobody buy anything from the Chilled Food Counter? Underneath every goal sit the tasks: the concrete steps a designer takes to get there.

- Understand why people use Netflix, Apple TV+, Disney+, HBO Max, Peacock, Amazon Prime Video.
- Work out what attracts each audience to these services — and what repels them.
- Surface the friction points people hit on Netflix specifically.

It helps to think about goals on a cadence, because a research goal rarely lives alone; it's usually a rung on a longer ladder. A week's goal is to ship a feature or analyse something small. A month's goal is to move a metric. A quarter's is to grow revenue. A year's goal is whatever you can point to and say *this is what I got, this is what I reached* — experience, a promotion, income, a network, influence. Write the tasks up in SMART form, spell out *why* each one earns its place, and make a habit of crediting the wins along the way.

Before any of that, though, there's a short interrogation you owe yourself. You need to know which stage the project is really at and where you can do some good rather than just some work. So put these to yourself and to your colleagues:

- What is the product's business goal?
- Who is the target audience?
- What are people actually trying to get done inside the product?
- Is this their first attempt at that task?
- Where is the product useful — and where is it not?
- Why would this audience choose *our* product over the alternatives?
- Where will they use it, and in what context?

Some of those answers only exist in a stakeholder's head, and stakeholders have a gift for handing you something strange and abstract in reply. Your job is to translate the abstraction back into goals and tasks — which means it's worth being deliberate about who your stakeholders even are.

## Mapping the people who can make or break the work

A stakeholder is anyone with a stake in the project and the influence to bend it — up to and including the beneficiary who ultimately profits. The cleanest first cut is external versus internal, and for the internal crowd it pays to draw a **RACI matrix**, which doubles as a way to read how loaded each person on the team is:

- **R — Responsible.** Does the work, under direction.
- **A — Accountable.** Owns the outcome — usually the project manager, and the one holding the SLA.
- **C — Consulted before doing.** Supplies the expertise and the knowledge.
- **I — Informed.** Told once a task is done.

From there, an **Eisenhower matrix** helps you plan the actual tasks. The sweet spot, counterintuitively, isn't the urgent-and-important quadrant everyone panics into — it's the important-but-not-yet-urgent one, where good work gets done calmly.

![Eisenhower matrix for task prioritisation](http://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/Frame-41.png)

A third lens, the **stakeholder onion diagram**, lets you arrange people by how much sway they hold over the product: the closer to the centre, the greater the influence. And because managing people is never purely a matter of charting them, situational leadership theory is worth keeping in your back pocket — the familiar quadrant of management styles mapped against the maturity of the person doing the work. You lead a green hire differently from a seasoned one, and pretending otherwise is how projects stall.

![Situational leadership quadrant](http://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Group-184.jpg)

## Choosing a method

With the map drawn, you pick a method — and it will almost always be an interview or a usability test. If you want to learn what stands between someone and a visit to the Botanical Garden, that's a problem interview. If the product already exists and the question is whether a particular feature is comfortable to use, that's usability testing.

Once the method is settled, the substance of the work is simple to state and hard to do: ask a great many questions and get real answers to them. *Is there demand for this feature — and how do we actually know? Does our product roadmap answer what users and the business both need?* Put the following to yourself and to your stakeholders until the answers stop being guesses:

- What problem does your idea solve?
- How do people cope with that problem today?
- What other products already address it?
- How did the previous solution fail?
- Do users understand how the product works?
- What do people say — and think — about the service?
- Who are the competitors?
- What is the app or site *for*; which task does it close out?
- Is there genuine demand for our solution among the audience?
- On which devices do people use the product?
- Which scenarios does our product actually cover?

Plan your metrics from the very start, in two camps. **Strong (behavioural) metrics** capture what people *do*. **Weak (perceived) metrics** capture what people *say* — NPS (likelihood to recommend), SUS (usability), CES (ease of use), CSAT (satisfaction).

**CES (Customer Effort Score)** is a single question about how hard it was to get something done, answered on a five-point scale. Push it further and you're into True Intent Studies.

**CSAT (Satisfaction Score)** is, again, one question — *how happy were you with your experience* — and a scale to answer it on. It usually speaks to the interface, and it's the classic tablet-by-the-exit question you meet in shopping centres. There's a cheap, robust way to gather exactly that kind of feedback at scale: take a JTK controller and map any actions you like onto a keyboard, and you've built your own smiley-button terminal. Just keep the mentality trap in mind — a CSAT of 9 in England is not the same animal as a CSAT of 6 in Russia. For a task like *compare branches across countries*, you need threshold values per segment, or you track the trend over time rather than the raw number.

And resist the urge to inflate a metric purely to produce a handsome figure in a report. I prefer to watch the broad satisfaction parameters — improvement, efficiency, intuitiveness (usability), engagement, trust — and let each specific case decide which of them is worth measuring to move the ones that matter.

It's worth being honest about a trap here, because sooner or later someone asks how the metrics can look excellent while the experience, in practice, is not. The answer is *bounded rationality*: people make economic decisions through a narrow aperture, without weighing every relevant factor. They decide behind the wheel, walking the dog, half-listening in a meeting — attention scattered — and they'll write off perfectly reasonable factors as absurd, like paying for water in a café or a fee to move money inside their own bank. So the scenarios can run start to finish without a hitch and the UX can still be poor. A bank may offer a dozen well-designed cards; all you have to do is read every set of terms and pick the optimal one. People hate that. They want *one* card and the ability to tune it to the situation afterwards.

## Preparing for a usability test

Usability tests vary by how you talk to the respondent, by the kind of project, and by what you're after. Games, for instance, are tested by observation — you watch the play session unfold and interview afterwards. The skeleton is always the same: an opening interview, the tasks, a closing part. Sorted by purpose, four types earn their keep:

1. **Formative** — you're hunting for what's *awkward* in the product. Qualitative.
2. **Summative** — you're establishing *how* (in)convenient it is. Quantitative, with a questionnaire, and from the sample you infer how widespread a problem is across the whole population.
3. **Hybrid** — formative problem-hunting combined with the capture of success, productivity and satisfaction metrics.
4. **Comparative** — weighing the pros and cons of several competing products against each other.

The conversation itself can be run in person, remote-moderated, or remote-unmoderated. Sorted **by type of project**, tests split differently:

1. **Classic** — you test a finished product before release. The respondent gets tasks, you observe and ask questions. The build is properly put together and has been through QA, with no glaring bugs. If the interface is complex, you show the recording of the user's actions afterwards and ask them to narrate it — *retrospective think-aloud*.
2. **Playtests** — in games there are no set tasks; you simply watch, ask, and hand over questionnaires.
3. **First Click Test (FCT)** — you give the respondent a task to find a particular piece of functionality, having set the context first. Ideal when you have just one mockup.
4. **Rapid Iterative Testing and Evaluation (RITE)** — you test a prototype in iterations. In a single day you run 2–3 respondents, change the prototype on the strength of the results, and the next day bring 2–3 fresh respondents and fresh changes — repeating until you converge on the ideal, usually across five or six iterations.

When you need a screen recording, get on a call in whatever service lets you share a screen — Zoom, Whereby, Google Meet, Discord — and read the room: with gamers, Discord is the natural home. You can try Fabuza, which has remote-moderated testing built in, but it's usually simpler to reach for the tools everyone already knows. Set the broadcast up with OBS:

## Interrogating the manager

Preparation isn't finished. When an outside manager turns up wanting to commission a usability study, your job is to bury them in questions. In the ideal world a product designer is simultaneously the client side and the delivery side; but if you're operating as an external UX researcher, you dig:

1. What's expected from the research?
2. Have you worked with a product designer, UX researcher or UX analyst before? Do you have a clear picture of the deliverable?
3. What information is missing — and how might we get it?
4. What problems already exist, and why are they problems?
5. Is there analytics or statistics set up properly, and can I have access?
6. Why have you come to this so late?
7. Where does our users' attention actually live — where will they find us and form the habit of using the product?
8. Are you the decision-maker?
9. What prompted you to schedule the research now?
10. Do people grasp the value of the product? How does that show up?
11. Are there hypotheses already written down?

The same interrogation is worth running from the other direction too — as a short intake questionnaire you put to a business *before* they've ordered a scrap of design. Something in this spirit: What problem are we solving? For whom? How does the user solve it today? What do they want to get or do? What do *we* want from the user? Which metric will measure engagement? How do we motivate people to tell their friends about the service? Against which goals — and on what timeline — do we call the product a success? How does it differ from competitors, how is it wired to the company's other products, and how will it change the company itself? Ask those eleven early and half the fog lifts before design begins.

**About the project.**

1. What are the expectations for launch?
2. Which product metrics will be tracked?
3. How will the project be monetised?
4. What user behaviour is expected — and useful for the business goals?
5. What development stage is the project at?
6. If this is a redesign, what's changing? Are there new features?
7. On which devices will we test the prototype, and where do we get them?

**Target audience / respondents.**

1. Who is the audience? Do we even know?
2. Into which groups can it be split?
3. Are there results from earlier research?
4. Who are the competitors?
5. Are we testing on people who already have experience with comparable services?

**Constraints.**

1. What limits are in play — app version, platform, browsers?
2. Which scenarios in the prototype don't work?
3. Can users with different roles see the functionality? (Relevant for live B2B products.)

**Logistics.**

1. Who pays to source respondents?
2. Is there an understanding of how agency recruiting works?
3. Can our own staff be respondents?
4. When are you ready to start?
5. Who else might have a stake in the research?

If you prefer a mnemonic, run the Five Ws — Who, What, When, Where, Why.

**Who** — questions that get at the audience. *Who influences whether people find our product on the market? Who comes to mind when we mention Product X? Who did you talk to while building Product X?*

**What** — the internal motivation. *What irritates or grinds you down most in a working day with Product X? What can't you do in it that you wish you could? What do you find yourself doing that you'd rather not? What's the single best thing about it? What's the most important part of your day working with it?*

**When** — the external motivation. *When did you first notice the problem? When did you first realise what solution you needed? When did you feel stuck, not knowing what to do next? When did the product irritate you? When did you start using it? When did you decide it was the right fit for the job?*

**Where** — the context of the surroundings. *Where do you look for products that meet needs like this? Where do you get updates for Product X? Which platforms' reviews do you actually trust?*

**Why** — the implicit drivers. *Why do you think Product X beats Product Y? Why? Why? Why? Why? Why? And — why not?*

One more thing worth naming before we recruit, because the manager question *"why so late?"* has a shadow side you'll eventually meet from the other chair. Sometimes a large client dangles a big project for a year, stalling and trying to load impossible terms onto you. The practical read: the moment the project becomes both urgent and important to *them*, they'll come running to work on your terms and hand over a proper budget. After each block of work, insist on a letter of guarantee — and keep a good IT lawyer who knows that, when it comes to it, the civil code outranks the contract.

Then you launch the recruit.

## Questions for the prototype

In time you'll have a prototype or product that needs testing. These are the general questions to put to internal users when you're evaluating prototypes at the alpha and beta stages:

- Is it clear to the user what to do on the current screen?
- Do users understand how the flow works, and which actions each step demands?
- Does the user know what they're meant to do?
- Where are we running the study — lab, office, field, with a broadcast or without?
- Is it clear which actions lead to the goal?
- Can the user tell whether their actions were correct and successful?

## Preparing the script / guide

Open with a greeting, a word about yourself, and the aims of the interview or test. The point is to build trust with the respondent and give them a general sense of how the study will go.

- Thank them for taking part; greet them.
- Say who you are.
- Describe the goal in broad strokes.
- Warn them about the duration and about the audio/video recording.
- Handle the NDA and consent to process personal data.
- Walk them through how the interview will run.

Then the warm-up questions: what the respondent does, their hobbies, how they spend their downtime — plus the screening questions that confirm they have the experience you care about and filter out the tourists.

- *What do you do for work?*
- *Tell me a bit more about your hobbies.*
- *How often do you use your home computer?*
- *How often do you buy things online?*

If your aim is to understand the *person* — their priorities and goals rather than their button-clicks — a few oblique questions open people up better than the direct kind: *If I asked your closest friend or a family member to describe you, what would they say? What's the most useful feedback you've ever had from a colleague? If a software team were a football side, which role should be yours?*

Now the questions on the actual topic. You are not here to sell the product, and you keep your own behaviour on a leash — never hinting at whether an answer or action was "right." Move from the general to the particular: *How have your buying habits changed over the past year? — Why did you start using grocery delivery? — How often do you use it? — How do you track the courier? — What went wrong on your last delivery?* And here's a useful battery with a fintech slant:

- What does your company do?
- Which services do you use right now?
- How long has the company been on the market?
- What's your role in it?
- What does your company have in place for…?
- Which tasks interest you, and why?
- How do you usually deal with your bank?
- Which banking products interest you?
- Who on your side interacts with the bank?
- In what situation do you approach the bank?
- How did you choose the service?
- Where did you hear about it?
- Whose opinion did you weigh when choosing?
- How long did choosing the product take?
- How often do you revisit the terms or the make-up of the service?
- How long have you used it?
- How do you prefer to hear about changes?
- What were your expectations?

Everything you gather here, incidentally, tends to crystallise into a user story, and the template barely changes across languages: *as a [role], I want to [do something] so that [outcome]* — three answers to *who, what, why*. For example: *as a pensioner with poor eyesight, I want to check every paper and read all the text on documents at the Post Office branch, so I use a special magnifier built into my glasses.* Keep those three anchors and the story writes itself.

Once every task is done, draw out the respondent's impressions and close cleanly: summarise the session, paraphrase the ideas back, ask whether there are questions you *should* have asked, ask who else you ought to speak to — and hand over the reward.

## Checking the prototype

Start by learning everything there is to know about the product. If the goal is merely *"let's see what's going on with the UX,"* that is not a goal:

- What product is this?
- What are the expectations from the research?
- Which problems already sit on the surface?
- Which metrics are affected?
- Is there statistics?
- Can we get away with cheap methods — session replay, a round of calls?

There's a whole toolkit for automating those calls — Dialplan, UniMRCP, AMI, Asterisk paired with Tinkoff VoiceKit — and you can even synthesise the speech.

Usability testing is, at bottom, a check on how comfortable the interface is to work with. If you like your definitions formal, ISO 9241-210:2010 should please you: *a testing method aimed at establishing the degree of usability, learnability, comprehensibility and appeal of a product under development to its users, within the context of specified conditions.*

Through the test you gauge the product's ergonomics against competitors and surface the patterns of use, the impressions, and the problems.

Everyone always wants to hear about the *problems*, and to expose one properly you must answer three questions: What did the user want — what were they trying to do? Why did it turn out the way it did? And what happens if we leave it exactly as it is?

After a prototype test, these are the further questions whose answers you should be holding:

- Can you get your everyday tasks done in the prototype?
- Does the design fit the product's purpose?
- What's the first thing you want to do in it?
- What irritates you?
- What pulls you away from your goal?
- Which features got completely ignored?
- Does the information architecture match users' mental model?
- Do people feel the product was made *for them*?
- What might make them use it regularly?
- In their own words, how do users describe working in the prototype?

## Wireframe

If you're showing wireframes rather than a working MVP, I'd aim to answer a slightly different set:

- Before touching the prototype, what functional expectations do people hold?
- What look do they expect to see?
- On first glance, is it clear what the service is about and what you can do in it?
- Does the first impression match what came before?
- What functionality is missing?
- Which elements sit in illogical places?
- How does using the prototype *feel*?
- If they had a magic wand, what would they change?

And the extra feedback:

- What are the three hardest moments in Product X's interface?
- What did you want to do and couldn't?
- What functionality is missing?
- Which product would you like Product X to resemble more?
- What's the single most irritating thing about it?
- If you met an expert on Product X, what three questions would you ask them?
- What eats the most time in Product X?
- Which products do you use alongside it?

That's a lot of questions — which means a lot of answers, though not all of them need to be spoken aloud. You can work with a respondent in four registers:

1. **Observation** — give the task, ask nothing, watch.
2. **Think-aloud** — give the task and ask them to narrate every action.
3. **Active intervention** — give the task and ask questions as they go.
4. **Retrospective think-aloud** — give the task, record the session, and afterwards ask them to comment on their actions from the video.

## SUS

After the test you drop a questionnaire on the respondent — the SUS is the classic. You're rating how usable the system is: ten statements, each scored 1 to 5 for agreement.

1. I think I'd like to use this system frequently.
2. I find the system simple.
3. I think it's easy to use.
4. I think I could use it without a technical specialist's help.
5. I find the various functions well integrated.
6. I think the system is very logical and coherent.
7. I can imagine most people learning to use it quickly and easily.
8. I find the system intuitive.
9. I feel confident using it.
10. I could use it without having to learn anything new first.

Scoring the final number is non-trivial:

- For **odd-numbered** questions: subtract one from the answer — a 4 becomes 4 − 1 = 3.
- For **even-numbered** questions: subtract the answer from five — a 3 becomes 5 − 3 = 2. Each question now sits between 0 and 4.
- Sum all ten values.
- Multiply the sum by 2.5, landing you between 0 and 100.

That figure is the average usability score. Against expectation, a SUS of 50 is not a decent middle — 50 is practically bad. The gradation runs:

- **92** — exceeds expectations.
- **85 — excellent** — somewhere around here people start recommending you to friends.
- **72** — good — the standard for commercial services.
- **68 — the average**, the equivalent of 50%.
- **52** — passable.
- **38 — bad** — somewhere around here you should start sounding the alarm.
- **25** — exceeds your worst expectations.

## Kano

The Kano model measures the *emotional* reaction people have to features, which makes it a questionnaire for prioritising a backlog. It's about how people *feel* toward the potential presence or absence of a function. I lean on it when deciding which functions to carry over from an old version of a product into a new one. Kano maps the range between a user's satisfaction and their disappointment — between a good implementation and a bad one. One caveat: I use it only with a motivated English-speaking audience, because a Japanese → English → Russian chain of translation doesn't inspire confidence and the phrasings themselves are genuinely tricky.

![The Kano model](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/Group-7.png)

The model comes from the Japanese professor Noriaki Kano. Each feature gets two questions — one about how people feel if the feature *is* in the product, one about how they feel if it *isn't*. The whole thing rests on asking what the user feels in the presence and the absence of a function.

Two questions per feature, and the wording matters enormously:

1. How do you feel about **[feature]** being in the service?
2. How do you feel about **[feature]** being absent from the service?

Answer options for **presence**: *1.* I like that it's there; *2.* I expect it to be there; *3.* I'm neutral; *4.* I can tolerate it being there; *5.* I dislike that it's there.

Answer options for **absence**: *1.* I like that it isn't there; *2.* I expect it not to be there; *3.* I'm neutral; *4.* I can tolerate it not being there; *5.* I dislike that it isn't there.

You can safely reword the answers to fit the question — for instance, *if our company introduced this service, how would you rate that?*

- I'd definitely like that you offer such a service.
- I think a company like yours simply ought to have it.
- I'm neutral.
- I don't like the service, but if you rolled it out I could live with it.
- I don't need it, and I wouldn't like it if you introduced it.

Out of this fall five kinds of feature:

- **Attractive, WOW features** — the stuff of adverts, the things that raise a shiver of delight. Unexpected pleasures, wow-effects. Smart-animate in Figma: they shipped it and the community was ecstatic.
- **One-dimensional.** Your product's core characteristics. The faster the internet, the happier the user — though there's an acceptable floor (hygiene). Features like this are called one-dimensional.
- **Must-be characteristics — hygiene.** A messenger must be able to send messages; a bank must be able to pay a phone bill. If these are missing it's a catastrophe, and they have to be done well. They're the necessary set — the baseline expectation of what the product can do. There's no point building an online bank you can't check your balance in, a phone that can't make calls, or a car with no brakes.
- **Indifferent** — unimportant; features nobody particularly needs.
- **Undesirable** — the things better removed altogether.

![Kano feature categories](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Group-304.png)

And you come to understand the drift over time: if smart-animate was a coveted differentiator at launch, today it's a desired function, and in a few years it'll be hygiene — the kind of thing whose absence makes launching the product pointless.

To place a feature in its group, use the table below.

![Kano evaluation table](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/sf.gif)

The recurring trouble: it's hard to hold the line between "present" and "absent" at the level of how you *phrase* the question. Wrong semantics, two near-identical questions differing by a single word, and the results are safe to ignore. If every feature you test lands in the performance (desired) bucket, that's a tell that your study design has a problem. It pays to combine Kano with the classic battery of ratings.

Kano is a fairly quick study. For anything more serious, look toward **MaxDiff**. Say your task is to choose which 3 of 10 branded phone cases go into production for nationwide sale: you run MaxDiff, where respondents pick the most and least appealing cases, and then you bring in **TURF** to understand the size of the audience that a limited selection of cases would actually interest.

## NPS

A questionnaire about satisfaction with the product. It gauges willingness to recommend — the story of customer loyalty. Two questions:

1. How likely are you to recommend X to friends and acquaintances? On a scale where 0 means *no chance I'd recommend it* and 10 means *I'll absolutely recommend it.*
2. Name the main reason for your score.

By the results, respondents fall into three groups:

1. **Promoters** — 9–10.
2. **Passives / neutrals** — 7–8.
3. **Detractors** — 1–6.

The simple calculation is NPS = %promoters − %detractors. I usually feed the results into an [NPS calculator](http://www.npscalculator.com/en) and get an index — say NPS = −34%. Ship a feature, watch how it moves. If it climbs to −12%, there are fewer critics, but promoters are still in the minority. Bear in mind that across a whole market a negative NPS is often the *normal* NPS.

NPS is also a filter: sort out the detractors — everyone who answered 1 to 6 — and invite them to a problem interview, since this is usually the group leaving the negative reviews. Don't hang serious conclusions on it; it's a very simple instrument, useful mainly for reading the overall situation over time. And it certainly doesn't correlate directly with the Wallet Allocation Rule. A business cares far more about profit than about public affection, and the highest-paying customers can hand you the lowest NPS.

The audio of an interview is either transcribed in full or written up into a protocol. That protocol usually lives in Excel or Google Sheets: the columns hold the themes you discuss during the interview, the rows hold the respondents' names.

With those tables built, you run a thematic analysis — going through every protocol and drawing out the themes. *Six of eight respondents flagged courier speed,* for instance. To do the coding and categorisation at any scale, there's dedicated software: on the academic side, NVivo, Atlas.ti 6.0 (my pick), HyperRESEARCH 2.8, and MaxQDA. If you've only a handful of interviews and everything's kept lightweight, Excel does the job fine. From all of it, you build a report.

The first and most important question to put to the client about reports is: *what will you actually do with the results?* Present them to colleagues, prioritise a backlog with the product manager, enrich a CJM? The answer picks the format:

- **Google Sheets** is strong for reporting quantitative studies.
- **Online whiteboards** suit reports for a more sophisticated audience.
- **PowerPoint** — the 100-slide classic, minimal information per slide, built for pitching to a room.
- **A video report**, a little film about the results and the process — excellent for *selling* research into a company that has no such culture yet.

Report structure:

1. Goals and tasks of the study.
2. Methodology.
3. General conclusions and headline results.
4. Answers to the questions and hypotheses.
5. Quantitative analysis (metrics, questionnaires).
6. Detailed results.
7. A wish list.
8. Recommendations.

Recommendations aren't always wanted — clear that up in advance with the client. Sometimes all they need is a content analysis: describe the ideas, words and phrases common to every respondent, and let the team decide for themselves which terms belong in the interface. Or a thematic analysis — describing, for example, how using a social network about caring for animals changes a person's behaviour.

A problem on a slide follows a structure: 1) what's wrong in the interface; 2) why that's a problem; 3) how it surfaced during testing; 4) respondent quotes, if the problem is critical.

The convention is at least three slides per problem. The first covers **effectiveness**: *most respondents (7 of 10) didn't notice the new button* — plus how critical the problem is. The second covers **productivity**: the average time to complete the task. The third covers **satisfaction**: the value on a five-point scale, the SUS result. To rate all three at once, use Jeff Sauro's **SUM**. For a clean count, every respondent must get identical starting tasks with no interference from the researcher.

If you decide to build your own questionnaire, don't skip the pretest and the pilot. A **pilot** is a test on a small sample to check that the questions you invented — and their order — actually work. A **pretest** checks specific questions from the questionnaire rather than the whole thing.

Somewhere in here, most people also want to know whether it's possible to be *fast, cheap and excellent* all at once — and how to get there. "Fast, cheap, brilliant" is the folk reading of the triangle of time, money and scope, whose centre is quality. When a project hits trouble, the discipline is to work out which side it strikes: time, money, or scope. Pressure on one side of the triangle transmits to the others, which is why, at the outset, you have to know which side is fixed. The standard example: to hit the deadline you either add people (money) or cut features (scope) — or you move the launch date. Quality is the fourth element, sitting in the middle, and what "quality" *means* is defined company by company: landing on budget, release speed, the metrics.

## Analysing quantitatively

In quantitative work there's the notion of the **population** — every user you have. The **sample** is the slice the study actually covers, and it has to be *representative*: it must mirror the whole population.

Say we know our audience is 50% men and 50% women; a third orders food online, a third buys it at the corner shop, and the last third only eats out. Then a sample drawn from that population has to hold men and women 50/50, and each third of users has to fall into one of those three eating habits.

Quantitative methods demand a huge number of respondents. There are plenty of [calculators](https://socioline.ru/rv.php) where you enter the population size, the precision you want, and the acceptable margin of error in percent — the smaller the percentage, the more representative the result, but the larger the sample has to be. VTsIOM runs its polls on 1,600 people; large marketing studies take in 500. To find respondents you can use [fastuna](https://fastuna.ru/), or read [this article](https://your-scorpion.ru/product_design_approaches/).

When the aim is to capture *public opinion* — say, gauging the mood before a well-known figure's public appearance — reach for **probability-based panels**, which are far quicker than the budget non-probability online panels. Good examples are the [LISS Panel](https://www.lissdata.nl/) and the [GESIS Panel](https://www.gesis.org/gesis-panel/gesis-panel-home).

And when you've collected demographics alongside a set of preference data for each person and want to know how demography relates to *class membership*, the sequence is: first a **latent class analysis** to build the classes, then run it through **crosstabs** — or use a weighted regression.

Sometimes the data has to be pulled off the internet — from contractors, from open data sources, or over an API. An `http` request comes in parts: the address you're calling (the one in the browser bar), the technical information (cookies), and the request method.

The response carries a status: 200 for success, 404 if the address wasn't found, and so on — the full list of HTTP statuses lives [here](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Список_кодов_состояния_HTTP). Alongside it comes the body in the requested format — HTML, XML, JSON — or media files, plus more technical detail.

Over HTTP, requests are made with one of two methods: GET and POST. **GET** simply retrieves text or a media file at an address — we ask the server for a file, the server hands it over. **POST** is used when submitting forms and carries, beyond the address, extra data such as form fields or images.

Python has the `requests` library, which sends a request to a server with almost no ceremony. (And you can generate JSON straight from Figma sources with my service, [your-scorpion.github.io](http://your-scorpion.github.io/).)

```python
import requests
response = requests.get('https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/3.4.2020-403.json')
```

Check the response with `print(response.status_code)` and you'll get 200 — the code for a correct reply. The server answered and returned what we expected.

Right now the text sits in a plain string variable. You can turn that string into a dictionary using Python's *JSON* parser, or the `json` method already built into the `response` object.

More interesting, though, is parsing a page:

```python
import requests

url = 'https://your-scorpion.github.io'
response = requests.get(url)
print(response.status_code)
print(response.text)
```

That returns the page's raw HTML. To go further you'll want [`BeautifulSoup`](https://pypi.org/project/beautifulsoup4/), which pulls text out of the array of tags — `print(page.find('h1').text)` gets you the text inside an `h1`.

```python
from bs4 import BeautifulSoup
import requests

url = 'https://your-scorpion.github.io'
response = requests.get(url)
page = BeautifulSoup(response.text, 'html.parser')
print(page.title)
page.title.text
```

Now the results are parsed — handy when you can't get the data off a web page after a survey. Next, organise it. For experiments you can shove the data into Python lists. An empty list is made with `my_list = []` (the syntactic sugar) or `my_list = list()`, and you check with `type(my_list)`. A list with content looks like `count = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]`.

Lists come with a lot of useful methods. `split`, for one: `skills = 'UX,UI,JTBD,CJM,Persons,Dev'.split(',')` yields `['UX', 'UI', 'JTBD', 'CJM', 'Persons', 'Dev']`. The second useful one, `.join`, does the exact inverse of `.split`, and `.append` adds items. But when you need key–value pairs, dictionaries beat lists. You'll often meet a list holding another list holding a dictionary. Dictionary lookups are reckoned to be very fast — searching a dictionary of 500,000 values takes about as long as searching one of 500. For example:

```python
animals = {'cat': 'Murzik', 'dog': 'Bars', 'fish': 'boost', 'lemur': 'Mastik', 'parrot': 'Gesha'}
```

Look at the `dataframe`'s info. Not every field is filled in — surveys carry missing values, especially when you're polling detractors. Drop the columns you don't need:

```python
data = data.drop(['Comments'], axis=1)
data.info
data.head(10)
```

Now you can see the `Tool` column has two missing values. You could treat that as a signal in its own right, but here it's better to fill the gaps with the most frequent value — the mode.

```python
data['5'].value_counts()
fix_tool_column = data['5'].mode()[0]
data.info
```

![Cleaning the dataframe](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/PSSUQ_res.gif)

Column 16 has far more gaps, so fill those with the column mean. In ML, where you split into training and validation sets, the convention is to compute the mean only on the *training* data — you take the average answer from the training set and use it to fill the gaps in both the training and validation sets. This helps guard against overfitting. And if your dataset contains `N/A` values that you don't want pandas reading as `NaN`, use the `na_values` parameter.

```python
mean_speed = data['16'].mean()
data['16'] = data['16'].fillna(mean_speed)
data
```

You'll run into categorical variables. For something like gender, a single command settles it; but for columns with more than two possible values you'll need dummy variables. The easy route is `data = pd.get_dummies(data)` — except that converts *every* text column, and we only want to touch `Remotely`:

```python
data = pd.get_dummies(data, columns=['Remotely'])
data
```

Save the result to a new `.csv` with `data.to_csv('filename.csv')` — for example `df[[0, 1]].to_csv` — or push it straight to SQL with `df.to_sql(…)`, and analyse from there.

A quick word on cleaning before all this, since the same job often starts in a spreadsheet: to strip surplus spaces in Excel, use `TRIM`; to remove non-printing characters, use `CLEAN`. In Python, lemmatisation or stemming does the heavier lifting nicely.

## A closing note on guerrilla work

Not every study gets a budget, and the honest answer to *how do you research on a shoestring* is **guerrilla research (GRT)**. It's no substitute for a proper usability test, but it gets you feedback fast — you approach strangers and ask. The catch is cultural: in Japan there's no tradition of walking up to strangers and quizzing them about personal matters, least of all in places thick with business people. If you must, aim for Tokyo, and specifically Shibuya — a colleague swears people are more approachable there. Read the room, mind the mentality, and the templates in this piece will carry you the rest of the way.
