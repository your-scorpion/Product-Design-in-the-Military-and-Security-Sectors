# Green Design of the Urban Environment

*Adapted and translated from the original article by Maxim Tsvetkov (28 February 2022).*

More than half the planet now lives in cities. Many people first cross the city limits only in their twenties, and can't really picture a life beyond them: the forest is a park, the vegetable patch is the lawn beside the apartment block. Some cities can be reckoned as countries in their own right — Dubai, Hong Kong, Moscow, Singapore. It's no surprise that housing an enormous number of people on a small patch of land demands careful planning and greening of the urban space. Spaces that are uncomfortable to live in drive people away, and with them the city's financial health — because companies pay their taxes where the skilled specialists live. Green plantings are one of the key factors in the comfort and the aesthetics of an environment.

## Flower beds and greening

A flower bed is a plot of ornamental plants for a season or several. Walking through a city, you're bound to come across beautiful summer compositions of flowers. Naturally you'd want to see them all year round, but a garden of continuous bloom and low upkeep is hard — though not impossible — to pull off in the Eastern European belt. Since the weather differs everywhere, our first task is to read the ecological situation of the site: dry-sunny, moist-sunny, moist-part-shade, dry-part-shade, or shade-moist. And to decide the bed's function: on large areas beside ceremonial zones you'd use a parterre — a bed with a raised centre, once it runs beyond five metres — or a *rabatka*, a narrow rectangle running along the paths.

Then we choose a style. **Country** is about rural houses and the village. It uses simple cultivars (carnation, rudbeckia); nothing exotic, rarely annuals or sunflowers. The emphasis falls on fruiting shrubs — currant, gooseberry, edible rose hips — and vegetable crops. Growing edibles rewards a little agronomy. Potatoes, for one, will take remarkably varied soil, but they're happiest in a deep, stone-free bed that doesn't hold water, at around pH 5, on a gently sloping, sunny site free of frost — and a cool, breezy spot is no bad thing, since wind keeps down the aphids that carry so much disease. Sow the seed potato about 15 cm deep for good tubers, spaced up to 30 cm apart. For most vegetables you turn the soil to 15–25 cm and break the clods with a chain harrow, then roll the bed — before or after sowing — to improve germination. And never grow potatoes on the same ground two years running: rotate them — barley the first year, spring wheat the second, potatoes the third, barley again the fourth — and let the soil rest and be cleared of pests between seasons.

Another style is **woodland**, which uses plants with no love of the sun: goat's beard, bellflowers (lovely only in bloom, plain afterward), oxeye daisies, ferns, tiarella, hosta. The most popular city style is the **garden** style — bright, sun-loving plants: irises, daisies, lilies, a great many flowers, plus shrub roses, Japanese spirea, lilac, boxwood. One more is the **meadow** style — wild plants assembled into a natural biocenosis, a self-regulating community. Here we use grasses and flowering perennials. A natural biocenosis needs a large area, not a suburban tenth of a hectare (100 square metres); for perspective, even a *poor* biocenosis runs to hundreds of plants and living creatures.

Speaking of the city, we deal only with open ground: annuals, carpet bedding, biennials, perennials, and bulbs.

**Annuals** do very well in containers — unlike perennials, which freeze out in containers for want of soil. There's a current trend away from annuals; only the most beautiful are still sold. Annuals want fertile soil, but overfeed a plant and it starts piling on biomass, which isn't always what we're after. For poor soil, choose lavatera and portulaca. Other annuals — snapdragon, *Lobelia erinus*, *Ageratum houstonianum* — suit restaurants and car parks perfectly. Plant them by 15 September so they don't die over winter. All annuals love light; light part-shade gives a dreadful result. The first frosts damage the flowers; godetia, silver ragwort, and snapdragon put up at least some resistance and can hold on to 0 °C. High soil moisture kills such plants too, and given how much moisture Moscow carries, snapdragon, begonias, balsams, China pink, and sweet alyssum are no bad choice and can save the day. Consider cosmos, which is tall and doesn't ask for much soil; a touch shorter is scabiosa, very much in the post-Soviet idiom. For the front row, choose hybrid verbena, pyrethrum, and lobelia; where light is short, *Impatiens walleriana* shows well. Annuals grow in the year they're sown and don't overwinter here — in their native element these are perennials, but in our region their lot is the ceremonial bed. Annuals bloom all summer, come in every shade and shape, and are the backbone of the city flower bed. For a private garden they're too dear a pleasure.

Planting annuals means seedlings. What is a seedling? First the plants are raised in greenhouses, then pricked out into pots, then hardened off to open ground, and set out after 10 June. In central Russia there are return frosts in May that kill annuals, so planting them for the 9 May holiday risks losing the plant. But an order is an order.

So what can you set out in early spring, at the end of April and start of May, when the ground is warming? Poppies, cosmos, cornflower. Late spring — the first ten days of May, up to the 15th — is for lavatera, French marigolds, zinnia, nasturtium. And under winter, snapdragon and scabiosa, which you can scatter straight onto the snow.

If the brief leans on aesthetics — and it usually does — we choose the showy bloomers: ageratum, begonia, marigold, petunia, salvia. Every flower has many cultivars differing in colour and form; the details are always in the reference books. If we want a beautiful leaf blade, that's coleus in its variations, silver ragwort (cineraria) for grey, kochia (whose foliage can take several colours), and the classic begonias.

You can play with scent, too. If the audience has no allergies: sweet pea, night-scented stock (fragrant after dark), mignonette, tobacco (fragrant after dark). All scents intensify toward evening; in full sun the aromatic plants withhold their perfume. If we want beauty even from dried plants, that's statice, amberboa, gomphrena, and *Moluccella* — they keep their colour as they dry. For the gourmets, plant edibles: nasturtium and calendula. And if the aesthetic must rest on climbers, then sweet pea, morning glory, and scarlet runner bean.

**Carpet.** Carpet plants don't overwinter in open ground. A carpet is a low bed carrying a pattern; it's densely planted, loves sunny open spots, is usually set into a lawn, grows slowly, takes clipping well, and is very vivid. It comes in different shades. Cool shades — greys, blue-greys, silvers — include wormwood, creeping kleinia, cudweed, echeveria, and (highly recommended) santolina. Yellows — pyrethrum, coleus, marigold. Reds — begonia and barberry (which invites rust).

**Biennials** grow across two years: the first season gives only a rosette, and in the second they flower fully. After the second year the plant is either unlovely or dead. Biennials self-seed, but often don't pass their good looks to the next generation. They aren't planted in large groups. They can flower in part-shade — take the daisy, violas, forget-me-nots. The daisy needs to set seed and will then keep renewing itself in the lawn. Foxglove (poisonous, and liable to topple from its own height), carnation, and the hollyhock so dear from childhood, in the post-Soviet world and to the English alike. Biennials are raised not from seedlings but by sowing: grown on a nursery bed, then transplanted in spring; in winter they're covered with spruce branches to hold the snow.

**Perennials** are the main group we work with. Herbaceous perennials regrow from their own buds and overwinter without foliage — though there are exceptions: moss phlox, lesser periwinkle, heartleaf bergenia, *Cerastium biebersteinii*. They don't emerge from winter instantly decorative, but they're the first green patch after the snow, which is no bad thing. Perennials are what you use to compose a bed of continuous bloom; such plants change height as they flower (doronicum, delphinium, oriental poppy), though the tall ones lose their bush form and need cutting back. Many species can't live long in one place — the exceptions are peonies and goat's beard. Work the soil to at least 35–50 cm; the bed's base must be prepared in advance, improving the substrate. The soil needs to drain well and be rich in organic matter. A clay base is a problem — the bed won't survive waterlogging.

A slightly unexpected group is the ornamental alliums, perennial rhizomatous plants, and very much on-trend. They're sown into open ground from seed or planted as bulbs. A very popular member of the tribe is fragrant garlic chives (edible before flowering); if you have the willpower not to eat it, and can keep the city's residents from picking it, you'll enjoy the glow of it. In the city you can use the giant allium. All bulbs go in during late autumn, once the soil is at 10 °C; on top go compost, straw, bark, peat — we mulch. Toward the end of October, if you spot a fungus on a plant, treat it with a fungicide. Almost any soil suits ornamental alliums, but not clay and not sand, since standing water is fatal. The author is very fond of loam and has suffered much with peaty soil. For autumn: saffron and crocus in September–October, colchicum in September, *Allium ledebourianum* in early autumn, crocosmia in August. Cyclamens are very capricious and we don't count on them — not a plant of our belt.

**Bulbs** develop on an annual cycle and set seed. How beautifully to greet winter's end: snowdrops in late March and mid-April — also a bulb. Alongside them, *Crocus tommasinianus* and spring snowflake adorn the snow. After that, right at the start of May, come Puschkinia, squill, and some crocuses. Muscari appears last. In laying out a garden, the first flowers take the space between shrubs and large perennials. They're heat-loving, planted in warm spots. Only the snowdrop can live in part-shade — though it will still die in its second season, when it ought to live up to ten years, if the mice don't eat it.

**Tulips.** A very popular flower, but the popularity isn't warranted. Tulips shrink quickly, so the bulb has to be dug up and kept in special conditions — a bother, which is why we use tulips rarely. The choice falls to Darwin hybrids, Greigii, Triumph, and botanical hybrids, but not to tulips proper. Daffodils, on the other hand — Asian in origin though they are — have adapted quite well. Rhododendron flowers just after tulips; the finest are the poeticus narcissi. And the dandelion is a pest, scarring the lawn with bald patches.

### Ecological groups of perennials

**Heliophytes** are the light-lovers (wheat, all the steppe plants, alpines, bulbs). They can live in shade, but will scarcely grow. A typical heliophyte has a strong root system, tough leaves that store moisture, a sheen (a waxy bloom that reflects the sun), and a light shade of green.

**Shade-tolerant** plants (facultative heliophytes) grow in both shade and sun, though too-fierce sun will scorch them. **Shade plants** (sciophytes) grow under the forest canopy. **Hydrophytes** live wholly in water, at the meeting of water and land. **Mesophytes** live on land and in water, and **xerophytes** love drought (succulents and sclerophytes). Xerophytes (from the Greek *xērós*, "dry") live in deserts and on slopes — places where water doesn't linger.

What is a steppe? A dry summer, a cold winter, and rich soil — very much to the liking of sage, feather grass, sea holly, and gypsophila. On rocks the soil is poor, and only lyme grass, thyme, cerastium, and lavender will survive; they can't be combined with moisture-lovers. Dry southern forests are of middling fertility, part-shade, and always warm: periwinkle, barrenwort, and hyssop will appreciate them. All these plants carry very powerful roots for winning water; they hoard it; their little leaves are thick and can roll up; the plants themselves are fairly low and fragrant (lavender). If lavender won't do because it can't bear the winter, you can use cranesbill geranium, gypsophila, hyssop, bellflower, catmint, liatris, poppy, showy stonecrop, or wormwood. When the concentration of carbon dioxide rises, plants close their stomata. And if you take a real forest as your reference, remember that a young forest dries the soil while an older one moistens it.

![A xerophyte — a drought-adapted shrub with silvery foliage in sandy ground, palms behind](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/IMG_3309.jpg)

Gossypium lives in the desert.

**Moisture-loving plants** are usually large and vivid, heavy with foliage, with very long shoots for giving off moisture; a shortfall of water is easily read in their yellowing. They can shield their pollen from moisture, which is why the flowers are long and very leafy. Vivid representatives: longleaf speedwell, cranesbill geranium, bistort (the snake, the variable, and my favourite the candle-like) — covering them for winter with spruce branches or peat-compost is enough. Nor can we overlook the Siberian iris (splendid and blue), burnet, and garden phlox. They need well-drained, fertile, moist soils.

**Sunny and moderately moist places.** All of the following want very good soil and generous watering: aquilegia, aster, anemone, betony, veronica, cranesbill geranium, heuchera, oregano (to draw in insects), liatris, daylily (which can overwinter), lady's mantle (loved in Europe for borders), rudbeckia, wild blue phlox, echinacea.

And where we have frank **shade**, the shade perennials have their own peculiarities. Historically this is deciduous forest — leaf litter and forest-floor mulch. In coniferous forest the litter acidifies the soil and lets through very little light. Shade plants are small, with a very dense root system, underground shoots, horizontally held leaves (to take the most from the sun), and dark-green foliage. They have no protection against ultraviolet. Examples: brunnera (very hardy), waldsteinia, goat's beard (all but a universal plant), wild ginger (easily found in a wood), Solomon's seal, lily of the valley, lungwort, carpet bugle, hepatica. These are all relatively small; if you need something large, then rodgersia, tiarella (human impact + shade + originally poor soil = it survives), hosta, darmera.

## The design process

Plants are placed on near, middle, and far planes, and the bed's height profile is taken into account. There are many ways to arrange them: colour gradients, geometric drifts of "curtains" (the German manner), elongated ovals, gravel screenings. It's also important to reckon with the seasonality of decorative effect: spring runs from mid-May to mid-June, summer from mid-June to mid-August, autumn from mid-August to early October. For continuous bloom, the whole bed must flower without a break in every season — one plant fades, another rises — provided no phytophthora or other parasites have appeared.

### A bed's height for a moist-sunny setting

Step one: draw the mixborder with an explanatory note — where the bed sits, the particulars of its light, its area, the colour plan. Work out the quantity of planting material. In short, we plan, and try to stay within budget.

The implementation runs: prepare the planting spots, set out the plants, and maintain the bed. First the ground and the bed's base are readied — cultivation or replacement of the soil to 35–40 cm deep, using garden soil with fertilizer — and the flowers' dimensions are pegged out with builder's rods and string. Then the plants are set into the planting base. Sand is better than gravel. Remember that soil has volume: wait a little for it to settle before planting.

Planting: before the work, the bed is edged with border tape so the lawn doesn't encroach on it. The edging can be galvanized steel — just the thing for the city. For private houses, plastic tape will do, or plain stones, tiles, or a spade-cut trench. It doesn't always help, though; ground elder, with its deep roots, will draw resources from the bed regardless.

Care: watering, loosening, mulching, weeding, feeding (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, biohumus), cutting back, and removing plantain, dandelion, and couch grass. The most popular piece of internet advice — laying geotextile under the lawn — is harmful: it doesn't let water through. The weeds will survive anyway, and our more delicate plants won't. Better to use straw or coniferous litter. For a more rough-and-ready approach, dosed feeding of bulk materials is done with augers (screw springs) — ordinary flexible augers for animal feed — while for home use an MG90S servo is more suitable.

## Considering the environment

Before any work begins, it's important to understand the project's goal and get to grips with the context. A zone for comfortable living may be transit (the pavement along a roadway), pedestrian (places for strolling and rest), or residential (courtyards). For residential zones you need buffer zones. They're treated as mere elements of landscaping, though their use is far more practical than that: trees, like all plants, enrich the air with oxygen, cleanse it of harmful impurities and dust, and act favourably on temperature and humidity. For instance, in an hour at rest a person absorbs 19 litres of O₂ and gives off 16 litres of CO₂; over 24 hours a single tree produces as much O₂ as three people need to function normally over the same span. Accordingly, a hectare of green plantings releases the 2 kg of O₂ needed for two hundred people to work through a whole day.

You have to reckon with the infrastructure. Benches with backs and armrests belong in parks and by building entrances; the black backless, armrest-free benches go near the roadway. Such benches shouldn't stand on grass — in winter slush it's uncomfortable to sit. Winding benches nudge people into conversation, which means the surroundings should be beautiful — flowers, not nettles. Where there's a bench there should be a bin, large and tall, of textured metal, with a compartment for cigarette ends. The designer must also account for the soil type: conifers (firs, pines) need loose, well-drained, sandy soil, while flowers (roses, say) want chernozem. And so on — you can't plant everywhere whatever the visualizer rendered so prettily. Flower beds, likewise, should be raised, muted rather than garish, out of the way of cars and pedestrians, and planted with plants rather than litter.

Then the small things. Fences are bad; openness and freedom of movement are important factors in a comfortable urban space, and you can protect the lawns from cars with bollards. Fences are permissible around nurseries and kindergartens, where a child might throw a ball into the road and run after it under the wheels — but the point is not galvanized corrugated sheet or standard concrete slab fencing. Large canopies over the traffic lights, or very bright LEDs in their place; well-considered lamp posts, railings, and bins.

Those lamp posts and advertising columns don't simply drop into the ground, incidentally. A column has to sit on a concrete base whose surface is dead level. In the most rough-and-ready version, an extra layer of nuts is set between the column and the base plate and tightened to level it — but that isn't the recommended method. Better to make an additional cement base and level *that* perfectly. Either way you need anchor bolts, with several layers of nuts before and after the base plate; and if your climate is humid, the base plate may not bond to the ground at all. In short: look up mounting posts on anchor bolts.

![The base of a post: a flanged plate bolted to a concrete footing with anchor bolts and stacked nuts](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/photo_2024-01-16_12-03-51.jpg)

And, so that people and cars don't intrude on the shrubs' personal space, install road studs — cat's eyes — along the road.

![A road stud (cat's eye reflector) set into paving](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/aad.jpg)

And quite simply, people love trees; it helps them get away from the grey urban life of a sales manager at a middling bank. Don't forget to ask them, along the sociologist Sherry Arnstein's "ladder of participation."

The distances between buildings should be enough to give a sense of personal space. Wedging a single tree between two windows that face each other is no solution. Adapt the city to its growing number of new residents. Build local communities; improve the accessibility of the city's resources.

We don't forget accessibility, either. Everyone has seen the yellow textured tiles, especially near traffic lights — a special guidance tile for people with impaired vision. It's always in relief, and comes in two kinds:

- guidance tiles
- warning tiles

Guidance tiles carry raised lines that indicate direction, longitudinal and diagonal. Warning tiles are raised cone-shaped ribs that signal the need to stop. Tactile tiles are mostly made of pressed concrete and painted in bright colours. Their chief advantage is practicality: a durable, cheap material, easy to lay and lift. But they can be slippery in winter. Indoors — by which I mean stations, underpasses, shopping centres — porcelain-stoneware tile is used. Tile like this can point the way for people with limited mobility, since they too want to touch and smell the plants. And because such tile is bright, its colour and relief matter as an element of the design.

Does the space allow an automatic air-disinfection system? Splendid. Room for a raft of USB chargers for gadgets? Do it. Handrails with a "warm" coating over the whole area? Off we go. You can sell monstera, ficus, spider plant, calathea, and ceropegia nearby — plants like these need to develop and grow, they change in width and height, and they live in soil. Some need regular misting, and tending a large number of plantings makes for very humid conditions. Soil carries a smell after treatments and transplanting, and a large area risks plant disease.

All of these efforts bear, if indirectly, on one of a city's key metrics: FDI — Foreign Direct Investment.

## Water, finance, weather, environment

While working on a bed's design, a great many subtleties can surface. To begin with, you must satisfy people's basic demand for water resources first, and only then the plants'. A person needs at least 1,700 m³ of water a year; in Canada the reserve runs to some 100,000 tonnes per person a year. More than 10% of all water fit for consumption is used up by people over a year. But water isn't only for residents: producing a single kilogram of wheat takes a tonne of water, and beef takes 16 tonnes per kilogram. As you can see, sustaining society's life takes a great deal of water, and the quantity can't be changed — river runoff shifts little for all the increase in rainfall. We've learned to make rain, but it doesn't help much. So if a city has a water problem, handing water over to ornamental beds isn't always the best idea. Across much of Russia the natural waters are heavily polluted — the European part especially; northern Canada and Scandinavia have no such trouble. And there's no sense placing a bed where no one will see it. Obvious advice, seemingly, yet from time to time a state contract appears for a flower bed in an abandoned village. The average distance between sites in Khabarovsk Krai is 800 km; in Kamchatka Krai, 400 km; the maximum, 1,950 km (to the village of Arka) and 1,200 km (to Ossora) respectively. The road infrastructure is developed extremely poorly, and many sites have had no access roads at all — keep that in mind. From time to time we leaf through the regulatory base and read up on the particulars of territorial planning.

In Russia the most water is in the Asian part of the country, where you can spread out. The Northern Sea Route and the territories around it may, with global warming, become more relevant for plants too — as may the highly profitable Yamalo-Nenets Autonomous Okrug, home to almost all the gas and oil reserves. The greatest warming happens at high latitudes, nearly 2.5 times that of the rest of the world.

The enemy of ornamental plants is the wind. Is Russia's nature comfortable? Eastern Europe has never been famous for the gentlest of weather. Strong wind is growing stronger across the whole of Russia, save Western Siberia. Such wind is hard to forecast and appears mostly in the warm part of the day; it snaps flower stalks and can set off a fall of trees. Without good aeration, heavy metals will also keep hanging in the air, up to 100 metres above the ground. In the northern hemisphere the wind usually blows west to east, so all the pollution flies west to east as well.

And the weather can be shaped from unexpected directions. Take a factory with its chimney: if it's a hyperboloid cooling tower, there's a gentle heating at its base, and the water vapour travels from the bottom upward — in thermodynamics, heat moves from hot to cold. Modern plants evaporate not only water but a mixture of other vapours besides. As for the effect on a nearby forest, evaporation works better in a dry climate; if you know what an evaporative cooler is, you most likely live in one — the Volga region, for instance, is fairly dry, whereas Sochi and the Caucasus count as humid subtropics. In a humid climate they fit exhaust systems inside the tower, and often build other structures entirely, since the design works poorly in hot air even when it's dry. In a humid climate, too, adding humidity feeds tropical cyclones and hurricanes — which sounds alarming, but the maximum water vapour the air can hold is tied to temperature. Drop the temperature and the vapour turns at once to droplets, forming clouds; big clouds become storm clouds, and that means more rain — and more rain means a cleaner atmosphere and watered plants. Though more cloud also means less sunlight, and so less photosynthesis. All of it, in the end, is part of the same environment your plants have to live in.
