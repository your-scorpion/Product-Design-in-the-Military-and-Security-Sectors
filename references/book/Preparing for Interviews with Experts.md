# Preparing for Interviews with Experts

*Max Tsvetkov — 2 September 2020*

---

There's a popular claim that to immerse yourself in a subject you need forty interviews. But in my experience interviewing respondents on hard subjects, it doesn't always hold. It works well for investing or content creation, and badly for information security or medicine. Terminology, roles, and the internal wiring of each specific company leave such a strong imprint on respondents that, without serious preparation for *every* interview, you'll surface false insights — and development will march off in the wrong direction, which means the death of the business. There's a concept called peer review: only an expert from the same professional field can assess another expert's work, the analogue of code review. A good researcher must prepare for expert interviews, sinking into the context beforehand; a single kickoff meeting won't be enough. That's the pretty line a speaker ends a talk on before walking off stage — so let's actually look at the process of preparing for interviews with specialists in traffic analysis, and use it as the case study for how you ready yourself to interview experts in *any* hard domain.

## Who are we hunting?

Without a grasp of the subject area it's nearly impossible to sieve out the "walkers," and it's easy to over-recruit an unrepresentative audience — you'll end up with a heterogeneous sample. In information security, for instance, there are white-hat hackers who catch bugs and send them to large vendors for money; the field is called Bug Bounty, a whole industry with its own services — HackerOne, Pentestit, Hack The Box — which for us are sources of respondents. And don't forget the country tie-in: in some jurisdictions it's dangerous to look for vulnerabilities in a company's network infrastructure even where it's officially permitted, and even respondents who do such work won't talk about it, which makes recruiting harder still. Another role is the penetration tester, a distinct breed who breaks into a company's internal systems — breaching a bank's systems, say, to see how the paper-security people, the former law-enforcement staff, the SOC 1/2/3 (SecOps) rapid-response teams, and the security engineers react, and how ready the company was. A multitude of roles surfaces, each with its own tools and zones of responsibility.

How do you learn all this? You can't skip courses — Cybrary, Coursera, Pentester Academy, SANS, eLearnSecurity. A course walks you superficially through the core principles and terms of the field and gives you context; after that come books and articles on wired and wireless networks, reading discussions, listening to webinars. You have to enrich yourself constantly with knowledge, and sometimes make peace with a coarser register of speech. Arriving at an interview and asking a respondent about UNION-based SQL-injection cases that exist only in books equals a failed interview.

Recruiting the *internal* experts is a gentler problem than it sounds — a decision-maker often imagines you need a whole bespoke panel, when an email round is usually enough. You can pair Hotjar with Calendly for the survey and the scheduling, or lean on whatever internal tooling you already have; you *can* over-engineer it with something like Handflow or Bitrix, but that tends to hurt more than help. Finding experts on a *foreign* market is the harder case, and the simplest move there is to hire a local researcher or agency and delegate. Beyond that, some cohorts turn up on Fiverr or Craigslist, and the classics still work — email, Facebook, LinkedIn (which has LinkedHelper and Sales Navigator, able to friend an audience by set parameters and view profiles from your account, though it needs a well-established account and targeting there is expensive). In B2B you can even target a lead on Facebook by their phone number, or run interactive quiz-ads. The desperate, poor option is finding a Russian speaker inside the country whose subject you care about.

## The domain

Every project opens with a stage of analysis, an attempt to settle on a direction of travel — in the design world this is called *ideation*. For an adversary it's *reconnaissance* — analysing network traffic, for example. There's the term Black Hat, the malicious hackers: they hunt for old abandoned servers and open ports — reconnaissance — and then attack. Recon includes fingerprinting software versions, open ports, and so on, achieved by scanning, which is itself already an attack, because it can hang some industrial-control (ICS/АСУ ТП) system. On the company's side there's first defence, and then… nothing — companies rarely spend resources hunting the source of an attack. Though in game development the developers themselves may hand a closed hacker community a tool for cracking the game, winning trust that way and gaining access to the community, watching how people break their game and which loopholes they use. So a base set of interview goals about a company's network looks like this: what routing and switching design exists in the network; which link-, network-, and transport-layer protocols and technologies are used; the existing addressing plan and VLAN scheme; device naming; and the AAA parameters.

## Terminology

During the interview we tune ourselves to the respondent's vocabulary. If the respondent calls a training screen a "tutorial," we use that word too. A computer is an "asset" and any endpoint devices are "assets"? Then that's what we call them. We say *forensics*, not "incident investigation." We know that DFIR is digital forensics and incident response, and how it differs from incident handling — a technology story versus a managerial one.

Network attacks vary by their object (infrastructure, telecommunications services) and split by character into active and passive. An active attack is an attempt to swap an SSL certificate to eavesdrop on HTTPS traffic; a passive one is scanning. Our first question to a respondent should not be "what kinds of attack are there," but "which *passive* network attacks have you dealt with in the last two years." Feel the difference?

The OSI model — the OSI/ISO network-protocol stack — lets us structure attack types. One class of attack targets the physical layer, where the goal is physical damage. Cutting the wires or smashing a junction box comes to mind, but that's closer to vandalism; something more serious is stringing a wire across the rails so automated systems believe a train occupies the section. Or gaining physical access to a router and, through its hardware interfaces, reading the device's firmware straight out of memory — the sort of thing legitimate researchers do in a lab, and the reason physical access is treated as a genuine threat surface. White hats legitimately place a tap or a SPAN port and feed the data into an IDS or SIEM (data-processing and intrusion-detection systems); the SIEM then does retrospective analysis, correlation, aggregation, and containment, and helps express the security level numerically. That mirror port on the switch must have enormous throughput, since it duplicates data from every port into one.

Data travels from the network through physical cables — themselves a potential point of intrusion — and the analysis of hard problems, too, is conventionally begun at the physical layer. OSI is cumbersome, though; the more compact TCP/IP is implemented on every modern network operating system. But let's start with OSI.

The **physical layer** is Ethernet — copper cable, twisted pair plugged into the computer, occasionally coaxial (a single central conductor, one cable meaning one direction of data flow). The RJ-45 connector carries eight contact plates for the signal; twisted pair holds four twisted pairs of wires, and for 1000 Mbps all eight are used, fewer for lower speeds, which is why the twist order matters. The categories run: cat 1, a doorbell; cat 3, 10 Mbps (telephone, 10BaseT); cat 5, 100 Mb; cat 5e and cat 6, gigabit; cat 7, 10 gigabit; cat 8, 40 gigabit — plainly for data centres, not a desk. Don't confuse RJ-45 with the telephone RJ-11, which looks similar; of the eight wires only four carry data, the rest serving correct operation. Modern kit does Auto MDI-X, so all the old ceremony is now rare — but outdated equipment is an excellent target for an attacker, and good specialists always watch the hardware's end-of-support date, usually announced three to four years out.

Types of link: *simplex* is one-way, used mostly for broadcasting — one tower distributing a signal to many receivers — and if TCP/IP is in play, it isn't simplex. *Half-duplex* is two-way, but only one device may transmit at a time; simultaneous exchange is impossible because the signals would collide and corrupt. This is the classic bus topology, built on a single cable with CSMA/CD: charges of +5 V fly down the cable from one computer, convert to 0s and 1s, and reach every attached machine, none of which can send until the current sender finishes — and if two send at once you get a +10 V signal. Why would you want that? Old devices, management demands, cable quirks. And *full-duplex*, the familiar full connection, where data flows both ways at once — a phone call, where you speak and listen together. One way to implement it is over copper, which imposes a *physical* rather than software limit, since transmission runs on electricity: copper is capped in length, no more than 100 metres of twisted pair, a length that lets you detect a collision. Imagine, across the distances of Russia, running copper with that limit between cities. Copper also creates a high-voltage field, which can invite a lightning strike or disturb other devices, and wire thickness won't help. On the plus side, the speed of current inside a copper conductor exceeds the speed of light. Anything from 10 Gbps up is always full-duplex, and the general advice is: always set `duplex auto` and `speed auto`. The second cable type is optical, and here we speak of tens of kilometres — it needs less electricity, which matters in data centres (with hundreds of thousands of ports, less electricity means less heat means less cooling). Cables cross the Atlantic from London to New York; imagine the distances and the volume of data they carry. Some numbers: light travels at 300,000 km/s; in copper or fibre at 100,000 km/s; 1 ms equals 100 km, plus processing time — so a 1 ms round trip is achievable only within, roughly, a single apartment block. There are two kinds of optical fibre: multi-mode, cheaper and shorter (around 600 metres), and single-mode, expensive and using a laser rather than an LED, which can be stretched over hundreds of kilometres because the laser sends a single beam. Optical cable is fragile — glass inside dislikes dirt and cracks and clouds over time — and easy to break, itself a potential hazard level.

There's the notion of an *air gap*, physical isolation from external networks; then an attacker is left only with physical harm, or plugging in an infected USB stick, against which an air gap is no defence — such devices sell freely on sites like Hak5. Malware exists that can command computers even in closed segments, and wireless technology hasn't gone away: flying a quadcopter up to a coveted target is no trouble today.

Other OSI attack layers: the link layer (ARP, spoofing a node), network attacks (IP spoofing), transport (TCP, UDP, various scans), and session (SSL attacks). Since we can now open a website on nearly any device — a vacuum cleaner, a scooter — there are abstract network models, OSI and TCP/IP. TCP/IP is used almost everywhere, but the reference description of network interaction is OSI: OSI is the description, TCP/IP is the hands-on work. The layers don't depend on one another, which lets you roll out new technology like IPv6 without breaking everything (on Windows you can run `netsh interface ipv6 show prefixpolicies` to see your IPv6 parameters). Layers 7 through 5 are inseparable and integrated in one client application — the browser-and-software layer — and the TCP/IP stack has four or five layers.

Methods of addressing data: *unicast* addresses one recipient, *broadcast* is one message to all, and the rarer *multicast* sends to a group. The network we know grew out of the telephone data network, which is tied to circuit switching: place a call and a channel is occupied. Old rotary phones simply sent digits into the network as you dialled, the processing logic living inside the network — but that was the era of occupying a channel; smartphones send *packets*. To send a 1 GB file, it's sliced into many small packets, and the endpoint must know how to receive them and reassemble the file. Data at the link layer is called *frames*, at the network layer *packets*, at the transport layer *segments*, and at the application layer *streams* — though in an interview you can call all of it "packets," because that's how specialists talk day to day. Pick up a router and you'll find a WAN port — the whole internet, where the provider's cable goes — and LAN, the local network (a 20-office company is one LAN; a home router is a LAN too). There's also MAN, a city network linking several buildings; CAN for a university campus; SAN when you join many hard drives with layer-2 technologies; and PAN, a network of Bluetooth devices.

The **second layer** of OSI, the link layer, holds the addressing logic. Ethernet won — it needs no configuration; addresses are burned into the adapter at the factory and present out of the box. That address is the MAC address, a plain abstract 6-byte number written in hex. (If your free airport internet runs out, changing the MAC is often enough — it also helps avoid detection from a Wi-Fi radio constantly broadcasting its MAC to nearby access points; the point being that a MAC *can* be spoofed.) The link layer, in effect, splits a continuous data stream into a sequence of discrete, addressed packets. There are three MAC types — the popular unicast, multicast (which can't be a sender address), and broadcast (to all) — and a special broadcast MAC, `ff-ff-ff-ff-ff-ff`, the very last address in the range, meaning everyone who receives the packet must process it. Where copper is involved there's a risk of signal distortion from a strong magnetic field, so the electricity out can differ from the electricity in; checking output against input happens via CRC, a simple hash comparison — a mathematical function computes a number and writes it to the end of the L2 header, and the receiving side computes and compares. The layer-2 device is the switch; with full-duplex now ubiquitous, a collision domain is virtually impossible. Smart switches analyse MAC addresses, remember ports and addresses in an address table, and keep packets from colliding; inside is a special chip, an ASIC, that processes packets and trims headers to 1500 bytes. Chain ten switches together — they have limited ports — and a broadcast (ARP) to `ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff` passes through all ten: a broadcast domain. You can even loop the sending into a ring, and the packet will wander in a circle at layer 2 forever, which is why STP must always be on. (To see the ARP table yourself, run `arp -a` — and note it's a slightly dishonest command, showing only one segment's cache over 90 seconds, not who is on the network.) And VLANs let you pin three ports to accountants and three to designers, so traffic from one VLAN can't cross into another — one physical box, split into VLANs, an extra header at layer 2, with no crossing between VLANs inside the switched network.

The **third layer**, the network layer, answers for connectivity between hosts through global addressing (IP). Everything above works fine in a small 20-host network, but for the whole internet with billions of devices, messaging all billion is a dubious plan; we want to *assign* addresses rather than rely on factory-burned MACs. Hence the IP address — for each interface we can set one by hand, say 192.168.0.1, controlling the addressing and knowing where to send packets. An IP is simply a 32-bit number, four blocks of 0–255, each block an *octet*; to shorten the 32-bit number we split it into 8-bit groups and write their decimals separated by dots. The popular ones: *0.0.0.0*, the sender's address until an IP is assigned, and the default route in routing tables; *255.255.255.255*, the familiar broadcast, limited to the current broadcast domain and used in DHCP or PPPoE when the server's IP is unknown (to discover DHCP you broadcast across the network with sender 0.0.0.0 and recipient 255.255.255.255); *127.0.0.1*, the machine's own address, through which local apps can talk and debug and whose packets never leave the machine, often the address of a local service's web interface meant to open only on that machine; *192.168.0.1*, almost always the default for a home router, and *192.168.1.1* for a DSL modem, both local-network addresses; and *8.8.8.8* and *8.8.4.4*, Google's public DNS. DNS lets us type google.com to reach an IP; in the early internet people visited sites by IP, typing octets instead, but google.com is easier to remember, so the DNS record was invented — `nslookup mail.ru` or `ping mail.ru` reveal the IP behind a record, and `nslookup ya.ru 8.8.8.8` reaches Yandex through Google's DNS (`ipconfig /all` shows all your network parameters, including the DNS server). For IP work we use the router, which knows exactly where to send a packet and sends nothing if it doesn't know the address; its routing table starts empty and must be populated by network engineers, and routers need far more configuration than switches — either static routes by hand or automated dynamic routing. The configuration stage is the *control plane*, the operating stage the *data plane*, and routes end up compiled to machine code for the chips. Because L2 and L3 work together, you also need ARP, the protocol that finds which MAC belongs to which IP; ARP runs at the link layer while, say, PPTP runs at the third, and attackers and defenders secure each layer separately.

To reach another device with a given IP, first set your local IP and subnet mask by hand; then `ping 192.168.121.200` (that address set by hand on the second device, the two connected directly by cable since there's no router here); find your own IP with `ipconfig`; and on Windows PowerShell the excellent `tnc` checks a port, for example `tnc mail.ru -port 443`. Now the router knows where to send the packet — but into which application? For that we use ports. Web servers usually use TCP port 80 for HTTP, the safer HTTPS uses TCP 443, and remote administration over SSH usually uses TCP 22. If you want to play with SSH, download PuTTY, good for SSH and Telnet — but never send passwords over Telnet; use SSH.

The **transport layer**, the fourth, is about guaranteed and non-guaranteed delivery and lets you identify applications on a host; our server must work out what data is arriving, and UDP enters the picture. The layer is broadly about application protocols and their ports: DNS on UDP 53, HTTP on TCP 80, HTTPS on TCP 443, SSH and SFTP on TCP 22 — a port being a software layer for exchanging data between device and network, between server and client.

The remaining layers exist within OSI/ISO — application, presentation, session, transport, network, link, physical — and the fifth, the session layer, lets applications on different devices hold an active connection within a session, in half- or full-duplex.

With all this, we can formulate the right questions and ask them in context: How do you work with a breach of the confidentiality of information or a resource? At which OSI layer do you hit the most problems? What does a breach of integrity mean to you, and what risks does it carry? What follows from a loss of a system's availability? And we can go further — since the word *risk* has appeared, what *is* "risk"? There's a definition in ISO/IEC 27000, but do the managers hold to it? Is a vulnerability the same as a CVE? Then the reserve, context-setting questions: there is generating equipment in the fuel-and-energy sector that supplies cities with heat and electricity, so there is a body of physical equipment, whole facilities even, that may be incompatible with a vendor's solution — how does the head of information security for critical-infrastructure (КИИ) facilities determine the compatibility of production with a type of protection system to remove the risks of adverse impact? How are the response and reliability functions arranged? How is patch management arranged?

Analogous entity-division schemes exist in every field. In the information security of industrial control systems, five levels are distinguished: sensors, valves, and gates; controllers; the SCADA server, HMI, and other services; the demilitarised zone; and MES, corporate information systems, and SIEM. And there's the field's own legislation — mandatory certification of cryptographic means under FSB Order No. 66, say — which opens fresh questions on equipment uptime, the certification of cryptographic means by class, monitoring the load on ICS servers, vulnerability reports, MES elements, inventory, connection analytics, and the specifics of using scanners to remove or compensate for vulnerabilities. For fintech you can talk separately about 382-P, 167-FZ, 152-FZ, 187-FZ, 115-FZ on financial transactions, and CCPA and GDPR for companies aiming at international markets, topped by FSTEC Orders 17 and 21, STO BR IBBS / GOST 57580, and PCI DSS.

Testing whether the respondent even shares your understanding of a term is its own small discipline, and worth building into the guide. One check is to look at the search-query frequency for the words; a purely qualitative version is to give the words in context and ask the respondent to pick the intended one, then ask "can you give a precise definition of what this word means?" as a yes/no, followed by an open question. It sounds trivial and it repeatedly saves interviews.

## Exercise 1 — walking in their shoes

Meeting the terms and the respondents' surroundings is useful, but beyond that an interview needs *empathy*, and for empathy we must stand in the respondent's shoes and analyse traffic ourselves. We'll work with Kali Linux in Oracle VM VirtualBox: import the appliance, and in `Devices → Network` set a bridged adapter if you want internet (it would be odd to listen to traffic without any) or a host-only adapter for an isolated environment.

Now to the traffic, with our first tool, Wireshark — a sniffer, a traffic analyser. Installed anywhere but the gateway, and with no MITM in play, Wireshark alone is useless: you'll mount no SSL attack, and we have no such aim — we simply want to sit in the respondent's shoes and watch traffic, and for that Wireshark is among the best. It also shines when you're solving a hard fault with no hypotheses about the cause. Open *Statistics → Conversations*, configure it to taste, and you immediately see your traffic and can filter it every which way; Wireshark also ships with rules that colour problems by their severity — which is instantly a question for the interview guide, about the respondent's habits with the interface, the colours, and the filters. Visit any `http` site, enter any login and password, and set yourself the goal of finding unencrypted passwords, files, or any other valuable information in the flow. Hint one: files are usually large, and everything tiny (bytes) is just control connections and service packets — so sort by size and ask what other sortings the respondent needs. Hint two: we care about ports (0–65535) that services connect to; filter on `http.request.method==POST` and you'll find many rows with the OCSP protocol — so, at once, what do respondents look for first in such a table? Wireshark isn't the only tool — there are tcpdump, or Windump for Windows — so we must ask which software the respondent uses to capture traffic, and why. To find files there's *Follow TCP*, which surfaces them in encoded form (decodable through a decoder), and passwords on HTTP sites show straight in the logs. Credential-brute-forcing tools such as Hydra exist too, and the researcher's job here is not to run one but to understand *why the defender cares* — about lockout thresholds, rate-limiting, and login-versus-password attempts — and to turn that into questions.

A practical note on the *lab itself*, which is where several reader questions land. To capture in-session feedback while you practise, there are dedicated devices: a Stream Deck, whose keys you can label with any icons or emotions — buy one, add the InfoWriter add-on to OBS, and bind a key that records a timestamp with the feedback on press. And on the broader question of running experiments against *real* devices: you don't. You start with emulation and virtualisation, because no one will let you change anything in production unmonitored, and you'll never have the right hardware on hand — the employer won't buy a network mirroring production for your experiments, so you test the logic on virtual machines. You need a machine with plenty of RAM and a virtualisation-capable processor; then, since you can't simply run a router on Windows or macOS, you add a hypervisor to run another OS — VMware (one of the best; the free player enables hardware virtualisation), the built-in Windows Hy-V (which needs current updates for nested virtualisation), or Fusion on Mac; VirtualBox is weaker and I don't recommend it. The hypervisor runs a virtual machine, and on top you add network-virtualisation software — EVE-NG, the old and hard-to-download GNS3, or Cisco's paid CML (about $200 a year, technically similar but with a nicer interface) — which runs virtual routers and switches whose operating systems are identical to the ones on the physical boxes. Getting the image running is half the job; you still need a network between them. Cisco offers all its own images; with EVE-NG you download from vendor sites. You may want to pull an image off a real router, but a router has two halves, control plane and data plane, and the control plane's architecture varies — the processor may not be the expected one — which affects whether the router will run at all. With VMware you download an OVF (a ready VM to import) or an ISO (to install via CD-ROM), tick the virtualisation box, launch, enter the address in the browser, create a lab, and design your network; for a comfortable terminal you fetch the EVE-NG Windows client side and WinSCP for pushing images. It's worth knowing the built-in Cisco IOS commands, where `|` is the *and* operation: `show running-config | in ex s`; `show interfaces | in is up` to show only interfaces that are up; `show ip route <> longer-prefixes` for parent and child routes; `show processes cpu`; `ping 1.1.1.1 size 1500 source 2.2.2.2 repeat 10000 df-bit`, where you can set the size and use `repeat` for the packet count; and `telnet 1.1.1.1 3389` to check a specific service's reachability on a host.

## The information field

We've done one exercise; dozens are needed, and they reveal the *information field* the respondent lives in and their routine. As you work through the tasks you start hunting answers, and — having already done the courses and the books, so you know the terminology — you learn from Google and from in-house experts a mass of relevant resources. In our case you can wander open sources like [vulners.com](https://vulners.com), read up on vulnerabilities from MITRE, on Google Dorks, on Microsoft's vulnerability databases, and get acquainted with the CIS Benchmarks (the Center for Internet Security's best practices). MITRE is the well-known catalogue of tactics and techniques describing how attacks are carried out and how they're minimised — visually a framework of columns of step-by-step actions; in many defensive tools such as SIEM there's log-accumulation and analysis in which you can review staff activity and security incidents, then interpret the data through MITRE, which is the basis of a SOC's work and lets an expert see which step an attacker is on: techniques in the rows, tactics in the headers. Analogous tables exist for cloud services and mobile platforms, and there are other sources like [FSTEC](https://bdu.fstec.ru/) and [OWASP](https://owasp.org/) — the top 25 from these services being the core competence of many security specialists.

Knowing how attacks work, we understand which measures and means of protection to apply — but the question of the cost-to-protection ratio arises. How important is it for a company to build software by "Security by Design"? Will the profit from fewer zero-day vulnerabilities cover the longer release times? Companies sit at different stages of security maturity, as of design maturity: passive protection with utilities is cheap and closes many problems; then comes the need for active protection (AV, SIEM, NGFW); the next step is preventive work against cyberattacks; and beyond that, huge companies hire pentesters to break their own resources — all of it revolving around MITRE. MITRE assigns each vulnerability a number, say CVE-2020-003 (the third of the year); a security engineer knows their users' software, checks it for vulnerabilities, and either patches or forbids it — but there are "zero-day" vulnerabilities, usually surfaced by experienced specialists or hacker groups who learn that a targeted company runs some software while the large vendors have no information yet. At once, questions arise about roles, internal processes, precedents and their consequences, the cost of damage, and the demand for sandbox-level solutions alongside antivirus. Or something more technical, phrased for a UX researcher:

- What difficulties have you hit while deploying, operating, or scaling your SIEM?
- What problems do you face when applying Sigma rules to convert correlation rules into your SIEM, and where do you get the Sigma rules (a permissible hint: Atomic Threat Coverage, HELK)?
- What is a comfortable total cost of ownership for a SIEM, including staff and hardware?
- Which connectors will you need in the near term?
- How is your Identity Management arranged, and what problems do you face?
- What functionality lets you say you're looking at a next-generation SIEM?
- Why isn't the presence of an FSTEC certificate among your selection criteria?
- What matters most for meeting the protection requirements of state information systems (first-level ГИС)?
- With what data, from which sources, do you enrich the SIEM?
- Why this particular SIEM, and why not the Elastic Stack?
- What role does SOAR play in your security team's work?
- How would you feel if product *n* ceased to exist? (Sean Ellis's product-market-fit question.)

## Exercise 2 — passive reconnaissance

Passive network attacks are scanning the network in search of hosts — by ICMP, ARP, TCP RST segments, replies to non-existent DNS queries, or listening. Our second tool is Nmap, which has an official pack of scripts for vulnerability scanning and does far more than find open and closed ports. Because the theme is sensitive to the law, run every experiment against **scanme.nmap.org**, the practice site the Nmap authors maintain for exactly this. Scanning can modify TCP packets, sent to an address to reveal whether a port is open, gathering statistics — was there a reply to a packet with the RST flag, after how long, what OS or router driver (Windows and Linux answer differently) — so the results let you guess that a host runs, say, nginx 0.7.64 on Windows. From the Nmap book you can learn the port statuses: an open port is the desired target, a direct path to attack; a closed port receives packets but doesn't answer; and such actions leave *traces*, which any attacker wants to erase — perhaps through reverse address translation — again, a question for the expert interview after your desk research. Scripts can also name the vulnerabilities present for the discovered software, which raises its own questions: the regularity of vulnerability checks, which software is forbidden and why, and who is responsible for vetting new software in the company's infrastructure.

Scanning by hand is tedious, and mass-scanning tools exist that are dramatically faster — but this is exactly where responsibility matters more than any command. Keep experiments to scanme.nmap.org or a range you own; before touching anything, check where an address is physically located (a service like [1whois](http://1whois.ru/) will tell you) and don't scan resources in your own jurisdiction — scanning Uruguay for study is one thing, scanning your own country another, since state resources are monitored (in Russia by ГосСОПКА, a state IDS/monitoring system with SIEM as a mandatory technical means), Tor is restricted and works only over TCP, and a single random probe against a government site can find you trouble. That is the substance a researcher needs; the exact flags for firing ten thousand random probes are not.

DoS and DDoS are worth understanding as concepts. A DoS attack comes from one node; a DDoS is distributed, driven from a command-and-control (C&C) server — a control hub for many machines — and is rarely aimed at ordinary users' personal machines. It's tracked simply, by load: you watch the interface for activity in quiet hours, and if the CPU is normally 20 percent at night and suddenly hits 80, either staff blundered or you're under attack. A flood of meaningless requests is often *cover* for a more serious intrusion, and from a penetration-testing view a DoS is simply a stress test of a server's resilience — a TCP SYN flood, for instance, aims less to exhaust network resources than to exhaust the memory allocated for storing sequence numbers, while a UDP flood overloads the channel so the last mile lacks capacity. All of this must be *monitored*, so it's worth learning a simple tool like **nload**, which shows a window of live traffic. On the far side, real users can't reach the service, the business loses money, and competitors rejoice.

How to defend? Many anti-DDoS services exist — big players like CloudFlare and StormWall parallelise traffic in the cloud, routing it through virtual machines, good for externally hosted sites, giving DoS protection and content filtering. State bodies use dedicated appliances, called ПАК, to cut DDoS traffic; they're convenient because, beyond the WAF itself, they bundle many technologies. If you're small and non-commercial and hit by such an attack, you can see from the traffic where it comes from and call your provider, or block by GeoIP at your firewall. A WAF (Web Application Firewall) is that application-layer firewall filtering HTTP traffic at OSI layer 7 — inside, a set of rules with actions, able to block traffic in the response; in reality it's not only request/response (a bridge) but also a sniffer, a reverse proxy, and a passive log-analysis mode. The simplest form is a plugin for the web server, an Apache or Nginx module — pfsense, installable to turn a computer into a router and ideal when an admin must track who in the local network goes where; a typical module is mod_security, present in almost every Linux distribution, with the CRS ruleset baked in.

More serious than scanning are *spoofing* attacks, whose foundation underlies the man-in-the-middle. Technically an attacker poisons the routing so that all traffic passes through their machine, yielding a MITM position from which they can capture and modify packets, merely listen, or even swap a certificate. This is not necessarily malicious: a security engineer can run a MITM inside corporate networks for the company's own ends, monitoring traffic to catch a virus mid-download and feed a DLP (data-loss-prevention) system. It's already clear that black and white hats share the same tools and differ only in goals — itself a thing to ask respondents about. Certificate downgrade from HTTPS to HTTP is the classic move (the tool here is SSLstrip): an attacker pulls traffic to themselves via ARP spoofing, exchanges packets with the server as the client over an encrypted connection, and offers the client plain HTTP, which some accept — though nearly everywhere HSTS now guards against certificate substitution, so the attack often fails. In ARP spoofing the attacker answers a target's broadcast, claiming their own machine holds the requested IP and MAC, and receives the data, usually as the base for a more complex attack; the named tools include Ettercap, Arpoison, and Cain and Abel. I'm deliberately describing the *shape* of this chain rather than the runnable recipe — the value to the researcher is understanding it well enough to ask which of these tasks the security team actually handles.

## Exercise 3 — vulnerabilities

There's a standard, ISO 27005, that describes an *asset* as any entity of value to the organisation — sites, applications, servers — and a security engineer needs a map of such assets and an understanding of their potential vulnerabilities, which arise for varied reasons: programmers' errors in code (buffer overflow), weak login/password requirements (user/user), the possibility of SQL injection, and the human factor. Naturally, security scanners and antivirus find *known* vulnerabilities; you can't find what isn't known. You can talk separately about processor-architecture flaws (Meltdown, Rowhammer, Spectre): there are processor protection rings, a mechanism for delimiting access to a PC's resources — four in all, but usually only 0 and 3 are used, governing memory, I/O ports, and instruction execution — where user applications run at ring 3 and the kernel space at ring 0 (the kernel may do anything, ring 0; software may do nothing, ring 3).

Vulnerabilities are scored quantitatively on the CVSS index, a metric about a vulnerability's impact, maintained by a dedicated [organisation](https://www.first.org/cvss/); scoring is done by the community rather than automatically, so it's gradually moving to machine learning, and the attentive will have seen two scores on vulners — CVSS from people and from the algorithm. The metric rests on fairly simple parameters — impact on confidentiality, availability of remediation, and so on — and there's the excellent [cve.mitre.org](https://cve.mitre.org/); when reading a CVE the most important thing is the *impact*, for example that the vulnerability allows remote command execution on a server. CVE-2020-1350 carries the maximum criticality, a CVSS score of 10.0, because it's easy to exploit and extremely virulent. Many antivirus vendors build their own vulnerability-tracking systems — Kaspersky may know a sensor has preset credentials while Avast does not — and you can also read [cwe.mitre.org](https://cwe.mitre.org/), a list of software with vulnerabilities, hardware ones included, as general information. Knowing all this, security engineers track [Microsoft's guide](https://msrc.microsoft.com/update-guide/vulnerability) for the updates they must apply, and we can be responsible citizens and write to the admins that the UPnP protocol in use has CVE-2020-12695, which allows DDoS attacks and internal-network scanning. Broadly, assessment goes by CVE (words) or CVSS (numbers), and CVSS has three big blocks: the base metric group, temporal metrics, and environmental metrics — the environment being the chance to reduce risk through system settings, the temporal characterising a vulnerability's tendency to strengthen or weaken over time, and the base, first group holding many sub-items such as ease of exploitation.

Hunting vulnerabilities by hand is poor practice. **OpenVAS** is a vulnerability scanner and host-, OS-, and service-discovery tool, automated and free — though, again, it won't find zero-days, and be careful, since PLC controllers can drop offline under such automated checks. **Nikto** is another scanner (running `nikto -h https://your-scorpion.ru` against your own site is a standard self-check). Building a list of your own IP addresses is a very common task, helped by Dorks and Shodan, and by simpler tools; **RustScan** is an alternative. Any security scanner can work in *black-box* mode (modelling a hacker's actions) or *white-box* mode (an audit, where you hand the scanner all the credentials so it can investigate fully). Installing OpenVAS and running a black-box scan is a wizard-driven affair that walks you through the steps and highlights the important results.

## Exercise 4 — credentials

The most valuable thing is passwords, and the concepts here matter more than any command. First, three different terms: *encoding*, *hashing*, and *encryption*. Base64 is merely a substitution of one set of symbols for another and decodes trivially; hashing is an *irreversible* operation; encryption is harder still and needs a key — symmetric encryption uses the same key at both ends, while TLS uses asymmetric. On Linux the password hashes live in `/etc/shadow` (with account data in `/etc/passwd`), and offline cracking works by taking a candidate from a wordlist, transforming it by the known algorithm, and comparing it to the stored hash — which is why good wordlists run to hundreds of gigabytes and are themselves a prized, hard-to-obtain asset, and why the cracking tool must be told the algorithm in advance. The practical, *defensive* takeaways are what a researcher carries into the interview: that physical or boot-level access to a machine can enable a password reset — hence why physical security and boot protection are treated seriously — and that password policy, hashing choice, and lockout thresholds are exactly the ground to question an expert on. (I'm keeping this at the level of principles and defences rather than the step-by-step account-takeover procedure.)

## Exercise 5 — exploitation

Here we'd normally reach for the [Metasploit Project](https://www.metasploit.com/), and for **Metasploitable**, a deliberately vulnerable Ubuntu build with a heap of weak applications and open ports, made for training pentesters. It's the game of Blue Team (the good folk who set up the defence) versus Red Team (who attack and try to break everything) — terms, like most in the field, and like the ISSAF and OSSTMM methodologies, inherited from the military, with the pentester's findings delivered on a template. Two definitions are worth carrying: an *exploit* is a module that exploits a vulnerability — code that can use it — and a *payload* is what rides on the exploit, the shell that does the work. A payload may be delivered as a link with malicious content; a *stage-payload* is a small file that downloads a larger one, which antivirus doesn't always catch; and there's the file-binding family, where malicious code hides inside an ordinary file. Metasploit has its own scanners and fuzzers (flooding ports with junk data to force a buffer overflow), and its exploit modules are *ranked* by reliability, which is how a pentester plans their next moves. The point of all this, for us, is not to run the chain — I won't lay out the module-selection, host-and-port setup, exploitation, persistence, or log-clearing steps, which together are just an attack-and-cover-your-tracks playbook — but to understand the kill-chain well enough to interview experts about how it's *detected*, and to note that standard payloads are, thankfully, readily caught by antivirus on the host.

After reading this and the supporting material, a mass of questions should surface that would never have occurred to you before, and we've moved from the detail level of "how vulnerable is your company's network?" to "how did attackers register themselves in persistent mode in your critical infrastructure over the last two years?" — and can now interview experts properly. A few years in one field, and you can move from the structured interview to the unstructured. We understand that a security engineer's field of interest spans procedures (code review, policies), physical control (guards, access control, confiscating a phone), the perimeter (NGFW, FW, DLP, DDoS), the network (TLS, proxy), hosts (MDM, vulnerabilities, management), and information (encryption and protection). Nor should we forget the *business* side, for which security is a cost and profit is only the potential damage avoided — a reduction in potential expense — though money is also lost to idle salaried staff, information leaks, fraud, absenteeism, team conflict, and the theft and substitution of components. And the *end users* as victims, since we must protect not only the employer's business but its customers: attacks can be very simple, such as a transparent `iframe` over a button that redirects to the attacker's services — popular on pirate sites and called *clickjacking*, easily avoided in new browsers by forbidding the site's content in external frames with CSP, and universally by the `X-Frame-Options` header, which works for all browsers (`Content-Security-Policy: frame-ancestors 'none';` for none, or naming a permitted site). Duplicating traffic, finally, is another means of both attack and defence: you can monitor via port mirroring — a SPAN session on a switch, copying one port's traffic to another — and so watch whether staff are harming the company, a legitimate defensive-monitoring technique any network admin knows.

## In closing

For all that the preparation described here is deeply technical, we are still talking *with* people and *about* people. There are models for describing an attacker's behaviour; a popular one is the *dark triad* — narcissism, Machiavellianism, and psychopathy — which can be used to describe an attacker. It can be enriched with the OCEAN facets, the five key character traits — Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism — which break into sub-facets: openness needs intellect, to be ready for new ideas, hard tasks, and uncertain circumstances and so to be creative; assertiveness, intellect, and diligence are strongly interrelated and all tied to performance, which can make them especially useful in leadership research or personnel selection, while diligence without intellect is a sorry combination for hard-to-master professions. We can also measure seven aspects of a company's security culture: attitude to security, behaviour, knowledge, communication (not disclosing secrets), precise compliance, norms, and responsibility. And because modern security *is* social engineering, the first stage of a breach begins with gathering information, building trust with the victim, nudging the person toward the desired action, and finally obtaining the goal — using OSINT, any publicly available information, the favourite tactic of hacktivists, whether about an organisation or a person's private life.

The same interview discipline, notice, scales far beyond security — and when you turn it on a subordinate expert rather than an outside one, the aim shifts to gauging their level and motivation, for which the Dilts pyramid works well: its six layers run from mission (*what for?*) through identity (*who am I?*), beliefs (*what do I believe?*), capabilities (*how do I choose?*), and behaviour (*what do I do?*) to environment (*what do I have?*). Working the environment level, you can ask how comfortable the person is, what troubles them in their surroundings, where they want to be, and whether the environment helps them reach their goals; you find the weakest layer and concentrate on it. To read a team's general mood you can use *emotives* — the special units of language that name emotional states: love, joy, anger, indifference, indignation, grief, pain, envy, jealousy.

And when the question is no longer security but *money* or *brand*, the toolkit shifts again while the interviewing craft stays the same. To learn whether and at what price people will buy, there are the accepted approaches: Van Westendorp's Price Sensitivity Meter, quick and slightly dishonest in that it depresses the price, asking four questions whose answers *triangulate* an optimal figure — at what price the product is too expensive to consider; at what price it seems expensive but you'd still buy; at what price it's so cheap you'd doubt its quality; and at what price it's a bargain — with the intersection of "too expensive" and "too cheap" yielding an approximate price. Then conjoint analysis, where you build two or three fake products and ask people to choose — fresh milk in red packaging with lactose at ₽80 against powdered milk in white packaging without lactose at ₽50 — to learn how much freshness, lactose, or packaging colour drives demand; the survey is hard to design and lives in Qualtrics or Sawtooth Software (or via IRT/PCA), and in Qualtrics you can use JavaScript to build logic of any complexity. There's asking people to rank products by priority and probing the range they'd pay, a very ambiguous method where the product line and competitors can badly skew the answer, and being quantitative, even the median can mislead. There's simply calling people to interview and asking what stops them buying right now, why they pay a Netflix subscription, whether the price is dearer or cheaper than expected. And there's TURF analysis. To fold in a competitor's *price change* specifically, BPTO — the Brand Price Trade-Off, a price ladder across several products and a step before conjoint — shows a shelf with prices, asks which you'd choose, then re-offers the set with the chosen item up 10 percent, and raises another item's price the moment you switch; it's old and not very reliable, and the alternative is a price ladder built on the competitors. And to measure brand *perception* through emotion and image there are the projective methods above all, Brand Health Tracking, standardised residuals, the semantic differential, that same conjoint, and correspondence analysis, which shows a brand's attributes vividly.

The remote-testing platforms are worth a map of their own, since you'll reach for one constantly: at the simple, near-free end, usabilityhub with its reports, or the even simpler QuickTest; then Whatusersdo and Loop11 (which does true-intent studies); Userlytics, with decent panel reach; usertesting.com, a middling all-rounder, fine for mobile and with UK respondents, alongside the many similar — UserZoom, Usabilia, AYTM, MTurk; Optimal Workshop, quite good, with interview coding (Reframer) and excellent card sorting, whose modern card-sort alternative is the pricier Useberry (open and closed sorts both); Validately for complex remote testing with True Intent Study and built-in A/B, if you have the money for its panel; the wholly serious and expensive Qualtrics, a vast tool with every problem a large old product can have; and even Microsoft Clarity, from which insights can be teased. Whichever you choose, and whatever the domain, the discipline is the one this whole essay has been about: prepare until you can meet the expert as something close to a peer, and only then ask.
