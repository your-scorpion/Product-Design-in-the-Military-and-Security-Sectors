# Notes on Running a Design Department

*Maksim Tcvetkov · Product Design and Security Lead*

## The limits of attention

When dozens or hundreds of people report to you, you run headlong into a hard ceiling: the amount of attention you can spend on any one of them. Dunbar's number describes it — the count of stable social relationships you can hold scales with how often you actually talk to each person. Five or six people make up your inner circle: the leads, the seniors, the principals, your deputies. Another ten to fifteen are the ones you're growing — the people who in a few years might make lead or step up a grade. And then there are the remaining fifty to a hundred whose existence you're aware of, whom you cross paths with now and then, but no more than that.

There are simply too many people, and so a manager is forced to delegate; giving each of a hundred and fifty people your time is physically impossible.

## Delegating

To delegate well you choose the person, hand them a matching zone of responsibility, decide on the direction of their growth, and make sure they have the skills for it. Then comes the part most people forget: the person's capacity. If someone takes on extra work while already running at a hundred percent, something has to come off their plate. Check, too, that they have the access and the contacts they'll need — the lines to the decision-makers. For the first important meeting you can turn up alongside your designer and introduce them as the design department's point of contact for that particular team. In that meeting the designer can commit — agree to and promise the goals you've set. After that it is only planning and control, calibrated to the person's grade, plus an agreement on the artefact you expect at the end: the Definition of Done (DoD), the criteria under which the task counts as successfully finished. And eNPS, as a read on how satisfied the team is.

## Roles and personalities

What reports to you, strictly speaking, is not a human being but a role — a position, a slot. This is the formal subordination of one box to another, whether the reporting line is functional or direct, and it runs entirely along the skills and the job description of a specific designer. But wherever there is formal subordination there is also informal subordination. Take an ordinary middle designer, a perfectly machined cog, the ideal occupant of a role. In IT, though, the person inside the role matters — the one who cares about the outcome, who structures the work, who is tormented by an imperfect result, who keeps knocking on closed doors. You need boundaries here; you cannot dissolve the line between roles and personalities entirely. The org chart and the human chart of a team will never coincide, but they should complete each other. Need an expert from the trenches? That's the middle designer above, who happens to be the lead for iOS e-commerce. Need someone to wield formal authority and simply make the call? That's the lead designer.

In a system like this the team inevitably grows through the relationships between its people. One person might act as a coach — someone who asks questions, and the higher the position, the trickier and more high-level the questions become. Another might be a mentor — someone who gives advice. A mentor is always a playing captain, with energy in the voice and genuine interest and expertise in the subject; to be a good mentor you have to be interested in people. One measure of whether time with a mentor or a coach paid off is confidence: a lead can talk something through with a junior and come away surer of a decision — or at least step, for a while, into the role of someone still searching for the answer. Yes, that happens too. My own invention: in important meetings the juniors give their opinion first and the most senior people speak last.

## Risk management

Even if you have tuned every production process, even if your pipeline is predictable, even if your team clears a high bar for quality and its experience and ability outrun expectations — none of that cancels the risk that the client won't sign off on the mockup in time, or won't hand over the information and content you need. If your key person is out — sick, on holiday, gone — and cannot be replaced, that is a hole in the revenue on that particular engagement. And if you hand the whole team to an unqualified manager on the client's side, the probability of a small furry creature turning up on the project — the polite Russian stand-in for a catastrophe — is somewhat north of a hundred percent. This is exactly the sort of thing that deserves your personal time rather than being palmed off on your deputies. It is all part of risk management, and a manager's career is always on the line for failing to deliver. Every risk has a probability of occurring, and every risk has consequences.

## Letting people go

Deputies can handle dismissals after a few unsuccessful diagnostic sessions. If a person isn't especially valuable and keeps making a mess, that is dismissal under Article 81 of the Russian Labour Code. You cannot fire someone on the strength of verbal complaints; you have to document the violations. Leaving a laptop unlocked and wandering off for coffee is already a documentable violation — if the company maintains a proper NDA regime, that sets a precedent. But moments like these have to be spelled out in advance, in the employment contract or an addendum. You cannot spin up a DLP system to watch an employee and then use it as your evidence base — they will simply go to the labour inspectorate. First you legitimise the DLP system, you write into the contract that performance of duties will be monitored through DLP, and only then do you dismiss for moonlighting, for misusing company resources, and so on. You cannot fire someone for talking to their family during working hours — that is their private life, protected by the constitution. It is far easier to part ways over the regular, measurable things: a missed KPI plan-versus-actual, feature delivery time, the number of design bugs reaching production, the time it takes to test a hypothesis, which should be measured in hours, not months. But the employee can always call in the labour inspectorate, so tread carefully.

The dismissal itself sits on the same grade ladder as everything else. A lead or a C-level figure works out that, with profit down, it's time to cut the department by thirty percent. A senior decides who to let go, whose bonuses to cancel, and which activities to wind down to ride out the turbulence while keeping the core of the team intact for when — if — things improve. A middle shapes the communication around it and refuses to feed people's negativity. A junior writes a plaintive post online about having been fired.

Remember that letting someone go is a negotiation, and every negotiation has an offer on the table: you can offer help finding the next job, and references. And when someone leaves of their own accord, run an exit interview without fail. Its real jobs are to get the most honest feedback you can about the state of the department and the company — and to make a valuable person understand that the door is open for them to come back.

## Regular management, and the shift to an agency

Once the headcount climbs, delegation alone won't carry you — you also need regular management. The moment the team hits fifteen to twenty people is, to my mind, the perfect time for my favourite shift: away from funnels and random leads and a sales department, away from unpredictability, regional clients, tiny invoices, shallow and short-lived relationships, no industry focus, a murky stack, childish communication and fixed-price work — and toward proper clients, proper budgets, proper working modes, units and the managers who run them, an economy you can forecast and steer, and the genuine possibility of building an agency business. There is a catch, though: large enterprise clients usually mean fixed price. And fixed price has to be high, because it is the price of a result, not of a process.

You will also need regular reflection — holding in your mind a live picture of what a person is actually doing. It is the opposite of abstract thinking. Because reflection is unavoidably built on our own life and professional experience, there is a real danger of a particular trap: turning the past into the future and the future into the past. Put more plainly — expecting our past experience to predict what comes next, which is always a mistake. If fifteen years ago you managed to convince the C-level that the company needed a mobile app, there is no guarantee the person you have chosen will pull off the same feat. Not because they are any worse than you were fifteen years ago, but because the C-level's knowledge, the external economic conditions, the resources available on the market, the goals of the company and of the state itself are nothing like what they were back then.

## The vocabulary of grades

A few words that will keep recurring:

- **Grades** — junior, middle, senior, and the notional 80k, 150k and 300k attached to them.
- **Rates** — pay by the hour.
- **Outstaff** — handing a person over to the client's side, most likely a middle who has plateaued.
- **Outsource** — a person does the work for your client under a contract, with a guaranteed result. Demands very strong management.
- **Team lead versus tech lead** — the team lead is more about the business, the tech lead more about the technology.

## How people grow

The signs that a junior is turning into a middle: productivity climbs; they become self-directed; they own their mistakes without pulling a face; they communicate with the team and share what they know; they understand the business; they automate their own work; and they no longer need things explained to them classroom-style. A middle solves problems on their own, with only a little help. A middle is the grade people talk about least — they are simply there, in the company, getting on with it.

The signs a middle is turning into a senior: they go deeper into the technology, or show some other sign of specialising. They take an OKR for the quarter and, at the end of it, bring back a result. If a middle solves tasks, a senior solves problems. They start operating under a high level of uncertainty. Declaratively, a senior is whoever was hired into a senior slot; in practice, a senior is whoever helps the business grow rather than merely working through tasks. A senior takes a problem and resolves it instead of creating it, keeping the communication between teams in view the whole time. Think of the Wolf in *Pulp Fiction* — the fixer who arrives and makes the problem disappear. A senior can properly solve problems that have no obvious solution, and do it without resorting to command-and-control. They turn a complex environment into routine — and then, rather than putting up with the routine, which is just procrastination, they automate it. And they only start on a task once they have actually talked to the people involved.

The signs a middle is turning into a team lead: they take part in the company's processes and read its politics and its market context; they have the soft skills; they know exactly who to go to for any given problem. They understand andragogy — how adults learn. They begin to grasp why the Big Four consultants command enormous fees while someone with the same knowledge and skills inside a company earns the market average, because what you are paid depends on whose problems you have been set to solve and how big your mandate is. A team lead starts out as an acting team lead; the team grows around them; they steep in the culture.

The signs a senior is turning into a tech lead: they can move fluidly between the Hersey–Blanchard leadership styles — directing, coaching, supporting, delegating. They understand when a subordinate needs advice and when they need support. And they simply understand that the business expects leadership, results and product sense from them. Half of a product's success is the leader's belief in the product.

## What a manager actually does

You, as the manager, run one-to-ones and Performance Reviews. I also run the occasional dump-question session, where every person on the team has to bring one or two "stupid" questions about how we work, and the team talks them through. Another device I like is a User Day, when everyone on the team gets to talk to real users, informally. In any one-to-one it matters that you work with emotion: I-statements, mirroring, and naming and acknowledging what the other person feels.

You are answerable for the work, the budget and the deadlines. In more detail, the job tends to include planning resources to get the work done — systematically and for the long term; controlling deadlines and quality; setting up the project's infrastructure; watching the budget and the profitability of the work; accepting completed work; keeping documentation current; communicating with the client, both the business and the technical departments; escalating problems; framing tasks precisely for the people who will do them; and, in general, taking responsibility for the hard things.

## The competency matrix

Take a single skill — **creating design assets** — and you can watch the expectation shift as you climb the grades.

- **Middle:** iterative, predictable work to produce and hand over assets of adequate quality. Predictable means delivered on time. Also asks to buy a lot of software.
- **Senior:** turns out assets quickly, with a strong grasp of the features they are for, which helps the whole team move faster; the team's source of truth on best practice.
- **Lead:** the first port of call when a problem is genuinely hard, tangled or confusing; takes on the work that stumps less experienced designers; mentors the juniors.
- **Design Director:** does not make assets at all, but works with the team to define best practice and develop everyone so the department as a whole runs as effectively as possible. Knows their leads better than the leads know themselves. And when a middle asks for the shiny new tool, this is the person working out what it will cost and how to justify it to management.
- **Design Executive:** about building the culture and evangelising UX/UI.

**Technical knowledge:**

- **Middle:** has enough command of the tools to work quickly on standard tasks.
- **Senior:** expert knowledge of the tools and the methods; the colleagues' go-to on technical questions; often levels up without being told to.
- **Lead:** an expert in their area; talks to other departments about the hard problems; picks up new skills constantly.
- **Design Director:** a deep understanding of the technical side of design, though not necessarily still hands-on; owns effective communication between departments.
- **Design Executive:** understands design deeply enough to state the design department's position clearly and concisely, in line with where the company is heading.

**Decision-making:**

- **Middle:** decides by consensus and waits for sign-off from more senior colleagues.
- **Senior:** a seasoned decision-maker who can carve out the time to gather information from a range of sources and then decide.
- **Lead:** decides with confidence and deliberation; the person less experienced designers come to when they are stuck; can assemble the information for a well-founded call themselves.
- **Design Director:** makes high-level decisions that ripple across cross-functional teams; is good at diagnosing departments and processes; is seen across the whole organisation as a high performer and a decision-maker who unblocks others — decisions that let everyone else work better.
- **Design Executive:** makes the big strategic calls that move the company's bottom line; grants authority to others; shapes the culture of how decisions get made.

**Industry understanding:**

- **Middle:** a rough, general read on industry trends, and leans on intuition a lot when designing.
- **Senior:** well-versed in the trends and the standards, and puts that knowledge to work in the design.
- **Lead:** a high-level expert in design within their specialism; can articulate best practice for the team and help justify the product's design choices.
- **Design Director:** deep expertise in their own area, plus broad experience and understanding across other areas of design.
- **Design Executive:** an industry expert with a proven track record, widely respected in the field.

**Problem-solving:**

- **Middle:** understands the problems management hands over and can solve them, if not always elegantly; needs courses on the technical craft.
- **Senior:** strong at this — knows how to isolate a problem in its context, solve it, and get to the goal, working effectively with the other stakeholders along the way; needs courses on the organisational side and on building culture.
- **Lead:** frames problems cleanly for the team and pulls the team together to crack the big ones; communicates cross-functionally; always solves in the user's context while making sure stakeholder goals are respected too.
- **Design Director:** an extraordinarily effective problem-solver who holds both stakeholder and user needs in view, builds best practice and a positive, user-centred problem-solving culture, and unites design teams and other disciplines around the solutions.
- **Design Executive:** spots and simplifies complex, high-level problems, and creates alignment around the direction of design.

## Presenting, and pitching to the room

The same ladder shows up in how people present. A junior is expected, at the very least, to open with the context and the goal and to explain their decision with data. A middle already organises their own meetings and can hold a professional line under pressure. A senior can stand up in front of a large audience. And a lead is an accomplished facilitator — unfazed by running a workshop for twenty billionaires, trusted deeply by their manager, and clear-eyed about exactly where and how the company makes its money. From senior upward, a designer has to be able to sell — to the team, to clients, to colleagues. At partner level, no other skill matters much beside the ability to sell.

Which is why it is worth being precise about how you present to a room of senior people. The more sophisticated the audience, the less time you will be given. A pitch is a story: you tell it, you do not flip through slides reciting your ideas. The narrative has to hold together, and you are speaking for the sake of the meaning, not to read text aloud. The motivation has to come from you — you need to make your dream real, and that is where the meaning of the talk comes from. The structure I use runs: audience, then the problem, then the size of the problem and the revenue it represents, then the solution, then how you make money, then what you have already achieved, then unit economics, then the team, then competitors, then what you are asking for, and finally the unfair advantage or the one memorable fact. This is not an elevator pitch. In my experience a talk like this lasts three to seven minutes; that is ten to fourteen slides, one slide to one key thought.

The first and last slides carry disproportionate weight. Spend real effort on the title slide — people stare at it for a long time — with three or four words about the product, your name, and something that looks good. And mind the final slide, because it hangs behind you the whole time you are taking questions: put your contacts on it, and whatever it is you want people to remember about you. Double-check that you have included every impressive number, shown that you understand the audience, proven the idea works in other regional markets, and made the experience of the team and the founder visible, along with real sales and active clients.

If the deck is going to be sent rather than presented, it has to make sense without a narrator. A few ideas worth landing: that your product could become a billion-dollar company — which means annual turnover of three to five hundred million dollars, and that is only realistic in a market fifteen to twenty times larger, almost certainly a global one. In that case you are a venture-scale startup, where unit economics matter less than fast growth. If you are not that kind of startup, aim for dividends instead; the deck can show that you understand your unit economics and that you have a strong team — people who can sell, people who can build and grow the product, people who can study users. Show that the company can scale into other segments and regions. Unfair advantages — patents? The novelty of the technology you are using. Explain the co-founders' roles, and why you will not fall out when things get hard. At a later stage you can show the product itself, your growth rate, recognition from the industry press. But never — never — put video in the deck. PDF is the format.

## The finer grades, and turning research into a solution

Inside every grade there is a finer division. A junior-minus is yesterday's intern or student, with some sense of the domain — and probably still under the impression that money comes out of a cash machine. A plain junior is a specialist who is supremely, and quite unjustifiably, confident; this is the level freelancers so often get stuck at. A junior-plus is the one who has grasped the full depth and scope of the craft they have chosen, and is ready for the long, multi-year apprenticeship, mentors and all.

On turning research into a solution, the expectations climb the same way. A junior simply follows the department's rules and helps with the research. Wants to make middle? Then they learn to back their conclusions with quantitative data, to prepare the supporting material, to organise and run the discussions where problems get thrashed out, to set up sessions that answer the *what* and the *how* of what needs to come out of them. A senior, by contrast, will not let the team go until they have reached a result — or at least an interim agreement — and will see the change through into the product and propose new features besides. A senior tells optimism apart from overconfidence, where overconfidence is a wrong reading of one's own skills and knowledge that only harms the department, paired with a lack of flexibility. They prepare for a negotiation with the other side's point of view in mind, and they do not openly force their own view or resort to manipulation — though a decoy demand is a perfectly legitimate instrument. The department's goals always come first.

A lead runs the hackathons and the workshops, chairs every new meeting a little differently, and each time walks away with a "meaningful" slice of the pie through sheer patience and composure, even where empathy is missing. They hold the balance between "I'll get my way and to hell with everyone else" and "I'll get my way by finding the right answer for everyone." They negotiate from interests, not positions: do not rush to satisfy a middle's demand for a five-hundred-dollar raise — find out what the raise is actually for. If a raise looks likely to turn into a conflict, the lead switches into moderator mode rather than taking the company's side or the employee's. They complete the communication task a hundred percent of the time. And they never, ever let the design unit's zone of responsibility — its influence — shrink, because they know that responsibility is freedom. A lead does all of this without ever stopping to ask, *am I doing this right?* — because heightened self-assurance, combined with flexibility and a great deal of experience, tells them that they are.

A lead knows how to listen. Listening means listening — not parrying, not needling, not interrupting, not criticising, not scrolling on your phone, not jumping in with a solution. Listening is about understanding the other person. It is not about lining up your counter-arguments; it is about the follow-up question.

## Leads don't get sad

Leads do not get sad. Except that the statistics say leads burn out more than anyone, so let's untangle that. Sadness and resilience to the world's blows are, in a sense, the same thing. Resilience is growth through the ability to adapt; sadness is adaptation to loss and failure. The loss might be small — a tooth, a memory — or it might be enormous, like losing someone you love. In that case comes denial and shock, and it shows up in the body: the person stops eating, their chest aches, they cannot sleep. As a manager you cannot simply hand them time and wait it out. Sadness does not leave; it stays with us for good and reshapes who we are. For it to loosen its grip, the loss has to be replaced by something — and this is where our role as manager comes in. Help the person find that replacement in their life sooner: give them the time, the resources and the room to find a new source of joy. Make sure they do not wreck their body, their mind or their spirit, because if you do not, they will simply leave — the company, or the profession. You can help them lose themselves in something: creativity, dancing, being held, music, knitting, nature, animals, theatre, laughter, charity, exercise — any process that allows for catharsis. Shopping, even. Make sure they are watching classic films or some other reliably positive thing rather than the news. And do not shy away from medical support for depression, either.

That is how resilience is built — the capacity not to slide into depression, to form strong social bonds, to keep yourself genuinely healthy. Resilience is precisely the ability to believe in yourself and your competence, to tell the right decisions from the wrong ones, to believe you can actually change a situation.

## Soft skills, values, and emotions

Soft skills are hard to organise into neat, individual competencies. Fortunately there is Seth Godin's soft-skills matrix, from which I have pulled the points that matter to me. First and foremost come self-control and steadiness in a difficult moment. Then the ability to climb out of failures, adaptability to shifting requirements, a willingness to take risks, ethics even when no one is watching, the knack for finding ways to collaborate, and emotional intelligence. After that comes wisdom: mentorship, diplomacy, critical thinking, conflict resolution — and this is where you start to feel the passage from middle to senior or lead, from apprentice to master. They can defuse a conflict. They have learned not to project their own experience onto others, and not to seed hidden conflicts. Then influence: managing talent, building social bonds, giving feedback without ego, weighting positive feedback for creative people at five parts good to one part bad, assertiveness, and asking for feedback on your feedback. Then productivity: running meetings well, courage and an entrepreneurial cast of mind, delegating to raise output. And finally perception — an instinct for where the trends are going.

Values are easier to structure. **Attitude** is the mindset, the point of view, the way a person approaches a given situation or goal. **Motivation** is the internal drive, the enthusiasm that moves people to act and to work toward the goals in front of them. **Commitment** is the dedication and the staying power to see duties and promises through — holding focus, being reliable, delivering on what you said you would do despite whatever obstacles show up.

Emotional intelligence is just as structured, and for that we can thank Daniel Goleman, whose model is usually paired with Robert Plutchik's wheel of emotions. If handling an employee's emotions costs more effort and time than the employee is worth, questions about the partnership start to arise. Beyond that, the monthly digests should reflect people's output in proportion — little from a junior, a lot from a lead, at the same level of stress, involvement and effort. Regular calls to review what has been achieved are good practice too.

Underneath all of this sits a simpler set of questions about whether you are, in fact, a creative leader. Do your colleagues and your team respect you — do they, perhaps, even love you? Do they sometimes hold you up as an example? Do you champion values like respect and empathy at work? Do you feel a deep pride when someone you employ, or someone you have helped, hits a real milestone in their career? And do you expect new hires to arrive already carrying a vision of their own that they can bring to your projects — subordinating it to yours, of course, when the two collide?

## Motivation

Which brings us to motivation, because raising it is often the first thing a new design manager is handed. Motivation is always a reward on the horizon. The reward might be public praise, or it might be an annual bonus large enough to put toward a house.

Of the non-material treats, the most valuable is the sense of one's own significance. Every person should become an expert in something, and that unique competence should be visible in the skills matrix. People need regular chances to flex those muscles — to present, to share an expert opinion, to win an argument. Public praise and peer-to-peer recognition are an important part of the culture too, and they have to happen systematically, not now and then.

The second lever is to show the meaning of the work. We are not just closing tasks; we understand how they connect to the company's larger goals and to our own values. Everyone on the team should see the risks attached to their work and know how to manage them, which is why designers always present their solutions and always have an answer to *why?* — why this feature, this banner, this visual exists; why the button is on the left; why there are five filters. And when someone complains, your job is to remove the problem where you can. That builds a sense of belonging and deepens engagement — if "unhelpful" analysts are getting in the designers' way, for instance, then the fix is to build a business process that works. And, of course, every study says the same thing: the core motivators are money and comfort. So the reward structure deserves real thought — either a high fixed salary with bonuses tied to concrete results, or a standard rate plus a variable slice that has to be earned.

## How closely to steer

How closely you steer the team depends on how good the team is, and the situational-leadership model captures this well: directing, then coaching, then supporting, then delegating.

When the team is highly skilled, management is mostly support and delegation. An autonomous, competent team runs on what I think of as the chat approach — calm and flexible. On Fridays we hold a call to agree the scope for the coming week; afterwards each person writes their freshly agreed tasks into the chat. On Monday I repeat the list back in a message, adding my expectations for each item. On Wednesday the team posts a sync note, or shows progress on a shared call. My control — or the leads' — extends only to the key tasks.

When the team is less self-sufficient, when confidence and appetite for the work are sagging, I move to something more structured. Now there are daily morning syncs, where each person says what they did yesterday, what they are on today, and what is blocking them. No task is allowed to run longer than eight hours; anything bigger gets broken down, and every task's completion is checked by me personally, or by the lead if one is assigned. We add designer syncs twice a week, where the designers show what they are doing and what they have done. If there are up to a dozen designers I still run the occasional one-to-one; past that, I appoint leads and delegate part of the management to them, keeping the critical processes, the business-orientation of decisions, and fidelity to the design vision for myself.

The least desirable mode of all is micromanagement. If the bleakest hypotheses turn out to be true — the team cannot and will not work, there is a deliberate incompetence, there is no motivation and no communication inside the department, and people feel open revulsion toward the work — then it is time for crisis management, and we cross over from managing to controlling. To soften how that lands, I call it "high involvement in the process." Every task enters and leaves the department only through my hands. I, or the leads, give precise instructions on how a task is to be done. People get daily feedback on every task, and for the critical, urgent ones we sync several times a day. I check regularly that people are actually at their desks, if they are remote. And I am invited to every meeting that touches process, and copied on every email. People will be unhappy, and I will be temporarily buried deeper in the operations than in the strategy — but it is a transitional stage on the way to a more mature, self-directed, disciplined design department.

After the big releases: a post-mortem, or a retro.

## OKR

Every manager needs arguments to defend their budgets and resources with the bosses above them, and for that you need regular, sizeable wins — and their effect — rather than the mere completion of routine. This is where OKRs help: Objectives and Key Results, a method used in modern management to run projects, to synchronise team and individual goals, and to keep effective control over delivery.

Planning goes: goals, then metrics, then projects, then resources, with the goals and metrics tied to the OKR. A metric is "lose five kilos"; the goal is "look good on the beach." "Make the company five times the money" is a metric — but toward what? So the owner can buy a yacht and the factory can have new equipment by year's end — that is the goal. Each metric gets a target: how much growth, by when. Targets get revised; the metrics stay put for the long haul.

OKRs are planned by quarter and by year. There is an inspiring objective, and beneath it the key results that should get you there. Every employee, whatever their title or duties, should be able to tell you what the project's goals are. Whether it is a quarter spent growing a metric or a year mapped out in advance, it all comes down to answering one question: what milestone do we want to clear in the time we have got? Ideally people reach their personal goals through the company's goals — the leanest way there is to motivate anyone.

The bigger the team, the more it splits into grades and the greater the distance between the manager and the entry-level roles. People become harder to swap in and out, and a great deal of energy goes into keeping everyone in sync. Even inside a single design department you can get a chain where a designer is blocked by the design-system designer, who is blocked by the brand team, who is blocked by an outside agency, who are blocked by the accountants. The OKR is where you write down that you depend on another team's output — and where that other team writes helping you into their own OKR. Or they write that they doubt your result really depends on theirs, and the endless meetings begin. And do not confuse grades, levels, titles and roles.

## Cynefin

By now a few things are clear. The higher your position, the more manager and the less product you become. If you want a predictable result on a regular basis, you will have to bring in regular management. That management works in any environment, but it takes different forms and has to be adapted. And working out which environment you are in is where the Cynefin framework comes in.

Cynefin is a management concept that helps you make decisions according to the conditions of the environment your system sits in. In regular management there is no room for heroics — no all-nighter to build the deck the evening before the conference, none of that romance — because we are aiming for a predictable environment. Cynefin helps you work out which environment you are in by way of proven cause-and-effect links. It is built from three base systems — ordered, complex and chaotic — with disorder and simple added on. It does a very good job of clearing the mind.

![The Cynefin framework](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Artboard.png)

A **chaotic environment** is a genuinely rare thing: there is no time for proper work and you are shipping something, anything, to production. You act, you get results, you react. Plenty of people are sure they live in chaos, because everyone is running a "flexible" Agile — but the truth is that a chaotic environment means innovative startups. Building a messenger for the government and unable to plan a thing? That is not chaos; your processes just are not set up. In chaos nothing is understood, so you do something and react — you act first and only afterwards work out what you did. The start of the pandemic, say. There are no constraints, you need bold experiments, you reach for any practice at all and impose no limits.

It helps here to keep the different levels of the company's global work apart. The strategic level is about where we want to end up and what it will take. The conceptual layer is about thinking through a new product or a significant piece of functionality. The third level is the execution itself.

In a chaotic environment one mindset earns its keep: dividing people not by department (Vasya on project one, Masya on project two) but by project, so that both Vasya and Masya get thrown between projects, can work on the department's own internal projects, and are regularly pulled off to put out fires.

In a **simple environment** you punish mistakes. It is predictable and low-risk, and easy to analyse with a Shewhart chart. Stray from the instructions and it is a screw-up, so you get a fine, and that is fine — it is not the error of an experiment, it is plain carelessness: the person knew how it should be done and did not do it. Here everything runs to regulation, a documented order of operations with stages, owners, deadlines and control points. In big companies whole departments write the regulations; in product development it is the development process, the rules for technical review, the process for releasing and rolling back and returning to re-dev. A regulation for storing everything you learn from every study, say, along with who owns what — where, if a product manager runs a study, the researcher still owns the result. For a regulation to work it has to be part of onboarding, written in plain language, and kept somewhere visible.

Mistakes come in kinds. I sort them into three: mistakes of missing skill, mistakes of wrong decisions, and mistakes of interpretation. A switch with its on and off states inverted, a failure to follow the regulations, weak aesthetics, over-complicated components — skill. The wrong behaviour in an unusual situation, the wrong reaction to an emergency, overstepping your authority — wrong decisions. A bad estimate of how long something will take — interpretation. All of it can be fixed, and none of it depends on who the person is. But some things cannot be tolerated: ignoring a safety briefing, aggression, deliberate sabotage, unsanctioned contact with the client. In a flat structure it is perfectly normal to call a meeting with your manager's manager; otherwise, that is a very serious breach.

It sounds uncreative and dull, but there is an upside. In a simple environment unpaid overtime is off the table — things are just as predictable for the managers — and management debt is not forgiven either. Simple, or Clear: we know one plain, obvious path from A to B. A routine problem in a bank branch does not need Agile; a dentist who fixed teeth by Agile rather than by a clear, proven procedure would be a bad dentist. It is ideal for operational teams — nothing but best practices and hard constraints.

A **changeable, tangled environment** needs regular management even more than a predictable, simple one does. It gives creative thinking room to breathe, because it makes time and space for experiments. By regular management I mean a system with documented processes, responsibilities distributed and written down, resource planning, a clear set of leading metrics, predictable results and — just as important — predictable effort. In chaotic and tangled environments you punish the breaking of a commitment, or any breach of trust. Financial fines are very rarely used; they are the last resort, because a fine is the manager signing their name to their own helplessness (in complex IT, at least). In a tangled environment it is normal to discuss up front how big an error is acceptable. If people know fines are a thing, they will work to dodge the fine rather than to grow as specialists. As a punishment you can trim the perks — sick leave only through a formal process, for instance. The non-material punishments are shrinking someone's responsibility, cutting the interesting work and piling on the routine, dropping a grade, negative feedback with none of the positive, tighter control, less attention, exclusion from decisions. And if the number of people failing their role and needing punishment keeps growing, you will have to switch on crisis management with its micromanagement — but that is the format for black swans and shrunken budgets.

**Complex:** there is a starting point A and several possible points B. This is the home of Agile and experiments — you experiment, look at the result, and react, working out the constraints and how they push the product forward. On my own team I introduced a monthly "our screw-ups" meeting, where people share their strangest, most unexpected mistake and I pick the best one, and it counts toward the OKR. It builds the right culture around getting things wrong.

**Complicated:** you build the plan on a belief in the idea, you move toward the idea you want, but you still have to experiment — and react. It is a complex but ordered environment, and the simplest example is software development. This is Kanban. It is rarely clear at the outset what to do at the level of detailed tasks, but because the team knows how to build software, it will: you make a minimal product, the market reacts, you gather the analytics and think. Those are guiding constraints. It calls for very qualified, experienced people who know the good practices. OKRs and a Gantt chart are the main tools, along with the Deming cycle — Plan, Do, Check, Act — the one that delivered the Japanese miracle. On the Gantt chart, different roles get different information: the timeline can be detailed by day or by month, you can layer in the comments from the important meetings, and every task has an owner.

Environments tend to shift. Every industry begins in chaos, then becomes complex but comprehensible — probe, results, react. Over time it improves and the operating model moves to sense, analyse, react. The end state is a world where everything is clear: sense, categorise, react. It is worth remembering that you should not expect instant change in inertial systems like departments and companies — there is always a lag before the result shows, and sometimes that lag is measured in years.

There is no universal department structure that fits every environment. But something close to optimal looks like this: each person is tied to a set of projects, usually two or three, and the load across designers will *not* be equal. Some are overloaded, some under — and that is not a reason to switch to a model where every designer works on every project. The ones with slack pick up the department's own work: building and giving the presentations about results, writing up cases, learning new technology, going to conferences.

From the manager's chair it is easier to hand any task to any designer — easier, too, to defend the department's budget that way. People underloaded? Do ambitious concepts and research. Performance review coming? When every designer has worked across a dozen different products, scraping together the KPIs and OKRs is that much simpler.

For covering a designer during a departure or a holiday, it matters that every designer has a picture of their colleagues' projects — and it is the colleagues who need that knowledge, not the leads. Leads should not be reordering the columns in a mockup while the designer is away. The cross-pollination happens through design review: it is the designer's peers who check the work at review, and the leads are brought in only for the key tasks where the cost of a mistake is high. The culture has to be open enough that people are not afraid to ask for feedback and respect one another as professionals. A lead takes part in design review, accepts work, knows the state of production — but a lead's real strength is the ability to become the pivotal part of any conversation while leaving nothing but good feeling behind. Why spend a talent like that handing out tasks?

A lead is answerable for the hardest, longest, most cross-team work: pushing new processes through, stopping the marketing department from meddling with the product's design, dragging in the new version of the design system — not assigning and accepting every little task. Talking to the business about the things that matter is a lead's job; talking to a project manager about updating a colour on an old mockup is not. Unless, of course, you want micromanagement and total control over every task — in which case the leads become the bottleneck, checking every hour of work and every task that moves.

## The budget

There are various ways to fund a design department, but whichever you choose, you count money, not hours. Broadly there are two kinds of budgeting: fixed price, or time-and-materials. Fixed price usually favours the supplier, because the risks are priced in — and the risks might never materialise.

Say the budget is fifty million across thirty designers — roughly 1,600,000 per person per year, or 125k a month, or 744 ₽ an hour. The salary maths is simple enough. Personal income tax (NDFL) is currently 13%. The social funds — Pension, Social Insurance, Mandatory Medical Insurance — come to 7.8% for IT companies. So it is salary + (salary × 0.13 ÷ 0.87) + (salary + tax) × 0.078: for example, 200,000 + 29,885 + 17,929 = 247,814. Now add a dose of realism — 247,814 ÷ 135 (the monthly output once you strip out holidays, days off, weekends and sick leave) = 1,835 — then pile the smoothie budget on top and admit that you are a grown-up company now.

More simply: there are about 247 working days in a year. Take out holidays and illness — call it 210 — divide by twelve months to get 17.5, times eight hours a day, and you have 140 hours a month. Apply an idle-time coefficient of 0.6 and you are down to 84. In other words, on the agency model you will be paid for 84 hours a month while having to pay a salary as though for 144. If someone earns 400,000 ₽, and we assume 500,000 ₽ gross, then 500,000 ÷ 84 = 6,000 ₽; add the indirect costs and you land at 10,000 ₽. The quick sanity check: multiply the salary by three.

A real startup is not about smoothies — there is no money. If the client pays you in dollars, your people should be paid in dollars too; otherwise a swing in the exchange rate can ruin you and leave you unable to meet payroll. Another popular way to open a cash gap: you take a project and its advance, but your people are on deferred pay. The projects get more complex and no new ones appear, so sooner or later a project drags on — while salaries still have to go out, reliably, once a month. A plain example: a turnover of 200,000,000 rubles might yield all of 1,500,000 in profit, and an ordinary twelve-person design team with a payroll fund of 1,700,000 — 2,300,000 once you add the taxes — no longer pays for itself, and there is simply nowhere to raise salaries from. Financially, all that is left is to hand out options. If no new project turned up to cover payroll out of a fresh advance, a lot of people's lives are about to get worse. And if you are inside a corporation, raising a salary means going to talk to C&B.

Salaries go up on reputation — not on blackmail with an offer from another company, or complaints about a mortgage. There is a nuance: an offer that came out of active headhunting is one story; an employee who went looking for any offer at all to use as leverage is another. But even when someone is paid below market and genuinely matters, the conversation about lifting them to the offer has to be built on their achievements. If someone complains that a colleague in the same role earns more, that is often a breach of the employment contract, and someone should be fired or fined. Nor should you hire people in at wild, well-above-market salaries; moving from an old company to a new one, the pay should not jump much, or it is no longer clear whether the new hire is here for the interesting work or only for the money.

You should be shedding fixed-price work in favour of T&M, energetically, by the time you hit fifteen to twenty people, or the department — the studio — simply cannot grow. I tend to take people without a narrow industry specialism, which keeps me clear of cookie-cutter solutions — and I apply a discount for the fact that the agency will sell you the best version of its people while the ones who actually work will be middling, at best: people you will have to train and spend time on, people you will need to prop up with one of your own seniors, which neither the senior nor the agency wants. And if you work for a large company, bringing in outstaff for the critical tasks is an enormous financial risk.

## Raising money from outside

All of which raises the question of money from outside. The first thing to settle is whether you need investment at all — and for building an MVP, mostly, you do not. A proper MVP should not cost more than about three thousand dollars or take longer than two weeks; empirically, an MVP should cost about a month's salary. If the launch makes it clear there is something worth scaling, then you go for investment — if it is genuinely necessary. The most expensive thing a founder owns is their undiluted stake in the company. Investment is for the full version of the product, for getting the unit economics to converge, for entering new markets and widening the client segments. That is a seed round — around eight hundred thousand dollars in the US, three hundred thousand in Russia. If the seed round does not come, then it is bootstrapping: friends, family and fools, and your own money.

Remember that an investor's money is very expensive money — far dearer than a bank loan — up to and including the loss of control of your startup. Ideally you raise when you do not need it, when investors are the ones knocking on your door, because that is when the money comes cheap. Work out how much you need, who can give it, and what you will say across the table. Find a list of startup questions online and answer them, short and dense — no backstory, no musing, no conclusions; the answer first, the explanation after, if one is needed. Then you send your application to the top accelerators — a description, a deck, a short video. They will most likely ask: how strong and experienced is the team; do you understand the dynamics of your TAM and SAM; are there first payments; is there a ready go-to-market strategy; is there a market for your product, and how much room to grow; have you worked through the business model and the exit plans; do you have customers yet, and do you understand how to win them and where the money is; what is the unique selling point; is there a prototype or a working product; and do you know how to set yourself apart from the competition? On competitors, the answer has to be "yes, there are competitors, and here they are" — otherwise you will not get the money. They may also promise investment and, in the end, hand over nothing.

For the pre-seed stage a couple of pages on the team, the company and the market will do; larger sums need more documents. A self-contained deck is fine too. You will want the CVs of the key team members — the team is the main asset — a financial plan, and your product-development and operational plans: where, how, the strategies. And keep in mind that the mere existence of software will not sell it; you need the value of that software, something like unique AI models. What you end up with is a data room: every document an investor needs, plus a table to navigate it.

## Hiring

Which is a good moment to face a question every manager runs into sooner or later: when you need a design lead, do you promote from within or hire from outside? Here is how I reason about it. With someone already on the team you know the salary band and, most likely, their expectations. The old hands tend to know the processes, have built relationships across the company, are a known quantity to work with, and have proven themselves — not always for the better, of course. And such a person will be more loyal to the manager who promoted them. But the word "old hand" carries the key drawback inside it: nothing new arrives, the processes do not change, it stays a buddy-buddy enterprise running on rails, and the culture stays exactly as it was. You will have to train the person up to lead level and teach them that they are no longer the one badmouthing the bosses in the bar — they are the one the colleagues are discussing in the bar. I usually try to find someone from outside, but it is no universal answer.

Whichever way you go, hiring itself has its instruments. HOGAN is an international, independent personality-assessment system for selecting and developing people — another standardised questionnaire. To surface what drives someone, a Hogan test is often run; understanding a person's lead motives is the key to hiring key specialists. There are spiral-dynamics tests, too. And among the older classics, the good old house–person–tree: the candidate draws a house, a person and a tree, then fills in a special questionnaire — draw the tree first, for instance, and the candidate is about conserving their spiritual energy. Another is the non-existent animal, after which you ask what the animal eats, where it lives, what it is afraid of.

If you want to go a harder route, you can rank your experts by the Hirsch index — the h-index, which nobody likes but everybody uses, built on the number of published papers and how often they are cited. Sound like it fits us? Probably not — so reach instead for the second popular method, PageRank or HITS: the analogue of ranking web pages, but for finding experts.

Some questions worth putting to a candidate:

- What do you need in your next role to feel you chose the right employer? What is non-negotiable for you?
- What is the most valuable piece of feedback you have ever given? And received? What made it so valuable?
- Which of your achievements went unnoticed by your manager?
- Which of the company's metrics have you moved?
- What key points of professional growth do you see for yourself over the next two years?
- How many years of experience do you have? How comprehensive is it? How far can that experience be trusted?
- How many people from your last job still work with you now?
- What are you proudest of to date? It does not have to be work.
- Can you tell us about a time you got constructive feedback, and what you did with it afterwards?
- Can you tell us about a time you gave constructive feedback, and how it was received?
- How do you know when something is "good enough"? How do you spot flaws and put them right?
- You are not going to finish a task by the deadline. What do you do?
- What does success look like in this role, and how do you see it developing over the next one, two, three, five years — depending on how stable the economy is?
- What has been the most stressful thing in your work? Your biggest win, your biggest failure?
- What have you done outside your remit?
- What would you do if you thought someone was acting against the company's interests?

And the questions you should have answers to yourself:

- What is the policy on unpaid leave?
- What has to be achieved during the probation period?
- What do you value most in the company's culture that you would never change?
- Can holiday days be taken in advance, before they have accrued?
- How is pay split between salary and bonus — and is it grey, white or black?
- How is the bonus calculated, and what has it been over the last year?
- Does salary drop during probation?
- Is there a grading system for transparent growth?
- What kind of office is it — open plan, cubicles, separate rooms?
- Which OS do people work on: macOS, Windows, Linux?
- Do you install time-trackers and screen-grabbers?

Rehearsed, off-the-shelf answers in an interview with you as the manager are a warning sign. Asked "what are your weaknesses?", a candidate should answer honestly, not with "I'm too honest" or "I'm a workaholic" — and to head that off, reframe the question into something less standard, like "which skill did you find hardest to develop?" A candidate should not lie; someone who makes things up on the fly is trying to reach the goal by the shortest path, consequences be damned — is that the person you want? And if "why are you leaving your current company?" gets an answer about "not enough help" or "I can't get on with my colleagues," that is a red flag; the answer you are hoping for is about why they want to come to us, as a company and as people, or at least that they see no more room to grow. A candidate who immediately dumps all of their company's problems and runs its people down — especially if they speak badly of its key people — is not a red flag but a black cross.

In any case, we do not just hire the best people on the market. Every stage of a business needs particular specialisations. Are we in second place and trying to unseat the leader? Or do we just need hands to get the operational work done? The point is to hire not simply a specialist but the right person for this stage of the company's and the department's development. Someone used to strict, predictable processes will not fit a chaos that needs a one-man band.

## Judgment Day

At least once a year you run a performance review of your people. You owe them feedback on the level of their work; you might raise a salary, or trim a bonus. The ratings can run: meets none, meets some, meets most, meets all — and, on the brighter end, exceeds expectations, greatly exceeds expectations, redefined expectations. A rating obviously is not the manager's gut feeling; there are criteria. The first and most important is impact on the product — did they move the metrics? Second is the level of technical mastery — did they avoid wrecking the design system or writing unmaintainable code, and have they learned to generate a wealth of arguments in a debate and stay emotionally in it? Third is how well they manage themselves and the team, and how they work through tasks — do people enjoy doing work to your employee's instructions, and does that employee have a good reputation? You can hang all of this on a competency map, a star map, marking the skills a person sharpened or let slip over the year, and in doing so close the department's gaps in competence by growing the competence you have.

The same day is when the IDP — the Individual Development Plan — gets written, ideally with an assessment of the person's social standing in the company by the SCARF model to hand. None of it should get in the way of the person being comfortable at work, and comfort always flows from the bottom up, from what people ask for. Someone needs a better chair? Let them put in the request; making sure they get that comfort is my job as a manager — even if they are not especially good at their tasks.

Yes, much of what is here could be called bureaucracy. But it is the necessary, minimally viable kind.
