# Selling Your Experience in a Brutally Competitive Market

*Maksim Tcvetkov · 26 September 2025*

We've all sold something at some point — at minimum, ourselves in a job interview. In your twenties it's always easy; any specialist under 30 gets snapped up for top positions without a fight. Closer to 40, you have to actually fight for a good seat. And a fight is a story made of wins and losses. So never give up. You've only lost once you've quit. Learn to take rejection — you'll hear "no" constantly, and that's normal, but don't accept that answer. Keep pushing your case.

In any kind of selling, your attitude is everything. Attitude plays the deciding role. A neutral or negative attitude is already a request to fail. Think your product is garbage? You won't sell a thing. Think you're not good enough to work at a major company? Fine — you never will be. Attitude forms before the first call, the first email, the first conversation with a prospective employer. Always ask yourself, before sending a cover letter: am I, right now, the person who'd actually get hired? Because you're not applying for a job for your own sake — you're applying to solve a pain point on the company's side. They need the best specialist available. Otherwise there wouldn't be an open position in the first place.

Attitude. Not problems — opportunities. Only positive energy in how you speak. Every hour of your calendar should have a plan; don't let yourself drift. Nothing on the calendar? Call up someone at random for a game of tennis, help a neighbor fix their car. As long as you're moving, the negativity falls away. Get up earlier than everyone else, leave later than everyone else. The people who leave early simply don't have the drive or the attitude toward their work that the job demands. Your kids and your spouse should see you come home a winner, not exhausted. And always act. Don't wait — act. Call, write, shout, change things. Use Gmass for mass outreach. People respond to actions, not your feelings.

Always stay busy. Never share bad news — nobody wants your negativity, and you especially don't want people associating you with it. Read a lot of books. If you can't get through an interesting book, how are you going to read and actually understand a contract's terms?

Find people who'll push you forward — a coach, a spouse, a boss, anyone. Someone has to push you to the next level; these are the people who believe in you more than you believe in yourself. Seek out difficulty. The harder an apartment is to buy, the easier and more profitable it becomes to sell later.

Once you go into employment, you're going in to serve a client — sincerely, and with positivity, following the person leading you. Your age doesn't matter here at all. That's where the actual selling process begins. Positivity gets maintained by building a positive environment around yourself: don't read horror stories about job hunting. Opening the news? Don't — you have no idea what's actually in there. Content needs to be predictably positive. Watch successful people, build the environment that reflects that. Work toward the goal, and don't complain. Back hurts? Can't find a partner? That's what children sound like. If you've got something to complain about, it means there's some part of your life you're not controlling. Nobody's to blame but you. Not the economy, not the world order, not your spouse, not your boss, and not even bad luck. Every failure in your life is on you. So we don't complain — everything except your health can be changed. We keep the positivity up, since it's the energy that comes from enthusiasm. The brain defaults to hunting for flaws, since that takes less energy. Everyone around you is looking for your weaknesses — your job is to look for the good in yourself. Against all odds. Dress positively, be a little unreasonable about what you expect of yourself. Set the bar high. Do more than expected. Do the hard part first. Run at full throttle.

## SPIN

Step one in SPIN selling is figuring out value. Before an interview, it's better to work out in advance what the company's actual needs are. The job description is the bare minimum — go deeper: what does this specific HR person actually want? Ask more open questions. Build credibility with numbers: not "grew the financial metrics and hit our OKRs," but actual figures, striking phrasing, real achievements that aren't a stretch.

Your capacity to sell yourself depends directly on the size of your network. Grow it constantly — ideally well in advance. When you actually need to job-hunt, you'll find yourself unable to reach the right person even for money. But if you've been someone's contact for a long time, and even chatted here and there, a direct message reads as a request for help, not a sales pitch.

A company is looking for an employee. You need to know why they're hiring, what for, and why now specifically. No need to play detective — you can ask the hiring side these questions directly. And you probably won't get a detailed answer, which is on you. Be persistent, keep pushing until you get one. But never ask unnecessary questions at the start of a conversation, and certainly nothing out of place — like, "so, am I getting hired?" or "what's the budget for this role?" The salary number isn't where a negotiation begins.

If your mindset is "I just need any job," you'll be searching a long time and land the minimum salary. Your attitude and mindset need to be as positive and ambitious as possible, regardless of circumstances. It's easy to show that attitude through your words: I'm genuinely thrilled, this is exactly the kind of work I'm here for, you're right, I agree with you, great idea, you're already my favorite manager so far. Make people happy, stay positive. And do it fast — big money and high speed are basically synonyms.

On the first call, the priority is making a warm impression — and that's what decides who's actually steering the conversation. Greet people with enthusiasm, and memorize every participant's name beforehand. Smile. Never argue, unless you already know the company culture runs on constant debate. Think about your surroundings, what you're wearing, what's visible in your background. Never admit you don't know something. You need to inspire trust. A person's natural instinct on meeting someone new is to gauge how safe they are — and safety comes from shared interests. If you're trying to sell yourself as a proactive specialist and the hiring manager is looking for a quiet order-taker, there's a real conflict of interest, and it isn't in your favor. Your job is to find the shared ground: "our tasks are pretty boring" — "I know, but for me these would be the most interesting tasks in the world."

Don't say "if you hire me" — say "when I join the team." Not "your design department," but "my design department." Describe the upside of every achievement, don't just recite the numbers off your resume. Finished a great university? Describe what your education will actually give the company. All of it, with enthusiasm.

During negotiations, NEVER go on the defensive. They're dragging your test task through the mud? Wonderful — engage with it, don't make excuses. Criticizing your experience? Pile more on, lean into the conversation. You need to sell yourself at $6,000 if you want a salary of $3,000 — sell for more than you're actually asking. Convince yourself you're worth at least a million a month. There's no shortage of services online for real salary data — Teamblind, Levels.fyi (FAANG), Indeed, Glassdoor Salary.

If an interviewer asks where your real lean is, UI or UX, they expect an actual answer to that exact question — pick one, not "I'm good at everything, they're inseparable anyway." Hedging isn't always the right call either. When you're asked what you prefer, the expected answer is genuinely about your preference — that's how the recruiter checks a box (or doesn't) on your focus during screening. Selling is about closing a need, filling a gap. The same logic applies to questions about difficult decisions — the question isn't about how hard the decision was for you personally, but how significant it was for the company's growth. Something wasn't working, it mattered to the business, and your decision fixed it. A difficult decision is one you can't roll back. Adding NFC to a phone, say — that immediately raises the budget question: the cheaper the NFC chip, the less data it can transmit. Decisions like that get made fast, at the speed of the market, or faster.

Another example of a hard decision: it's time to cut the call-center headcount. But the managers won't accept the change and say they'd rather quit than accept any of it. The right path here is rolling changes out gradually — except the deadline's already burning. That's a genuinely hard situation, with real consequences.

Remember that pleasing everyone is an impossible task. Some people want case studies from a junior who grew a startup into a multibillion-dollar near-monopoly. Others want stagnant, unambitious, highly skilled workhorses. Knowing this makes selling your candidacy easier. That's where your case presentation begins — the one that actually sells you to the client. Always show the right product to the right audience. At the key moments of a presentation, explicitly ask for your client's full attention. Direct their attention with your words: pay attention to this, you really need to see this. Money follows attention — you need to impress, and control where the attention goes. And ask at the end: have you seen enough to make a decision?

## Resume

Your resume isn't the story of your career — it's a sales script written for a specific client, tailored to every single vacancy and the specific ATS behind it. If the posting says "UI/UX Designer" and your resume header says "Total B2B Product Designer," it goes straight in the trash. If the requirements list Photoshop, you don't prepare a defense of Affinity Designer as the superior tool — you add Photoshop to your resume. If a posting doesn't mention 3D skills, cut them from your resume. Under heavy competition, you need a 100% match, not "well, I could probably handle it." Some ATS systems are configured to auto-reject on a match against certain keywords, and that keyword might be "Sketch," "Master," or "iGaming." Under GDPR Article 22, a candidate can't legally be rejected by a fully automated system alone — which is exactly why "unsuitable" resumes get dropped into a folder HR never actually opens. To keep your resume out of that folder, it needs to match the posting 100%. An ATS isn't AI — it just compares words between the posting and the resume, then hands the recruiter the top matches.

Tailoring a resume to every single posting is a lot of work — use AI tools or services like [myperfectresume.com](https://www.myperfectresume.com/) to help. You'll never account for every nuance anyway, since HR runs on a whole zoo of tools — Bullhorn, iCIMS, JobDiva, SuccessFactors, Workday, Kenexa/BrassRing. Workday, for instance, automatically sorts candidates without local residency into a separate folder, and there's nothing to be done about that. You can always upsell your skills once you're talking to the actual hiring manager, but HR isn't interested in that — they're working off a checklist.

If several people are on the interview, work out the roles ahead of time: who actually owns the decision (cares about hiring a genuinely strong specialist) and who's the loudest voice in the room (the team's opinion leader).

Your most recent job on the resume needs to closely match the role you're applying for — that's the only way you're actually competitive. The standard resume structure: contact info > work experience > education > personal projects > key skills as tags. Work experience reads best structured this way:

- A brief note on what you actually did in that role
- Which of your own personal achievements are worth highlighting (if you merely took part in a project without a distinct personal contribution, cut it)
- The main technologies you used, also as tags. On achievements: they need to genuinely match your level of seniority — nobody's expecting a part-time junior to have delivered a landmark achievement.

Every market has its own template — Europass for the EU, HH for Russia. A big, well-known company on your resume always beats a small, unknown one — your odds of clearing screening go up slightly, and known brand names on a resume tend to "pull" other known brands toward you down the line.

Working and studying at the same time is normal in Russia; in the US, it's practically unheard of, and only really understood as part-time work in a student's final semesters. Education in the US is expensive, costing a fortune every year — many people take out student loans and want to get everything possible out of that education. They're also required to complete a certain number of credit hours; skipping isn't really an option.

And here's the essential catch. If you do everything correctly, you still won't succeed — because every other participant in the market is doing everything correctly too. Everyone reads this same article, watches the same YouTube tutorials, gets feedback on their resume from the same handful of people. Everyone's running the same sales scripts, everyone read the same books and the same advice. So don't wait for the client to come to you. Go to the client yourself and pitch the product. Find people's emails, message them on social media. Follow up every 5–10 days. Expect to land a job after roughly 1,000 relevant applications. Send out 10 applications a day, and within about three months you'll likely get an offer. If you have money but no time, automate your applications with [wobo.ai](https://www.wobo.ai/) or something similar. And even if you're fated to bomb an interview, at least fail because you tried too hard, not because you fell short. Do everything as if your life depended on this one task. If you can't answer some question, steer the conversation toward the area you're actually strong in.

## Intermediaries

Recruitment agencies can genuinely help. In the CIS market there's a certain distrust of external agencies, but internationally, they help enormously. The reason is simple: internal company HR teams are lazy, and they simply don't review 1,000 applications a day. They wait for an external agency to bring them good resumes, or for a friend to refer a friend. External recruiters get thousands of applications a day across different roles, screen the ones that clear auto-filtering, and open the first 5–10. They get on a call with you, check basic sanity, and pass your resume straight to the team leads. Simple math: you're one of 500 applicants, 500 / 10 = 50, meaning your odds of even being looked at are 2%. Apply to 100 postings, and you're nearly guaranteed one review. Out of 10 reviews, you should land one offer — which is exactly where that 1,000-application figure comes from.

## Going international

However strong a lead you are out of a top CIS bank, on the international market there's always someone better than you: cheaper, more experienced, speaks more languages, needs no visa or insurance, was laid off from a MAANG company just yesterday, holds three passports, and has lived in Paris for ten years with a paid-off mortgage. Betting purely on hard skills is a losing strategy.

But here's the real secret: being the best isn't actually the deciding factor. Hard skills alone don't sell anymore. There are plenty of reasons you might not get hired: they only want people straight out of MAANG, or holders of a specific passport. They might only hire people they already know, or HR might simply be sabotaging the hiring process altogether.

Which is why it matters to write directly to decision-makers — you can buy their contact information through services like Datanyze. There's no point selling to someone who can't actually make the decision. Find the senior manager, or your potential future boss. Befriend them, build a sense of urgency around solving some real problem, show them data on how competitors are already eating into the market. Propose a goal and a plan to reach it, with you as part of the execution. And here's the catch: almost always, you'll still get sent back to HR for the formal process — but at least you'll have broken through the ATS filters. Lay the groundwork for selling your potential, and only then talk about your past accomplishments. Don't dwell on the past, though — past success is no guarantee of future wins.

The less free time your prospective contact has, the more money they command and the more consequential the decisions they can make — which is exactly the signal that you've found a real decision-maker. In an in-person context, dress for the interview at a premium level. Comfortable for the client, professional. Carry yourself like a winner — a champion's stride, fire in your eyes. Look people directly in the eye, with a smile. Keep that smile even when you're being rejected, or hit with a hard question. And if you're the one declining something — an offer, say — don't do it with a flat "no, that's final." Say instead, "the offer's genuinely great, but here's what would need to change…" — with a smile. Make this a habit: smile like a baby. A baby can't actually do anything — it just makes noise and causes trouble — and yet everyone's enchanted by its smile. You're wrong about something? Smile anyway. Nearly every successful person is wrong about plenty of things. Have you ever met a boss who reliably makes the right call every time? No such person exists.

## Answering questions

You need answers ready for every possible question — rebuttals prepped for every objection, an ace up your sleeve. Here are a few pointers:

1. What's a difficult decision you recently had to make? — one you can't undo.
2. Which social networks do you use, and why?
3. How do you prioritize tasks? — they're expecting an actual framework, not "by feel."
4. A project you're proud of?
5. How do you resolve team conflict? — reference a real model: ADKAR, McKinsey 7S, Lean Change Management, PDCA, the SBI-G feedback framework.
6. How do you handle rapid context switching?
7. Walk me through a recent difficult work situation in detail. — answer using STAR (situation, task, action, result).
8. Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager.

A "difficult situation" answer is almost always a STAR structure:

- **S** — situation: several companies merging into a single holding company;
- **T** — task: migrate several of those companies onto a unified design system;
- **A** — action: building a single design department across every company, consolidating every design specialist under one lead;
- **R** — result: a unified look and feel across the refreshed product line, and better-optimized resource allocation.

## Conflicts

Conflict is unavoidable. You're trying to push through a service, sell yourself, an idea — and not everyone's going to like it. A simple example: you bring a feature to the development team, and development decides to sabotage it with a 900-hour estimate. That's a conflict. And there's an unpleasant wrinkle here: you don't have the option to just walk away from the deal. The developers hold the stronger negotiating position. If you're the one obligated to reach an agreement and your counterpart isn't, you're always at a disadvantage. Have your arguments ready ahead of time: let's push hard right now, we need to ship in three weeks, and it'll be lighter next month.

The key to resolving conflict is priorities. Say you've got two tasks — one improves existing functionality, the other adds something new. The improvement speeds up how fast a procurement request gets filled out in the system; the new feature lets you work with a new type of counterparty. What matters here is figuring out which one saves the company more money — something you work out with the relevant manager. New counterparties might solve more problems than faster request processing. So, absent other context, the new feature gets the higher priority. The most consequential change is always the top priority.
