# Advanced Principles of Designing Television Interfaces

*Maksim Tcvetkov · Product Design and Security Lead*



## Designing for the couch

I have a fairly long track record at companies that proudly call themselves system integrators, developers of a unique OTT platform, creators of a full stack of solutions running from the video-storage station, encoding, and content handling all the way to the end client devices. I would like to share what I have learned about improving those client devices and their interfaces.

By the statistics, 74 % of Russians watch television every day, which lets the telecom operators feel quite comfortable. Ordinary linear TV is still popular: the user sits on the sofa doing nothing, absorbing content — exactly as you are doing right now at your computer. The user must not be disturbed in the enjoyment of the broadcast. This is the traditional, relaxed model of consumption from the sofa, and the user will not want to climb off it, will not want to spend energy and move. The less energy-costly the actions demanded of the user, the higher the quality of the designer's work can be judged to be. The Koreans love to cram a heap of features into their interfaces, which leads only to unjustified complexity; their "creative" thinking breeds a swarm of on-screen buttons you must hit with a mouse — or a hand. Just picture it: you raise your arm, tense your palm, and start flipping channels. A nightmare? It can be made worse with a voice interface — try saying "next page" aloud twenty times as an experiment. How do we avoid all this horror and make interfaces comfortable?

## The remote control

Plainly, the user cannot be distracted while watching TV, so at what stage of the work can a designer show their worth? In designing the remote, for one. I have taken part in designing and engineering television remotes, and I can say for certain that this is one of the most important parts of TV-interface usability, because **a bad remote will kill all the careful usability** you poured 800-plus person-hours into. In the end I made a remote with these buttons: power, a universal exit, up, down, left, right (the D-pad), and apply — with every label set above its button rather than below. That is all; the user needs nothing more, and I will guarantee you that. Why not add a button for jumping quickly to the store, and light it up? Marketing would be delighted to advertise a glowing, many-buttoned remote-contraption that looks like a spaceship, but all of it would make the remote worse and harder to operate blind — and the more buttons there are, the harder it is to operate blind. Many will object: "But what about more functions? Our technical spec lists a pile of requirements for video scrubbing, quick Catch Up, fast jumps, and so on." Here is the professional secret: remotes have the long press, which lets you wriggle out of almost any dead end. A person can hold up to about five objects of attention in mind at once, so five buttons is the maximum, and if you end up with more they must be grouped. Try to keep to the rule of three buttons for the process and two for navigation.

## Response speed and animation

The remote's response speed is a vital part of the ergonomics of using a TV; the reaction to a button press should be close to instantaneous, with 40 ms a good result. And do not forget the animations. Consider the case where a button is pressed on the remote, the animation plays, and during that animation any further input from the remote is blocked — that is bad, so let the input not be blocked. Now you get a queue of animations instead, because pressing the button again while one is playing stacks them up one behind another, which drags on the interaction with the device — so let all the animations accelerate under repeated presses. This holds not only for TV interfaces but for every field of interface design, game development included.

Animation, in fact, follows the principle of continuity, exactly as in any interface: on a change of page state or a transition between pages, the animation should take less than a second, yet the user must see the feedback and the process of the interface changing. As for the unpredictability of an animation's duration, which can run from a few milliseconds to infinity, animations come in two kinds — deterministic, like cyclic progress indicators, and non-deterministic.

![A looping deterministic progress animation in a material-design interface](http://www.materialdoc.com/content/images/2015/11/compatibility--v1.gif)

For non-deterministic animations the main rule is simple: if an object should appear in the scene it flies in quickly and decelerates, and to remove an object from the scene it should start its movement slowly and then accelerate. The standard duration for any interface animation, 200 to 500 ms, works for televisions too, and a service like the Ceaser tool at matthewlein.com helps greatly when you need to describe such requirements to developers.

There is sound reasoning behind that 200-to-500 range. Any action faster than 100 ms is perceived as instantaneous; anything under 0.3 seconds reads as an immediate reaction; an animation faster than 5 seconds still keeps the user's concentration; up to 8.5 seconds remains acceptable; and beyond 8.5 seconds is a failure, since attention switches to other things. Context matters, of course — switching a channel in 5 seconds is far too long, while reheating food in 5 seconds is plenty fast. Duration even lets you add weight to a product, because a service that solves a task in a single second is not perceived as valuable and may merely irritate: a chess AI deliberately slows its moves by a few seconds, well-made chatbots first show a bubble with a message being typed, and aggregators show loaders as though they were polling a heap of sites — all because users feel discomfort when the computer does everything too fast. Up to 2 seconds a loader suffices; from 2 to 5 seconds a progress bar is better; beyond 10 seconds you must hold attention with noticeable visual treatment.

Handing an animation from After Effects to the developers is its own small craft. The route is the Lottie library — `lottie-android` from Airbnb — whose project page lists exactly which After Effects properties you can export to JSON and pass along, though the library itself is enormous, so prepare the development team for that. Say you want a particular animation in an Android app, one where a shape morphs and objects shift in position and opacity.

![An example shape-morph animation targeted for an Android app](http://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/g.gif)

To get there you first need a different library, the bodymovin plugin for After Effects. If installation from Adobe's official add-on store refuses to work despite a valid licence (as it did for me), extract `bodymovin.zxp` from `build/extension/` into the Adobe CEP extensions folder — on Windows, `C:\Program Files (x86)\Common Files\Adobe\CEP\extensions` or `C:\AppData\Roaming\Adobe\CEP\extensions` — then open the registry key `HKEY_CURRENT_USER/Software/Adobe/CSXS.6` and add a `PlayerDebugMode` key of type String with value `1`. The plugin then appears under Extensions; its interface is simple and logical, you export the animation to JSON and hand that to the developers, and the player built into the plugin lets you preview the result. If you simply need a starting point for passing animation to developers or measuring timings cleanly, begin with Bodymovin and with `inspectorspacetime`.

A help screen, incidentally — that wall of solid text everyone dreads — can be delivered through a human-assisted bot. Bots are worth a brief word here, since the social networks and messengers opened development interfaces to them in waves: WeChat and Line launched an API and marketplace in 2013, Telegram its Bot API in 2015, Facebook Messenger its API in 2016, VK its BizMessage API in February 2016, and WhatsApp its API across 2016–2017. Bots are still used rarely and tend to be very simple, but they can initiate interaction with the user on their own, whereas apps are more complex, used more often, and win as an interface solution — and platform-dependent features cannot be built through bots at all. The death of mobile apps has been prophesied before, both from the side of web apps saved as a link on the phone and from the side of voice interfaces; my own guess is that messenger bots will grow complex enough to need a complex UI of their own, becoming apps within apps.

## Other controllers

Game controllers exist too, and they are varied. They all place Select and Back in roughly the same spot, but the arrangement of the other buttons and triggers differs everywhere. Joystick navigation is convenient first and foremost for gamers, and thumb buttons are also very comfortable — more so than the cross of a D-pad.

## Text and the rule of 13

The television screen sits far from the viewer, two to five metres away, and you cannot bring it closer. On the web or in a mobile interface I can afford to shrink the body text and place small type, and a person will still make out the information; on a TV that risks lifting the user off the sofa, and with it goes their zombified state of relaxation. So keep the rule of 13: the screen may hold a **maximum of 13 lines of text** — 14 is already unreadable. That is the smallest point size at which the user will not squint trying to read your text, and every television channel observes it. So you keep to your 13 lines, all the necessary text does not fit, the designer wails and weeps, and even condensed typefaces fail to save the day — what now? Remove the content entirely, or move it to other screens of the interface, or onto the second screen. And if for some reason you cannot test your product on a real device, hold to these floors: headings from 90 pixels, ordinary text from 26 pixels, non-essential text from 18 pixels, and thin fonts simply do not read.

The wording on those screens deserves as much care as their size, and the platform guidelines are usually in English and so not always applicable — it is enough to choose a good word, and a good word is easy to recognise because an icon comes to it readily. A button or menu item is a verb of action: *exit*, *save*, *add*, *enter*, with the perfective aspect suiting one-off actions and the imperfective suiting regular ones. A plain menu item answers "what?" — *about the app*, *events*, *calls*, *registration*. Object selections, checkboxes, and radio buttons answer "whose, which, who exactly, what kind?" — *red*, *large*, *state-owned* — and are best phrased positively. An input field takes the object of the action — *age*, *sex*, *I am looking for*. Sliders take a complement — *little*, *much*, *loud*. Pushes and other status changes answer "what is this?" — *viewed*, *debited*, *credited*. If you want to do it really cleanly, remember the coherence of the text: the interface should read like a connected sentence, and lean on the passive voice — "payment sent" rather than "payment was successfully sent."

## Keeping focus

On the web or on mobile, the focus state is usually added as an afterthought. In television interfaces you must think about focus constantly: the focused element should be larger, brighter, and slightly animated, focus must always be present, and there must be exactly one. Focus is designed in from the very beginning — if you make the OK button yellow and the Cancel button blue, then a user who looks away and returns to the interface will not understand which button the focus is on.

## Showing content on the big screen

So everything is done, sensibly, to the guidelines and recommendations. What now? Now you must show content on a big, bright screen, and this is the most interesting part, the one where your designers can really show themselves. Say you want to show the traffic on the home screen. "Draw the congestion score in the top-right corner," many will say — but you could instead shoot a set of mini-clips and play them behind the main menu of your interface: clear roads, roads in heavy traffic, and so on. You can also wish the person a happy birthday, show advertising, and offer all sorts of tips.

## The second screen

Where else can designers play with type and more? The second screen. Every user has a mobile device, and connecting the television to it is no great trouble. Typing text on the TV with the remote is its own kind of hell, and doing it from a mobile device is incomparably more convenient — even the handiest form controls, such as segmented controls, switches (yes or no), and plus-and-minus buttons, are nicer to use on the phone. Show character biographies, ratings, and comments, add a social layer, and your second screen becomes a continuation of the big television screen.

It is worth not tying the second screen so tightly to the television, though. A car navigator on a mobile device is also a second screen — the first screen, in that case, being the windscreen. And the second screen should not be confused with multiscreen: a multiscreen can live inside a second screen, but not the other way round. To put it plainly, some devices reproduce everything that is playing on the TV, while others exist to provide supplementary information, and only the latter bear the proud name of second screen. The technology is younger than multiscreen and is used first of all by marketers, almost always badly and crookedly. The best current practices in Russia are the ability to watch video from another angle and to pull up supporting information; among examples, the market still lacks a sufficiently polished implementation, except perhaps for ESPN, which has interesting work where you watch a sports broadcast and receive unique video fragments, texts about the players, and much else in real time — and the sports industry is, to my mind, the most interesting field for the second screen overall. The bottleneck in developing the technology is the preparation of the data behind it: imagine an EPG, an on-screen programme guide, where the full schedule is missing, every second programme is absent, and the times are wrong — that is roughly the state of the second screen. Looking ahead, the technology will grow thanks to the telecom operators who build their own set-top boxes and mobile apps, since it is easiest for them to develop a closed ecosystem and generate the content; and technically, within a single provider ecosystem the synchronisation runs through the main cloud service, so at any moment we know what the user is watching. There is also a less elegant route, through recognition of the audio stream, in which case the TV and the mobile device are not linked at all.

## Catch Up

Catch Up is an excellent technology, and a designer who analyses it can build a stunning service from it. Catch Up is the automatic recording of linear channels and the provision of access to those recordings; every interactive-TV service offers it for an additional fee. This service must be sold to the user well, with quality, and it must be upgraded, or the competitors will do it first — it must be made better than theirs. How? Do not ask the business-development director, who will think up nothing new. Ask the designer.

Users do not understand the full value of Catch Up and use it very passively. Long ago people bought VCRs, set programmes to record, came home, and watched them. The telecom operators caught on — "the service is in demand" — invented the DVR, and sold it to users. But the operators began recording absolutely everything on air, which, as it turned out, the user did not particularly need, because the user did not record it themselves and does not know what was on through the day, so many simply forgot the recorded-programmes service existed. Just picture it: the user is forced to walk down the channel list, look through the programme list, and pick out something interesting, which is usually slow and inconvenient, and the programmes are not advertised, they just sit in one of a heap of lists. In short, the EPG does not sell passive linear viewing. How to solve this? Look at the statistics. Thirty channels make up 80 % of all viewing; a hundred channels yield about seventy films a day; the main quality content is concentrated on a small number of channels.

The conclusion is that users should be handed quality, interesting, relevant recordings at once and automatically, chasing not quantity but quality and variety. Editorial work is the most important thing here: the editor must be good, choosing the best content and feeding it to users without repeating themselves, must know films and TV programmes well, and should be given an automated recommendation system, of which many are sold. Let the editor fill that system, because the robot recommends poorly, merely by title; spare the user the choice and let them tap straight onto good content. Show not 150 programmes but 15 good ones. Some 400 to 500 films are made a year, and at best 50 are worth the attention.

## Presenting the content

So the content is selected — how do you present it to the user? You must enrich the information: give a good description, add good screenshots run through [my algorithm](https://your-scorpion.ru/script-photoshop/) for optimising graphic content for stores, perhaps make a mini-trailer, add handsome headings, news bulletins, reviews, and ratings from various news sites. And all of it must be shown to the user — create a separate section, lay it out by genre, rank it by popularity, and feature new arrivals every day. Catch Up is a new kind of VOD content, a library of the day's content.

A word on the video itself, since people ask about frame rates. A DCP is generally 24 fps, bounded only by what cinema projection can manage, even when the material was shot at 60 fps; but because television broadcast wants 25 fps there are nuances, as content may have been shot for TV, web, or direct-to-video rather than for cinema in the first place. The DCI INTEROP standard calls for 24 fps, and 48 fps for 3D; the DCI SMPTE standard allows up to 120 fps, but only a handful of cinemas in the world can play even 96 fps. That is the theory; in practice, ad agencies happily output MP4 at a bitrate of 20,000 Kbit/s or higher in Full HD 1920×1080. From After Effects this comes out as an image sequence, assembled with a tool such as DCP-o-matic.

## Smart TV, Google TV, and the set-top box

By way of conclusion, two pieces of bad news. The first: many will try to apply the practices above to Smart TV, and rightly so — but Smart TVs are very slow, awkward devices. Cramming a heap of good hardware inside the set would of course solve the problem, except that a television's life cycle is seven years, and the Smart TVs already on the market in your clients' homes will not be going anywhere before 2020, with expansion modules powerless to save the situation. The second piece of bad news concerns Google TV and Apple TV, which are accessories to mobile phones and nothing more. So everything said above applies 100 % only to the set-top box, which has all the goodies — 4K, h265 support, voice control through a speaker-and-projector or a microphone in the remote.

That said, do not treat Smart TV as a closed environment for which an app must be built. Few apps are being developed for it, true — but in 2015 sales of smart televisions overtook sales of ordinary ones, and that is without counting set-top boxes or Chromecast, so the market, and therefore the demand, exists. It is better to extend the mobile app's ecosystem through the Smart TV's: use the phone to control content on the TV remotely, stream media or gameplay from the smartphone to the set, use the smartphone as a games joystick (a phone holds nineteen different kinds of sensor). There are many directions to grow in — the key is to remember that the phone is the primary device.
