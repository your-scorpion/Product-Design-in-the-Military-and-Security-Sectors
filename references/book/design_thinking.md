# Design Thinking
*Maksim Tcvetkov · Product Design and Security Lead*


## Introduction

Agile, Scrum, Waterfall, Kanban, sprints, planning, retrospectives, and a great many other fine words are meant to help us work on projects efficiently and predictably. Yet somehow the process and the result don't always match what the team and the client expected, and the principle of "do it properly and it'll turn out fine" fails to fire. The reason is that development throws up a heap of constraints, from the engineering side and the management side alike, and the result is a constant compromise between design and development. You can resign yourself to that fate — or step up a level and start influencing not only the design but the product.

To do that you must play a key role in the speed and quality of scaling the product. The general process, which hides skilfully under the words "information architecture," is this: gather information (define the problem to be solved); design (research and develop options for solving it); and choose (analyse and select one of several options). Design thinking is an important part of your skill set — and not in the sense of a five-phase model for teaching sprints, but in the sense of keeping the user at the very heart of the design decision. The approach focuses on studying users' expectations of a product or service and delivering that experience to them, and at the meeting point of design thinking and information architecture you gain the ability to design interfaces with maximum return for the business.

![The intersection of design thinking and information architecture](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Group-40.png)

The first thing design thinking begins with is collaboration with the future users. The design team meets people to discuss an existing product and to learn the consumers' requirements and needs, and the feedback helps not only to remove the product's flaws but to keep the team's eye from going stale.

## What design thinking is

The method was created at Stanford University and rests on two principles: a deep understanding of the client's needs, and step-by-step validation of the business idea. It works for innovation in a small private business and a large company alike, and it works best in combination with Agile. I want to fix one idea firmly: being a designer and thinking like a designer are different things — the first is *doing*, the second is *thinking*. The popular phrase "design is too important to be left to designers" hints unambiguously that the scope of design thinking varies into an endless number of applications; as Einstein is quoted, "we cannot solve our problems with the same thinking we used when we created them." Design thinking is simply a way to step beyond the standard approach to the work.

A designer's process runs roughly like this: meet the client, extract every detail of the project, then run problem interviews with potential users and find their pain. Pain and needs can be found through interviews, but also through a panel study or any other method; you analyse and structure the data into personas, customer journey maps, and other artefacts. Once it becomes clear that you cannot find a solution to the users' pain, you run a design-thinking session — its purpose in the ordinary development process is to find the solution to the problem. Say you want to help a telecom sell modern intercoms: you apply design thinking and invent intercoms with one-time entry codes, a notification to the phone, gate-barrier opening, and the transmission of electricity-meter readings. The session itself looks like this: day one, a map; day two, sketches; day three, the customer journey map; day four, a prototype; day five, a prototype test.

![The design-thinking wheel](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/wheel_design_thinking.jpg)

Design thinking can fail for many reasons — leadership ego, too much money in the company, prejudice, laws, the environment, the physical location of the business, technology, trends, time — but even at its minimum it brings the team together and unites it. It is not a concrete method or framework but, as the name says, a way of thinking about the product; IBM has a whole division that sells the design-thinking function to outside clients.

## Generating hypotheses

Design thinking is the generation of **hypotheses**, always taught in workshop form, usually offline (online is harder and demands more activity). Don't be afraid to propose bad ideas, like taking over the world — that helps too and is an integral part of the method; at this stage be as playful and enterprising as possible, because no idea will face harsh criticism, not because everyone is kind but because criticising ideas is forbidden. Why is all of this needed? Creative people — artists, musicians — often lose their inspiration, that flow of magic energy from the cosmos, and fall into creative stagnation, alcoholism, depression; designers are prone to it too, taking on a commission and doing nothing, unable to find their muse, sitting and waiting for inspiration to strike, and when it finally arrives there's no time left to do the project well. Creative methods put the generation of ideas onto a conveyor belt. The essence: create an enormous number of hypotheses, where any idea is a hypothesis — and a hypothesis is an assumption that requires confirmation.

For design thinking you need a team; enthusiasts in a garage no longer generate brilliant ideas, because everything that lay on the surface was implemented long ago, and you have not heard a global success story about one person building something and becoming a billionaire in a while — teams do it now, a team generates more ideas per unit of time. Offline you need a space, or any online-whiteboard service when working remotely; ideally you simply book a meeting room for a week, bring in sticky notes and markers, and off you go. The team should not be only designers — better to diversify it with analysts, managers, and programmers, for many-sided idea generation. Tim Brown compared multidisciplinary and *inter*disciplinary teams: in a multidisciplinary team the designer answers for design and the developer for code, so there is constant friction as everyone defends their patch — the designers voice a hypothesis and the developers immediately assess the difficulty of building it, and the creative wave dies. An interdisciplinary team is better, one where designers understand development, developers understand design, and the manager at least understands *something*.

There are many variants of design thinking, but they are all very similar. Globally it has three steps — inspiration, sifting the ideas, and implementation — and every variant embodies the principles first described by the Nobel laureate Herbert Simon in his 1969 book *The Sciences of the Artificial*. Classic design thinking has five stages, the five-phase model from the Hasso Plattner Institute of Design at Stanford, also known as the d.school: brainstorm (map, empathy); focus (sketches, definition); the customer journey map and the generation and sifting of ideas, which can be "as is" and "to be," then choosing an idea; prototype; test.

![The five-phase design-thinking model](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Slice.png)

All the stages run iteratively. And although 95 % of the Russian-language information you find on design thinking will be about the five-phase approach, understand that the method can have anywhere from three to seven stages, all built on the same principles characteristic of Simon's 1969 model — the original of which has seven stages, while the six-phase version gives a day each to generating ideas and choosing one.

A simple example: a few years ago a lorry driver tried to pass under a low bridge and got firmly stuck, able neither to go forward nor to back out, causing massive traffic problems, so emergency workers, engineers, firefighters, and other drivers gathered to agree on ways to free the vehicle — arguing whether to dismantle parts of the lorry or chip away at the bridge, each proposing a solution that matched his own level of knowledge and experience. A passing boy who witnessed the tense discussion looked at the lorry, the bridge, then the road, and to everyone's astonishment said: "Why not just let the air out of the tyres?" When the solution was tested, the lorry drove free with only the damage from its first attempt. The story symbolises the struggle we face, where the most obvious solutions are often the hardest to reach because of the self-imposed constraints we work within.

## Lateral thinking

Hence the term *lateral thinking* (lateral = sideways: we walk not the beaten path but a strange, unexplored one). Edward de Bono published *The Art of Thinking* in 1967, and its essence is to reject the accepted patterns of thinking and doubt everything. Look at a black square and you immediately associate it with Malevich — but what if it isn't, what if it is a different black square? Such provocation lets the brain start thinking; you create a problem for it, which lets you notice what no one noticed before. There are more than thirty patterns of brain provocation (lateral-shift mechanics), but the main ones are: add a word, remove a word, substitute a word, exaggerate (scale up or down), and reorder.

![Lateral-thinking mechanics](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/Group-29.png)

Take adding a property to an object: you have a thermos, and you add the preposition "on," getting "thermos — on — a board." A board that keeps food warm? Or exaggerate: take a village house and enlarge it twentyfold — what comes out, and how could such a building be used? And immediately reorder: people do not come to the house, the house comes to the people. There can be dozens of such solutions. The next step in generating an idea: you have an enormous village house that must come to us, and the brain is uncomfortable with it, so write out the idea's pluses, minuses, and interesting points — the most useful material sits in the last block, the questions and ideas the solution raises.

![Sorting an idea's pluses, minuses, and interesting points](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/Group-32.png)

Then establish a link with reality: you have an absurd idea, and it is time to map it onto the real world. Look for the benefits and advantages, break the new hypothetical scenario into steps, and pick out the ones that are buildable, rational, and fit the business, building on the pluses.

## Filtering the ideas

We have covered the first stage, inspiration; after it comes the stage of sifting out irrelevant hypotheses. Inspiration needs a small team, because beyond about ten people groupthink sets in and suppresses each participant's creativity — the simple rule being that the more people, the less creativity each contributes, with too many opinions forming, the facilitator unable to record every hypothesis, and everyone hoping they can hold back. Around five people per thinking session is good, and the implementation stage can take more; if the participants stop generating hypotheses, the facilitator should toss out questions like a toastmaster.

There are three pillars of uniqueness, each a constraint and a criterion. Technology is a constraint. Business is a constraint too, on the principle of "will there be money?" And in classic design thinking we start from the person and their needs — whereas we tend to invent the project idea first and only then think about how to graft it onto people's needs, which is why 42 % of startups fail for lack of demand. We profess the approach of taking the person's needs first and dancing from there.

For prioritising, there is the ICE score, derived from the classic RICE: RICE multiplies **Reach × Impact × Confidence** and divides by **Effort**, while ICE is **Impact × Confidence × Ease**. Alternatives are Kano, Value vs Effort, and Story Mapping.

## Our role

A large company needs a product designer to improve its products and embed innovation in them, and the designer takes responsibility for the decision — you cannot offload responsibility onto the client. When we speak of the designer's decisions, we mean significant improvements and innovations: innovation is the leap from the horse to the tractor, while progress is merely improving the tractor, and for progress an ordinary designer who finished a UX course is quite enough.

Studying information architecture, you move from prototyping to *designing*. Prototyping follows design, so the prototyper works with information prepared by the information architect, who describes what matters and what does not in the project, designs, validates ideas, writes the copy (copywriting skills are mandatory), presents professionally, and knows enough to defend the decisions — a genuinely complex role. The information architect's chief advantage is that they improve the business processes first and only then make the interface, because until the business process is efficient the design will not be either. In an information architect's CV, the "tools" line always starts with notebooks and mind maps for associations (XMind, Miro), online services for teamwork, and specialised software (Axure RP, and frameworks for getting around Axure's limits).

## Running a session

To start running design thinking, talk to the product owner with simple, general questions about the problems of the last six months, then sketch a rough customer journey map on your own. The identified problem must be big and significant enough to bring a group together. In a small company you can invite the CEO, the product manager, the CTO, the head of design, and other important people; in a large one, reaching such senior people is hard, so you invite someone important from sales, someone from marketing, a couple of developers, and analysts. You can gather up to seven, the figure coming from three people on the design-agency or design-department side and four on the business side; more than seven is bad, fewer is fine, and four to seven is the optimum. Most important, there must be a decision-maker — the feature owner with the power to act on the session's results and launch development or change in the company — or the whole exercise is useless. The session should run for four days. The most popular article online will say five, but the market has gradually settled on four, and you need the full set of people only on the first and fourth days, so the busy, powerful people are pulled off their work for just two days (even less).

The four-day version: on day one we run interviews as the main part of the brainstorm, draw an experience map (spending no more than an hour on it), and define the goals; on day two we vote for solutions, focusing on the potential ones; day three is the prototype; day four is the prototype test. So where the classic runs brainstorm → focus → CJM/idea-sifting → prototype → test, our version runs brainstorm (interviews, empathy) → focus (set goals) and vote for ideas → prototype → test.

## The type of thinking

To the profession of user-interface designer you must add an understanding of the financial model and pricing, which means researching the *market*, not only interfaces. The next important area is the product life cycle, which is strategic, tactical, and operational; designers usually work the operational stages, making an interface purely for the current tasks, whereas a customer journey map should lay in a tactical plan for nine months, and the standard eighteen months can be taken as the strategic horizon.

There is the divergent and the convergent phase, or mode, of thinking. At the inspiration stage we generate as many ideas as possible and avoid all criticism, sifting nothing (the first idea is always to take over the world); at the second stage we sift and pick the most promising and buildable. It has become common to validate ideas by SCAMPER, a simple checklist.

![The SCAMPER checklist](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Slice-1.png)

Who can help a product designer? Whoever defines the metrics that matter to the business — a good metric is comparable, easy to understand, quickly measurable, and decision-ready. There will almost certainly be someone with business-analyst skills in the company, and you should befriend them; they should know cohort analysis and churn and have a strong mathematical background, and with their help you build cone-of-uncertainty models showing how uncertainty behaves in well-managed projects, which lets us value the product not in the moment but along a nine-month vector for the customer journey map. All this needs metrics, which are comparisons of quantities — without them you face uncertainty, one decision equalling one metric. But not all of your team can support you in this, and you must be ready for that: most technical specialists have convergent thinking, the linear kind that proceeds step by step along simple, clear algorithms ("do it properly and it'll turn out fine"), and a metric is precisely a way to simplify the world, tracking a value instead of trying to understand a changing, complex system — and not everything can be measured. Metrics are input or output: output metrics are about a result we deliver, like the share of the design system covered by code, while input metrics are about external factors, like the number of days from a designer's hire to their first mockup. For creative people, though, that approach is unacceptable; divergent thinking should be closer to you, the creative method of seeking many solutions to the same problem, and it is well worth getting acquainted with lateral thinking, based on shifting away from traditional thinking.

![Convergent versus divergent thinking](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/Frame-564.png)

The process of recognising and defining a problem runs through vision, business goals, metrics, market analysis, audience, monetisation, and a road map, and at every stage of recognising the business's problems there is brainstorming — a wonderful way to dream within a clearly stated task, a way to generate ideas unlike the standard ones. Apply it at the start of the "What if?" stage, and for it to succeed the participants must care about the problem, which means you have to show its consequences for people. (What you make should entertain, impress, intrigue, and teach — a spicy video being the ideal to aim for.) So there are people about numbers and people about features, which sets innovation against product. Technology matters, but packaging it into a product that fits into a person's life and changes it for the better — something the user will pay for — matters more; designers help justify the existence of technology. Apple invents nothing technically innovative, and is often accused of reusing existing technology and reselling it with small improvements, but Apple does the main thing — it packages the technology into a product people love, and charges accordingly.

During the brainstorm the flow of thought should follow a model, and a good one is the "Five Planes" model that Jesse James Garrett proposed for designing the user experience of web applications — surface, skeleton, structure, scope (features and content), and strategy — which gives a basis for discussing experience problems and the means of solving them. Many think a designer resembles a game designer, whose profession means inventing games, a lot of mathematics, the ability to write much and well, and competence in the subject area; like any creator, 90 % of a game designer's ideas await the bin, and they must work with the technical director, the art director, and the producer (the one with the brilliant ideas). Designers can draw much from game development — but never forget that UX design exists to reduce the number of problems, while game design exists to create problems for the player that are interesting to solve. And within design thinking it is logical to say that a product's value must exceed its price for it to sell — though there are also habits, and I assure you that every one of us, myself included, uses less-than-optimal services out of habit; a service can be expensive without our realising it, through habit or perception. And price is not always money: price is also time — time to download, time to learn something new — and learning is itself a price, an effort over oneself.

## Target audience and personas

For empathy to appear, you must have a persona formed and hanging before your eyes, and it is not a socio-demographic portrait but an archetype with its own customer journey map. "A man aged 25–35 with a median income in a large city" is *not* a persona; he becomes one when we have found his problem and he starts behaving in a particular way to solve it. A man walks down the street and sees that an old lady cannot cross the road, and in that moment his traits appear — compassion, agitation, the wish to help — and the question becomes "What does he do in this situation?" The right answer: to satisfy our personas' needs there exists our client (Jobs To Be Done). There is research of the Empathy Research kind, which requires you to live the user's path yourself — to stand an hour and a half in a queue at the Russian Post or work a day as a taxi driver — which lets empathy emerge.

![An empathy map](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Group-40-1.png)

We must know everything about our user and the services they use — region, economy, infrastructure, cultural specifics, who the competitors are, the company's resources, goals, and values. Personas are an image of the target user that helps us focus on the typical client of our service, and the information must be 100 % based on data from the client at the briefing stage and on research. The persona is a simple tool that everyone applies wrongly, because 90 % of the persona examples online are made wrongly, which has somewhat discredited it.

Research usually produces several personas, but you cannot build a product for everyone at once — a particular product suits a particular person. Say you have a live-speech translation app; ask who could use it — children, adults, extraverts, introverts, pensioners, mechanics, the middle class? Find products with similar functionality and study their audience, which surfaces both your potential audience and your competitors; then segment the target audience until you have a number of personas. For any persona, set out a photo or illustration (ideally with context in the background); a name, and not just "Vanya Ivanov" but "Vanya the skilled plumber" or "Galina the supermum"; a quote, life slogan, or short scenario in context; demographics (sex, age, marital status, role, industry, city, country); goals, outlook, and fears; personality traits (extravert or introvert, and for games necessarily achiever / killer / explorer / socialiser, Jung's archetypes, high or low social status, high or low digital fluency — Steve Jobs is confident and loves rebellion and his work passionately, Martin Luther King is an intellectual, decisive and self-assured, Batman is adaptive, secretive, smart); a short biography with life goals, motivations, and pains; and the technologies used (the internet, software, social networks, iOS or Android, whether they watch TV), plus the experience and the skills that match it. Personas are like postcards from a trip that bring back the memory of it — but you cannot just buy a postcard of the Maldives and reminisce over it if you have never been to the Maldives.

A persona is a collective image of each segment of the target audience, and with personas it is easier to build a product for a specific user, whom you picture and run thought experiments on, for which empathy must be well developed; empathy lets you understand how the user would behave. You should not *rely* on empathy, but you must be able to use it, and you can find people who match your personas closely from talking with the client and speak with them: either you find potential clients and build the personas from dialogue with them, which is the right way, or you build personas from any indirect data. Then choose the key persona, one person — the most profitable or the most numerous — and build the product for them. Usually you take the persona whose pain is most pronounced: the one-handed person who must open a washing-machine door with one hand, which covers not only the genuinely one-handed but also mothers whose one hand is always occupied with a child; or online universities, since not everyone can attend a university or move to another city for it. This is inclusive design — making something accessible, including someone in something, for example including a person with disabilities in the world. The iPhone and Coca-Cola are the same for everyone; presidents and the homeless drink the same Coca-Cola. Inclusion is counterintuitive, because the brain finds it easier to think about why people might *want* to use our product than about why they would not want or be able to.

## Empathy

There is an empathy map into which you record the answers to: what do people think and feel, what do they hear, what do they say and do, what do they see — along with any pains, values, and aspirations. The map is filled in after the interview: into the "says and does" section go the respondent's verbatim quotes, while the "thinks and feels" section is filled with your observation-based interpretations, for example "the respondent is worried about whether he'll be able to care for the greenery." It can be made for a single respondent after each interview or as an aggregated empathy map across all interviews. For hiring repair specialists, the "says and does" section might hold quotes like "I'm willing to overpay but be sure of the quality of the repair," "I'd like to see examples of the work before hiring," "I need not just hands but experience and advice," "at our place my wife chooses the stretch ceilings, I stay out of it," "we have aggressive neighbours, it's risky to renovate with them behind the wall," and "I'd like to hand the specialist all the work of dealing with the contractors"; the "thinks and feels" section, observations like "would like a guarantee that the hired specialist is an experienced expert," "doesn't know whom to ask for a recommendation — everyone has only bad experiences," "not ready to pay the whole sum up front," "afraid the deadlines will slip," and "sure he'll be cheated."

Once we have climbed into the user's skin and felt their pains, we understand where the uncomfortable moments arise and mark them in the emotion block of the CJM — doing it at once, because we often get used to inconvenient situations and stop noticing them over time, so we observe and analyse the respondent's behaviour rather than only asking, though we ask too. The general idea is to understand what your potential user wants: their pains and problems, and what they secretly desire even without realising it — especially important with children, who cannot *tell* you but can *show* you. A typical CJM template always contains the persona, the stages, the touchpoints, goals and expectations, metrics, the customer experience, recommendations, and context, and the emotion graph can be built with an isolated metric per step or cumulatively, where, say, steady negativity accumulates from the very first step so that even a great mobile app leaves a strong residue and bias. The emotion part is one of the most important, because most human behaviour is driven by emotion rather than reason — to cite a Western author, the psychologist Peter Murray used MRI to scan the brain and found that when a consumer evaluates a brand it is the emotions (feelings plus experiences) that form the assessment, not the information the person holds about the brand.

Research exists to bring empathy out, and the first stage of design thinking is **Empathy** for good reason: you pack your things, put yourself in the place of the person with the problem, fly to Africa and try to strike up a conversation with a foreigner, go to China with a pocket electronic dictionary. At Zappos, staff from development first work in the company as salespeople. This is the PoV — the point of view — where we understand how to help a person by looking at their need (their pain); a need is always expressed in a verb (*what to do?*) and in barriers, and the simplest indicator of pain is that the respondent spends money or time solving the problem, checked against Ben Hunt's ladder of awareness. Empathy is also about interviews — engaging and sympathising with people, understanding their experience and motivation, and immersing yourself in their physical surroundings for a deeper personal grasp of the problems — and it consists of four steps: said, did, thought, felt.

## Focus

**Focus** is the second stage, about thinking through all the information gathered in the first and identifying the person's main pain — excess weight, spam calls, trouble with loan repayments — which is the search for demand. Demand can not only be found but imposed, created, legislated for, or driven by fear (fears monetise best of all), but we go the interesting way and find demand through design thinking. Focus is about understanding the problem, and without understanding it correctly we will not invent a solution.

How to do it: cluster similar problems, pick out personas, and select grains of knowledge such as user quotes — each quote a hypothesis — which is essentially a brainstorm. In practice you can approach it variously: take a pad of sticky notes and come up with 20–30 ideas just to warm up, stick them on the board, and sort them into groups, the rule being that we don't criticise, we're all friends and support one another. Then choose the product or service idea that best satisfies the potential client's need, by a vote, weighting selection criteria — the most innovative, the most unexpected, the most rational, the most technically buildable — each member having three votes to give to one idea or spread across several.

A useful approach is the HADI cycle: hypothesis → action → data → insights. The hypothesis stage isolates the key indicators of the project, startup, business, or marketing message and forms hypotheses to test or improve them, phrased as "If…, then…." The action stage carries out the work to launch the experiment and makes the change in production. The data stage collects data over a set period and analyses the changes against the key indicators. And the insights stage interprets the result — you can see how successful the hypothesis was and draw conclusions.

## Prototype

The favourite part of the work: the interface, the converter between machine and human, and small user stories — sometimes epic ones, like a personalised portal home page or a huge tabletop card game in Tabletop Simulator, but all of it made of modules, which are small user stories. Prototypes are created early and often, primarily to get information rather than to test a theoretically finished proposal, and the process should be simple and fast so as to "make mistakes sooner," identifying the areas to fix while reaching agreement on the elements that work.

For a prototype you should have these documents: an information plan; a high-level (surface) prototype for writing the copy and placing the emphases, a document for approving meaning and content; a wireframe as a high level of abstraction; for very complex solutions, such as tariff calculators or document-flow relationships, a clear scheme; a journey map for viewing the user's problems visually; and the character — an archetype as a customer journey map or user flow, where at this stage it is customary to call the user a person. You need not work through every document; the more capabilities (tools, resources, documentation) you have during development, the more brainwork it takes to create and hone the project. A good way to analyse a document is to pull out key words and run a concordance analysis over it. A prototype is the answer to "How can we help?", and the day you build the prototype is always the day you stay late at work; at the test stage you may still make changes and refinements to the prototype or even to the original idea.

At the heart of good decisions sits complex Big Data analysis. The ideal modern digital product is a construction kit, a set of UI elements (a UI kit) from which the site is assembled dynamically for each user, which big data already allows; this is the Data-Driven Design methodology — designing the product on data, research, tests, hypothesis checking, and big data — which covers all users and analyses data about their interests, lifestyle, key problems, and social status, and if you have access to such data at the prototyping stage, use it.

## Information architecture, not site structure

Information architecture is not site structure, and it is wrong to call it the naming and structuring of sections — yes, that bluntly is how I break the stereotype of 95 % of designers. Information architecture is the design of the user experience and the solving of the project's tasks, working with meaning. A watermelon and a tomato are berries only from the standpoint of science; in practice you must place them in both the "vegetables" and the "fruit" sections. The information architect's task is to find common features in the mass of content, form groups of objects, and express them as clear tags, meta-descriptions, and navigation, after which an understanding of users' needs, the specifics of perception patterns, and information about the environment comes into play. (There is no such thing as a creative sabbatical.)

The next stage is closer to a UX designer's work — the decision points such as the home page, the registration form, the product page, the interactive calculator — and since the writing can run to hundreds of pages, you always make simple schemes of the human-product interaction, or even service blueprints. Here you must decide which navigation format you need: hierarchical means you move from the centre inward, each object able to have a child, essentially a "tree"; scenario means the user passes step by step along a defined script, as when you show the optimal purchase or registration path; matrix lets the user not depend on the designer's will, who would otherwise box them into a hierarchy or scenario, and pick any path they like — by intention or by topic. Then you create a conceptual prototype (for an example, look at the work that Funky Punky released publicly), and this is the stage called information architecture, where the "layout" of the scheme happens with regard to space and content prioritisation. Design ends with the prototype realised in a design mockup and then in the final build, which is why authorial supervision matters.

A prototype should reflect important small things from the UX standpoint, and game examples show this best, because not all interaction can be drawn. In GTA 5 the character jogs while X is held, but tap the button rapidly and he runs faster; the R1 button is held to hold another character's hand. Say the player controls the wind and collects insects — make a prototype using the gyroscope so the player feels every tilt and turn with their body; if you control two characters at once, you can drive one with the left hand and one with the right, and losing a character makes the physical motor control weigh on the player's emotional state, so the loss is felt. Even the familiar modal with Yes/No buttons need not be a plate with text — the user could nod their head to say yes or no, which hurts usability but is still an interface.

Prototype usability is measured by the ISO 9241-11:2018 standard, broken into effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction (and it is worth reading 9241 itself). By metrics, the SUS questionnaire is the classic for usability, the MDT (with its 118 adjectives) is for impressions and perception of any product, and SUM is about success, time, and satisfaction with the process.

## Design and content

A company's products can come in very different forms — SMS messages, a personal account, a website, a mobile app — and a separate set of interfaces is developed for each, all assembling into an ecosystem with a synergy effect, for which you must decide on the supported platforms. Along the way you will discover pleasant features such as payment from a messenger, deep linking, or browser notifications; one of my favourites is that a browser can access the smartphone's gyroscope, so you can easily tell whether the device is lying flat or being held.

![Designing an ecosystem of products](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/asia_box.png)

The base platforms remain the classic web and mobile. The breakpoints are 1366×768, 1600×900, and 1920×1080 for desktop; 1024×768 portrait and landscape for tablet; and 320×480 portrait and landscape for phones. The grid matters for every breakpoint, its elements being the column, the gutter between columns, and the overall margin at the edges, and there are two design approaches: a fluid grid, where the number of columns stays the same and they change width, or a stepped grid, where the column count changes at breakpoints and the layout is redrawn.

![Fluid versus stepped grids](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/1-7YeOvzoYgUEDJdfQy2ERXg.png)

It is very hard to work with ideas if you cannot visualise them. Start with mobile-first: draw the interface for the phone and scale up to tablet and desktop, keeping only the most important information on phones, making everything large and bold for touch, and keeping it lightweight (little graphics) — though a desktop-first approach is also possible, starting from desktop and degrading to the phone. Mind the line length (45–75 characters is ideal), and remember a vital nuance: on phones it is not a grid but a *step*, so think about how the blocks can transform; for modularity I am usually inspired by Sasha Gladkikh's ideas, whose interfaces are all touchable, even the desktop ones. A few useful numbers: 72 as the base baseline; micro-animations of 200 ms (60–200 ms looks very fast); meso-animations of 400–600 ms; and macro-animations of any length, usually on onboarding pages. On output you hand over (or receive, depending on your role) style guides, a type grid, and necessarily mockups (high-quality sources), and it is good to create your own stylistic system the way Google did with Material Design, which advanced clear theses describing its style — surface as a textureless container, every object with depth, every transformation a manipulation of magic digital paper. Prototypes are made for everything: micro-animations, the main animations, presenting the project, and checking the navigation.

In brief, the design-thinking process rests on these tools: empathy (sympathy, mirroring emotions, gathering information, UX research); iterations and sprints (Agile, fast idea-checking, where many mistakes are allowed); prototyping (throw out Axure and Xcode — take the most basic user scenarios and prototype them as fast as possible, fast meaning one minute, with cardboard, glue, scissors, and napkins, or Lego Serious Play for objects); teamwork, discussing on the principle of "yes, great idea, and we could also…" rather than "yes, great idea, but it won't work!"; and storytelling, a clear account of which problem you solved. So: empathy to gather information → focus on what matters → generate ideas for the problems we fixed → choose the buildable, good ones → prototype → test — but it is not a linear process; iterations happen regularly, from focus back to empathy, or from testing back to idea generation.

## Releasing and monetising

Don't forget the product release, which can be time-driven or feature-driven: time-driven means a firm release date that we hit even if the product isn't polished to a shine, while feature-driven means we must build a certain amount of functionality before we ship. A free-to-play product is usually time-driven, where you can sacrifice functionality for a good MVP, while a pay-to-play audience is more demanding of quality, hence feature-driven. The monetisation models I would single out are freemium (free use plus paid content); free-to-play (free but hampered without payment); premium (a product with a price); subscription (for services used regularly, now often with a free trial period); transactional (you are the intermediary taking a commission, easy for a marketplace living on a small percentage of a huge audience, as with any money-transfer service); the advertising model (a little use, watch an ad — and a click makes it CPA, a kind of affiliation redirecting clients to a big player — or hidden revenue from partners); combinations of the above (bought a subscription, but the unique content is still premium and ads appear); and selling add-ons, which is lock-in. To go deeper, read *The Business Model Navigator* (55 best business-model patterns) by Oliver Gassmann.

## Testing

Testing a prototype is not as expensive as it may seem — eight people per target audience suffice for quality testing, and five are enough to find the median. The most important thing is not the number of people but the interview, which is hard to moderate and hard to ask general open questions in (how was it, what do you think of this feature, what did you expect to see, why did you press this button — you must ask about motivation). Observe the testing in real time and with the development team, and you can go further and measure the respondents' breathing and heart rate; testing smartphones is harder, since you must create natural conditions so the person moves the phone naturally, and tools like UX Recorder, Dscout, and Chalkmark help, with secondary research (finding ready studies) often coming to the rescue. A more realistic and accessible process can run card sorting → tree testing → paper prototypes → wireframes → unmoderated testing → moderated testing.

You should get qualitative data from an interview — qualitative being the answer to "Why?", what people say and what they actually do in spite of their words — while quantitative data is about numbers, the answer to "How much?" Frame the task situationally, from life, and agree with the respondent in advance that their negative feedback will help us improve the product, which gives them the motivation to criticise. Nielsen's heuristics are useless in today's competitive market, since shallow analysis allows too many errors, and guerrilla (hallway) testing and the friends-and-family test are no good either, because you will not find your target audience in a hallway or at home — you can test interface hypotheses there, but certainly not product hypotheses.

By the end of testing you should reach clear conclusions, for example: schoolchildren and adults pay the same total, but schoolchildren pay little and often and adults rarely but much; people tend to refuse a choice and leave the default; no one reads tutorials, and hints are read only when trouble arises; a page with 6 colours sells far more than one with 24, though interest is equal; client-side response time should be measured (or at least server-side); the gear icon is pressed less often than the word "settings"; and Roboto behaves badly on Windows desktops.

The result of testing is not only an improved product but a useful, measurable plus for the designer's work — though, unfortunately, only cost reduction measures well, at least in my experience, since every project I improved cut costs by reducing the support headcount, while the other metrics can be shared equally among marketers, TV advertising, and programmers who improved the system's quality. You cannot judge an interface by metrics alone, because if the app crashes users leave, and that is not the designer's fault — and a 100 ms delay in page load at a large store costs 1 % of sales. But design quality can be measured, and you must learn to do it: if in 99 % of cases the designer's errors are small miscommunications, details not explained well enough to the user visually or in text, the design quality is good. Learn from statistics on a regression-analysis model, and if you have spent your life generating static mockups and the marketers won't let you near the statistics, Google's analytics help and other sources like SimilarWeb are worth studying.

## Why all of this

To avoid mistakes that kill the client's business. A bit of history, with Microsoft as the example. Mobile devices went badly for them from the start: by 1999 the market already held Palm, Symbian, and the Apple Newton, and Microsoft decided to enter with its WinCE OS. Despite a familiar interface, a heap of money, and plenty of software, Microsoft ignored working with the hardware makers (Hewlett-Packard, ASUS, Dell), which kept it from becoming a noticeable player; in time it understood the problem and began working with HTC, but a second problem appeared — the company did not know who its target audience was and tried to capture both business and ordinary consumers at once, forced to compete simultaneously with Symbian (Nokia) and BlackBerry (RIM). On top of that there were many usability errors: WinMo communicators needed two hands to use, one of which had to hold a stylus. Then in 2007 the iPhone arrived with a large 3.5-inch screen and a familiar web free of limited WAP sites, showing what a mobile device should be. A competent product designer would have foreseen this and steered the company's efforts, through the C-level, toward the market segments where it could lead.

![Microsoft's mobile missteps](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/5858.jpg)

The value of designers to business is now beyond dispute (thanks to Apple), but the development team will appreciate a good designer's results too: after release the project scales easily, because a product specialist accounts for the product's long-term growth, whereas solving narrow tasks gives a "narrow solution" if you ignore growth and expansion — bad for business. A product designer makes the product easy to maintain, raises the team's motivation, and removes irreplaceable specialists (everything structured, a low barrier to entry with good documentation), lets you quickly reuse what was built, and with the right architecture lets you react to any market change very fast; technical people are more motivated, since any techie finds it far more interesting to build something cool and correct to be proud of (especially if they are genuinely good — they are usually motivated by default). The upshot is a higher chance of explosive growth — a bold claim but a well-founded one: if the vectors point the same way they rush toward the goal, multiplying one another; if they point different ways, they cancel out.

Where is design thinking applied in business? In designing the company's structure — who reports to whom and who is responsible for what — which cuts staff turnover and lets the company grow fast without undermining its foundations, principles, and mission; in organising business processes — how tasks are set and controlled, how results are assessed, how staff are bonused, how finances are distributed, how work with partners is run. It helps solve not only business problems but ecological, social, healthcare, and education ones too — for example, since digital professions develop so fast that a five-year second degree would teach knowledge already obsolete by graduation, universities should give fundamental, non-obsolescing knowledge (mathematics, drawing, composition), and a design-thinking session can devise an alternative, faster mode of education. States love design thinking for designing infrastructure, roads, approaches to education, benefit systems, systems for motivating private business, and methods of attracting foreign capital.

And now the downsides of combining the product-designer role with design-thinking approaches: time and high effort. The information architect's work is an investment in the long term, very hard to get right the first time and doubly hard to survive the problems and shortcomings during implementation — but it is the only possible process for building a large business, and a small one (a startup) can work as an information architect too. High competition demands being a step ahead of rivals or at least level with them, and it is the information architect who solves that.

## The design toolkit: Sketch and Figma

Mind maps and transition maps can be drawn several ways: draw all the mockups in any editor, save them as JPEGs, launch Illustrator, and place every screen as an image (File → Place), so editing the JPEGs updates the Illustrator files automatically — the same trick works with Photoshop and Axure — or use a mind-mapping service like draw.io. To do everything in one program, UserFlows helps in Sketch; for Figma there are UX-flow kits (many free), and Figma itself draws arrows, while Overflow supports Figma if you would rather not work by hand. If a source won't load into Figma with a WebGL error, enable GPU rendering: in Chrome go to `chrome://flags` and enable "Override software rendering list," and in Firefox set `webgl.force-enabled` to `true` in `about:config`.

Keeping a Sketch project sane is mostly about symbols. To find and tidy them, symbol-instance-locator finds the symbols used and changed in a scene, merge-duplicate-symbols merges duplicates, and symbol-and-artboard-organizer sorts them neatly on the page; when pasting a screen between documents adds duplicate symbols to the library even though they're identically named, symbol-swapper resolves it. To corral the two-dozen-odd plugins everyone accumulates, use Sketchrunner (or simply Cmd+Shift+/). For a fresh workstation I always install Relabel Button, Shared Text Styles, Rename It, Autolayout (Anima), and Craft. Russian-keyboard shortcuts that stop working are fixed with sketch-lang-fix, and the macOS analogue of the Windows "character map" is summoned with Ctrl+Cmd+Space. A few more keyboard tricks live in the box: resizing an object toward a chosen side is done by appending the side's first letter after the `+value` — T, B, L, R, or C (top, bottom, left, right, centre) — rounding all fractional pixel values is Layer → Round to Pixel (worth binding to a shortcut via System Preferences → Keyboard → Shortcuts → App Shortcuts), the arrow-key nudge step is set through the plist from the terminal, and twenty-odd shades each of red, blue, and grey can be consolidated with the sketch-replace-colour plugin or with Craft's Create Styles → edit → Sync Styles. To check Sketch's cache and clear it, `sudo purge` works globally, or `sudo du -sh /.DocumentRevisions-V100` just for Sketch.

Moving mockups between tools is awkward but doable. From Sketch to Photoshop, use Affinity Designer as an intermediary — copy the artboard in Sketch, then in Affinity Designer choose File → New from Clipboard and export as PSD (every text and vector layer becomes raster) — or open the `.sketch` file in Photopea and re-save as PSD (it won't manage large mockups); to Illustrator it is simpler, since pasting a Sketch selection into Photoshop as a Smart Object opens nicely in Illustrator on a double-click, and Avocode can convert PSD to Sketch. Opening a source saved from a newer Sketch in an older version is best left to Sketch support, but the risky route is to open the file in a text editor or WinRAR and edit `meta.json`, changing `appVersion`, `build`, and `version` to the older build's values (back it up first).

Components and overrides are the backbone of a scalable library. In Sketch you place a solid colour, an icon, or a component into an override and switch it in the interface by any depth of nesting, and the same approach changes text colour or opacity. Figma's component system is broadly similar but more flexible through components nested in components nested in components: hold Alt to swap components within components (handy for icons and avatars), use text styles as components, and recolour by making a colour swatch a component for which an icon component is the mask. The organising principle many in the industry share is to split components across pages, then create frames holding the relevant components, adding hierarchy with slashes (three levels is plenty), so you get `Buttons (page) → Appbar (frame) → Button name`; the first page holds all the styles in separate frames (Colours, Typography, Effects), the next pages hold larger entities (Icons, Buttons, Inputs) and then big components (Appbars, Toolbars, Cards), all published as a Team Library, with components named in BEM style like `Button_Primary_Normal`. For managing whole icon sets there are geticonjar, svgsus, nucleoapp, and lingoapp. And for the mundane macOS touches, you can add a spacer between Dock icons with `defaults write com.apple.dock persistent-apps -array-add '{"tile-type"="spacer-tile";}'; killall Dock` (or `small-spacer-tile` for a narrower gap).

## Grids and screen sizes

Adapting an iOS interface to the 5.5-inch iPhone is not strictly necessary, though it sometimes makes sense: the slices for 5.5 inches export at @3x and for the 4.x screens at @2x, so the 1.5× difference compensates for the screen resize, which works if you do everything in points and let Xcode handle it. Sometimes you make a separate size set for the iPhone Plus — on one project I designed four base metrics for a chat photo preview (compact 157×157, normal 186×186, wide-plus 309×309, huge-tablet 262×262), since discrete values are easy to work with: the tablet uses huge, multi-window mode switches to wide, and wide also serves the iPhone 6 Plus; if that is hard, make a maximum and minimum window size and let the components resize.

Slicing for the iPhone without running into the screen edges means a lot of numbers. For the 6/6s/7/8 Plus, keep images no larger than 2208×1110 and always check that width and height divide by 3 (in points, 736×370); for the iPhone X, 2172×870 px or 724×290 points, accounting for the content-safe area. The 6s Plus at 401 ppi uses @3x resources at a virtual 2208×1242 (736×414 points) and then compresses to 84 %; newer phones use Super Retina HD (iPhone X, 1125×2436 at 458 ppi), Retina HD 5.5 (the Plus models, 1242×2208), and Retina HD 4.7 (iPhone 6, 750×1334), and most companies' designers draw at 4.7.

![iPhone screen-size and density reference](http://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/rosreestr.png)

With the iPhone 11 the default canvas shifted: the slightly odd 375×812 (1125×2436 at ×3) still applies to the iPhone 11 Pro, while the 11 Pro Max is drawn at 414×896 (×3 = 1242×2688) and the plain iPhone 11 at 414×896 (×2 = 828×1792). 414 is more attractive, since more new iPhones will have that resolution and 414 sits comfortably on the familiar step-of-2 grid; 375 stays relevant if you have many users on X/Xs/11 Pro, but it forces a 5-px grid (or a soft grid if you must hit 8/4 px).

On the Material Design grid versus Apple's: ideologically, Material's "magic paper" uses print-design grids built from guides that set edge margins to steer the reader's eye and structure information. Horizontally, the classic Material step is 8 dp, with a 72 dp left inset and 16 dp elsewhere, and 16 dp from the screen edges; vertically, the status bar is 24 dp, the toolbar 56 dp, a subheader 48 dp, a list item 72 dp, and the floating button 56 dp.

![The Material Design layout grid](http://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2017/08/идеи-2.png)

The difference from Apple's grid lives at a lower level. Element placement is described by border, margin, padding, and the element's own size — padding being the inset from an element's edges to its children, margin the free space to neighbours — all of which should bring the design to pixel-perfection, the fonts being the exception since they always carry aliasing, and an 8-px grid often adds a 4-pt baseline grid. Google is a "hard grid" with a fixed 8-px step, because Material is designed around 4 pt; Apple is closer to a "soft grid," where 8 px (or any value) is fixed only between certain elements and the rest is at the designer's discretion, possibly a consequence of resolutions like the iPhone 6's 375×667 — and where 1366×768 and 1024×768 work fine with 8 px, 1600×900 suits a 10-px step better. At a higher level of abstraction, Apple follows geometric construction on relative proportions (the golden section and the like) while Google uses algebraic principles (a hard grid, a multiple of 8).

## Metrics, analytics, and the numbers behind the work

The first metrics that matter are the cost of acquiring one user and how much money they bring over their whole life — **CAC** and **LTV**, a vital part of unit economics (in games LTV should be half the game's lifespan). LTV is mandatory to compute, for example on a subscription model (average cheque × number of deals over a period × average retention time), and must exceed CPA. Cost of acquisition per user is acquisition spend ÷ users: start the period with 800 users, end with 1,000 (a gain of 200), spend 40,000 ₽, and 40,000 ÷ 200 = 200 ₽ per user. CAC alone says little without conversion — at 2 % conversion and a 500 ₽ CAC, 500 × 2 % = 10 ₽ to acquire to the site — and CAC is a variable that floats with the number acquired, usually counting only direct marketing spend. **ARPPU** (revenue ÷ paying clients) gives the average cheque per paying user — 400,000 ₽ ÷ 638 = 626 ₽ — while **ARPU** (revenue ÷ active users) is 400,000 ₽ ÷ 1,548 = 258 ₽ (or, alternatively, quarterly revenue ÷ MAU). **Retention Rate** shows how often users return (not part of unit economics, but the product team drives growth through it), alongside the standard **DAU**, **WAU**, and **MAU**; the **sticky factor** (DAU ÷ MAU) shows engagement (10,000 ÷ 10,000 = 100 %, ideal), and the inverse churn rate should be negative. CAC payback is CAC ÷ (MRR − ACS). Cohort analysis of WAU tracks the health of user growth — if users arrive but total active users by WAU don't rise, trouble is coming.

![Unhealthy user-growth cohorts](http://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Group-119.png)

A good metric is comparable, understandable, quickly measurable, and decision-ready, and for prioritising metrics there are frameworks: the pirate AAARRR funnel (heard of the product → visited → did the task → paid → recommended); HEART, about users and the value to them (task completion, engagement, satisfaction); and the classic PULSE, a ready list of metrics for technical products.

When the revenue at a shoe store falls, the question is really about conversion (purchases ÷ visitors × 100 — say 49 ÷ 870 × 100 = 5.6 %), influenced by site usability, traffic quality, and much else. Advertising affects new visitors while a product change affects everyone, so first compare the conversion of different cohorts: if it shifted for all of them, it is a usability effect, so look at OS-plus-browser and resolution-plus-browser and watch behaviour through a session recorder, since the site likely misbehaves in a certain browser (a hypothesis for the testers); if only the newest cohort changed, it may be an advertising effect, perhaps an ad that didn't render in some browser or was set on Chrome users and was off-putting. Check the metric counting on the dashboard, then the historical data for seasonality, then which cohort lost money and whether it was conversion or revenue. When the numbers from different ad tools don't agree, decide by how much and on what attribution (last click), then tune the campaign — turning off semantics with low CR and ROI, expanding the ads, filling in negative keywords, cleaning placements — before strengthening it.

Tracking offline visitors without CRM access is done by geolocation — cell towers, Wi-Fi, GPS if the user permitted it — so a device lingering in your store's building marks its owner a likely buyer (Wi-Fi sharpens accuracy in a multi-tenant building); the metric is PVL (place-visits lift). The ROPO effect can be tracked with Consumer Barometer, joining online and offline by postal address or User ID, motivating sign-in with loyalty cards and promotions, and falling back on UTM tags and URL parameters, with the data usually unified in Google BigQuery. To build an e-commerce analytics system from scratch, first find the client's business goals (usually lead generation), start with free third-party data collection, hire a data miner when resources allow, rent storage like Amazon when the data accumulates, and build a dashboard for management from a metrics pyramid whose few top indicators tell the whole story, drilling down to an event map when needed; a wrong decision at the metric level kills the business, so a regression A/B test (deliberately worsening UX to see which metrics react most) finds the metrics to work with. Often you need call tracking plus a CRM plus Google Analytics, splitting offline ad types and assigning a unique short phone number per type, all built toward a Data Warehouse where logs flow to cloud storage, are processed (Elastic, Hadoop), parsed into columns, and visualised in BI systems.

Tracking scroll depth uses the non-interaction event in Google Analytics (scroll depth depends on performance, often tied to compositing layers, given interesting content), and in Yandex.Metrica you create a Scroll trigger in GTM and send a `reachGoal` event, or view it per page in the scroll map. For a simple trend forecast without programming, plot recent sales in Excel, right-click the line, Add Trendline, and tick "Display Equation" and "Display R-squared" — R² near 1 means the forecast is trustworthy.

![Adding a trendline in Excel](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/Cup-1.png)

For comfortable viewing angles on large screens, cinemas set screen height at half the distance to the farthest row (reducible by 20 %), which applies to monitors too, while complex information stays comfortable within ±15 degrees of the centre of vision; the distance to an on-screen object follows s = 0.536 × d, where s is the object's size and d the eye-to-screen distance.

## Working the data: Excel and dashboards

Joining data by a shared user ID across spreadsheets is the VLOOKUP job (`=VLOOKUP(lookup_value, table, column_number, [range_lookup])`): pick the lookup value in the leftmost column (the UID), select the source table, give the column number, and choose FALSE/0 for an exact match, locking ranges with absolute references (F4) so they don't drift.

![A VLOOKUP across spreadsheets](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2017/08/bigwall2-copy-10.png)

If numbers loaded as text and VLOOKUP won't treat them as numbers, coerce them inside the formula — `=VLOOKUP(D2+0, A:B, 2, 0)`, or double-negation `--D2`, `D2*1`, `D2+0`, `D2^1`. To find the nearest smaller value use `=VLOOKUP(C1, A2:A8, 1, 1)`, and for the nearest larger, INDEX+MATCH is faster: `=INDEX(A2:A8, MATCH(C1, A2:A8, -1))` (match type 0 for an exact match, -1 for smallest, 1 for largest). Splitting "Ivanov Ivan Ivanovich" into three columns is Data → Text to Columns, and joining back is `=A:A&" "&B:B&" "&C:C` or `=CONCATENATE(...)`. Sorting by several conditions and outputting only unique values is the Advanced Filter — duplicate a column header for several AND conditions on one column, put conditions on separate rows for OR — and to get unique records, tick "Copy to another location" and "Unique records only" with no conditions set.

![An Excel Advanced Filter](http://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/Routes_44.png)

For dashboards, Google Data Studio builds simple ones from Google Sheets, Analytics, AdWords, BigQuery, or Cloud SQL — enough for top-level analytics to spot anomalies and decide where to dig — and links to Excel from Word via Paste Special (tick "Link," hotkey Ctrl+Alt+V), updating through File → Info → Edit Links to Files, or Alt+F9 to reveal the field codes and repoint all the Excel sources at once. In BigQuery, a 7- or 30-day report is best handled by partitioning the table on creation, which adds a `_PARTITIONTIME` column you query with `WHERE _PARTITIONTIME = "2018-01-01"` or, for a rolling window, `WHERE _PARTITIONTIME BETWEEN TIMESTAMP_TRUNC(TIMESTAMP_SUB(CURRENT_TIMESTAMP(), INTERVAL 7 * 24 HOUR), DAY) AND TIMESTAMP_TRUNC(CURRENT_TIMESTAMP(), DAY)`.

## Interface patterns and feedback

Pull-to-refresh is a solution for lists and card interfaces where items sort strictly in descending order — a news feed or a mail list — with the refresh indicator appearing under the screen header; it does **not** suit maps (no primary content source to refresh) and should not be shoved onto every screen (a weather forecast doesn't change minute to minute), with Google Play, Duolingo, and the phone contacts list as good examples. An ordinary Refresh button is sometimes added, common in enterprise apps, more obvious but to be justified by your audience's statistics, because the right choice depends on your users' digital-skill level — in a study of 216,000 people aged 16–65, only 5 % could use modern technology well, 14 % could perform a simple chain of actions, 26 % understood something, 29 % could handle email, and the remaining 26 % could not use a computer at all.

Swipe-for-action on Android (the Material "Leave-behinds" control) I find insufficiently obvious; bottom sheets suit contextual actions better, or the Inbox approach — swipe to act, long-press for a menu and multi-select. The Material grid step is 8 dp throughout, the gutter and margins built from it. For marking required versus optional fields, mark whichever there are fewer of: if most fields are required and one is optional, label the optional one "optional field," and otherwise mark required fields with an asterisk or a bold label. For preview sizing on Twitter, keep illustrations at 16:9 (or 8:9, which crops cleanly), since the platform mangles other ratios.

A word on the design sprint, since it is the cousin of the design-thinking session: five steps over five days — Understand (talk, interview, user-centric), Ideate (each participant proposes solutions), Decide (discuss and pick one), Prototype (build a testable artefact), Test — plus the sprint retrospective. And when enough ideas have gathered, choose the one to build by asking how fast it can be done, how significant the positive effect is, how simple, how risky, how costly, and what intuition says — the "low-hanging fruit" being a solution that needs few resources for great return. Giving feedback to the person whose idea didn't win has two popular models. **COIN** (Context, Observation, Impact, Next steps) is given privately and from the manager's standpoint ("I noticed…," "I think…"): I noticed you're not taking part in the design system (Context); I think you dislike the style we're heading toward (Observation); but because of that your products aren't in the rebrand, and we want a unified portfolio (Impact); so let's discuss what you dislike and have you start contributing (Next steps). **BOFF** (Behaviour, Outcome, Feelings, Future): you're late delivering the design (Behaviour); it delayed the release and a competitor shipped the feature first (Outcome); that upsets me because it reflects on the design department's reputation (Feelings); what will you do so it doesn't recur — because if it does, fines will follow (Future)?
