# Preparing Specifications for Multiplatform Products
### What separates a production that survives contact with reality from one that doesn't — and the documentation that makes the difference

*Maksim Tcvetkov · Product Design and Security Lead*

All successful digital productions are alike; every unsuccessful one is unsuccessful in its own way. Which raises the obvious question: what actually separates the successful productions from the rest? The quality of the project's preparation. Successful productions study their subject matter thoroughly and exhaustively, minimizing the number of open questions once design begins — that's the whole secret. Cooper made much the same point when he wrote that specifications are the best way for a programmer and a designer to communicate with each other. But even once a designer has written the documentation and built specifications with real depth, they still have to stay continuously involved through development — at minimum, half of any project changes along the way, and every one of those accompanying changes has to be documented by the designer too. Build specifications, and beyond a successful project, you also get a working way to survive the bureaucracy around it.

Say you're in the following situation: your designer is sitting in Photoshop, Sketch, or Illustrator, working out the overall stylistic direction. That's good — there's no getting around it, it's the foundation of the profession. But the outcome needs to be a product, not just a set of mockups. So that endlessly maintaining a repetitive design doesn't demand enormous effort, and so developers can handle much of that maintenance themselves, your specialists need to build proper specifications. A specification is a document — in Confluence or any other wiki — that holds the following sections.

## The ecosystem concept

The services a company provides are multichannel by nature, and at the same time, every device delivering those services differs — different screens, different user cases. Take a self-service checkout kiosk: it has a scanner, a payment PIN pad, a fiscal register, a scale, and people only glance at its screen in short bursts, so linear information delivery doesn't work there. What's needed instead is persistent imagery, error sounds, voice prompts, video. Car interfaces, meanwhile, shouldn't draw attention to themselves at all. Even the familiar markets carry plenty of nuance, though Bootstrap and Foundation handle a good chunk of the problem, helping describe design principles in code through "living" guidelines. When you open a site on mobile in certain browsers and see nothing at all, it's usually because the developer or designer built the site around a client-side templating engine, and nothing renders because dynamic JavaScript isn't running. Even a familiar icon font (switch to SVG instead), rounded corners, and gradients don't work in proxy browsers. Smartwatch browsers can't use a keyboard at all — that lives on the phone. Add [smart TV](https://your-scorpion.ru/advanced_smatrtv_design/) problems on top, and the people who live in mobile-only. A mobile site isn't defined by screen width — it's defined by different user behavior. An iPhone user needs information fast. If large touchscreens are planned (a small kiosk or a wall-mounted display), it's worth accounting for the screen being rotated 45 degrees to reduce how visible entered information is to bystanders, and shrinking sensitive data like an email address so it can't be read from a distance — along with the viewing distance itself and the reach of an arm's movement.

Everything listed above is only about 5% of the technical nuance of multiplatform design that I'm even aware of, and no more than 1% of what actually needs to be accounted for on a single product within a single ecosystem. You might say all of this can't possibly be documented — but it really isn't as bad as it sounds. It comes down to answering two questions: how should information be presented, and how do you minimize the cognitive load on the user?

In game products, the UX specialist provides not just a screen map, but documentation covering every animation, sound, and device vibration too — the artists are left to finish the UI itself.

Documenting an ecosystem concept like this is exactly what helps you avoid a heroic ground-up redesign every year or two. If a new art director shows up and says "everything here is a mess, we need to redo it from scratch," send them packing — they're padding their own portfolio, not their client's wallet. The same instinct shows up in a product manager chasing challenges for their own sake. If a redesign isn't done incrementally (visual work, then technology, with a quick rollback path if something breaks) and without a clear picture of who it's actually for and what metrics it's meant to move, something's already gone wrong in the process. This article is about documenting a project properly as a designer, and I'm happy to share my own template for building project guidelines.

### Introduction

A glossary, the project's goals, an overview — because we've all read about the diff between a developer's plans and a client's expectations.

### Audience and personas

The audience's goals and tasks, and segmentation. Build out personas. Regardless of whether your segmentation came from intuition or from hard numbers, work out exactly which users your product will actually be used by. Don't be afraid to push assumed user behavior toward the extremes — hang pictures of these invented personas up in front of you and run your research with them in mind. You need a photo, a profile, a name, nationality, age, personal traits, the operating systems and ecosystems they use, their goals, their motivations, how susceptible they are to public opinion, their life mottos — basically, you need the equivalent of a social-media profile for a real person. And don't forget the conclusion: this person needs our service to achieve some specific outcome, and they'll use it according to a particular logic of their own. Shopping-comparison aggregators, for instance, aren't only for comparing products — they're also for checking discounts, or simply killing time at the office. Customer development shouldn't be forgotten either — testing an idea or a prototype of the future product against real potential customers. This doesn't take many resources, since a proper qualitative study (as opposed to a quantitative one) only needs 6±2 respondents from the target group.

For every persona you've built, work out and sketch the ways they'd actually use the service, along with their search scenarios (product-search scenarios, for instance). Sketching a screen starts from its goals — what task that screen solves for a given persona. To avoid producing an endless pile of mockups, some of them can simply be described in words instead. It's worth keeping the news hook in mind too, for positioning a new product in the market.

Don't forget users who are blind, low-vision, colorblind, dealing with cognitive impairments, or affected by motor disabilities — together these make up around 10% of users, with more than 300 million people unable to perceive color and roughly as many again dealing with various vision problems. These groups engage purely with content, and reach it through screen magnifiers, keyboards, Braille displays, audio readers, and font and contrast settings. Take any popular screen reader — JAWS (paid) or NVDA (free) — turn your monitor off, and try to get information off your site. Then go through and insert the `aria-label` tag with appropriate text wherever it's needed, and try again, repeating until you can work with the site normally without a monitor at all. After that, test with other screen readers too, like ZoomText, ChromeVox, and Serotek.

Always leave focus states on form fields intact — don't touch the outline settings. There's a useful [checklist](http://a11yproject.com/checklist.html) to set your conscience at ease before handing work to a client. Keep `tabindex` logical and negative where needed. Colorblind users can see green and red as identical, so every error needs to be marked with text, not color alone. Blind users will be grateful for a correctly set `lang` attribute, for an actual `<button>` or link instead of a `<div>` playing the role of one, and for properly structured `h1`–`h6` headings.

### Business and data

The business model and business processes. Use the logos of clients who are actually trusted. Analyze every real table, parameter, and characteristic, along with XML/JSON carrying real content — the prototype should only ever use real data too. A laptop has one set of specs, a smartphone has similar but different ones, and filtering by those parameters has to be handled differently for each. Don't forget visual and non-visual accessibility of information with images turned off (alt text for blind users, among others), print versions, and everything else that falls under a11y. It's worth understanding that blind users rely on programs that read page content aloud, screen readers like JAWS among them — and screen readers read everything indiscriminately, including the text inside an image's alt attribute. Which is exactly why stuffing that attribute with a list of keywords does real damage to part of your audience.

Check that the API is properly documented. At minimum, it should include:

- a request model (not required for REST, but required for gRPC; XSD for SOAP; a schema for GraphQL)
- the data-exchange format (JSON, XML, protobuf)
- an example curl call, and the request body for REST
- input parameters: what's passed in the query string, the path, the headers, and the request body
- output data
- response codes

People with dyslexia shouldn't be forgotten either. US regulatory guidance recommends the following typefaces: Times New Roman, Verdana, Arial, Tahoma, Helvetica, and Calibri — driven by the simple goal of using fewer decorative flourishes in the letterforms. For better coverage of reading-impaired users' needs, it's worth looking at [Read Regular](http://www.readregular.com/english/regular.html), [Lexie Readable](http://www.k-type.com/fonts/lexie-readable/), and [Tiresias](https://www.fontsquirrel.com/fonts/tiresias-infofont). Beyond that: text should never be an image, use less red and green, keep sufficient contrast against the background, and don't play with italics or bold weight without real reason.

On other accessibility considerations, blind users rely on screen readers (JAWS on PC, VoiceOver plus rotor gestures on iOS, NVDA and TalkBack on Android); users with reduced vision use Magic or ZoomText. Motor-impairment problems get solved through alternative input methods — a head pointer, a single-switch device, motion tracking, and of course voice input. Designing for VoiceOver can be done in tools like VoiceOver Designer, and design-system components should carry the right markup by default. If a user is deaf, it's worth accounting for video calling with a specialist fluent in your local sign language. And basic requirements matter too — a minimum of 26px for body text on a 1080p screen, for instance.

Visual accessibility problems can be split into color, contrast, hierarchy, sizing, animation, and form handling. WCAG helps establish a baseline set of requirements, though we don't rely on WCAG completely — it occasionally produces some fairly strange results:

![WCAG contrast example](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Group-37.jpg)

If the product will be multilingual, document the key information used in the product in every language it needs to support. Not everything gets translated — motorcycle and car model names stay as-is, while city names do get translated (Russia → Россия). Transliteration isn't acceptable. It's worth keeping in mind that most of the Russian audience rates their own English proficiency as "below average" (per the EF EPI rankings), so the impact of untranslated text on comprehension can be serious.

### Analytics

This is where every report, metric, market study, card sort, touchpoint, and conclusion gets collected — hypotheses, descriptions, and the market conditions the analysis was based on. If you've built a mockup in Photoshop, use sticky notes to briefly note why a given decision was made; the rest of the visual team needs this, and it helps a great deal in heated arguments later. You need well-thought-out scenarios — which persona is walking through which scenario (pulled from the Audience section), and how cross-channel conversion is expected to behave. Conversion can be absolute or relative: absolute conversion is N steps from the very first step, while relative conversion only looks at two specific steps in isolation.

An anxious person, for instance, deserves an orange button, since orange reads as urgency (see fast food); someone who needs everything calm and deliberate is better served by blue or green. We already have the project's goal and the user's expectations, but real impressions from actually using the product only show up much later — and those conclusions get filed into the analytics section too: what worked well, and, crucially, what didn't. Analytics data is worth collecting and storing in something like Amazon Redshift, or simply MySQL, or even PostgreSQL — PostgreSQL suits large projects especially well, since it supports cluster scaling and table sharding. It's worth budgeting for a recommendation engine too. Having a UML editor on hand during the analytics phase saves a great many frayed nerves.

A related, practical concern worth folding in here — since the audience for this section is precisely the people who need clean analytics data to draw the right conclusions from — is keeping your own staff and clients out of the analytics in the first place, so they don't quietly pollute the numbers. The simplest fix is excluding traffic by internal IP address, provided the address is static; choosing an exact-match filter excludes just one IP, while other match types let you exclude a whole range at once. Where IP addresses are dynamic instead, you can ask people to install a browser [extension](https://tools.google.com/dlpage/gaoptout) that blocks data from reaching Google Analytics in the first place. A more flexible, if more involved, approach uses Google Tag Manager: tag staff members against their user IDs once they're logged in, and block analytics data from being sent the moment that identifier shows up. Those tags need to live in a cookie, and since users sometimes clear cookies, it's worth duplicating the tag into local storage as a backup.

On the visualization side, when you need to compare how several intermediate factors — some positive, some negative — each contributed to a final value, a waterfall chart (a cascade chart) is the right tool; it's built into Excel 2016 out of the box, and in older versions a stacked bar chart can stand in, though it demands careful data formatting to work correctly.

### Concept

This is where the product's core idea lives, along with the information architecture and the general principles of interaction. This section describes functions, already paired with prototypes, and it needs to come before the UI kit is described, since in design, function dictates form, and form dictates color. This is also the right place for genuinely foundational things, like the grid — the 8-pixel grid Google champions is the most popular choice right now, and that 8-pixel step is especially convenient because it divides so cleanly by 2 (giving you 2-pixel corner radii, say, or just making it simpler to cut pixel-perfect vector icons). iOS is built on an 11-pixel base, Windows 8 on a 5-pixel one. If every product launches on the exact same set of components, unification comes bundled with a certain sameness — such services end up looking like identical twins. The platform, then, needs to allow for stylistic variation across products without touching the underlying principles the components run on. For mobile apps, those components typically arrive as bundles — distributable libraries with the design already baked in. The doctype gets settled with the developer ahead of time — XHTML 1.0 or HTML5. If you're using a CMS, the choice matters too: WordPress or ModX for small sites and blogs, Joomla! or Drupal for larger portals, Drupal or LiveStreet for social networks, and Magento or Joomla! for online stores.

### The logic of standard functions

MVP. Convenience comes before simplicity of form, which is why the standard-functions-logic stage begins well before the UI kit. This is where you describe everything the user should understand intuitively, or at least without having to think about it — navigation, the catalog, a bonus system, registration. Impressions formed at the intuitive level feed directly into emotion, and that's worth using deliberately: always check whether ready-made solutions already exist for whatever task you're solving, since that helps guarantee the intuitiveness of the resulting functionality. Color scheme also gets worked out based on function: bright [colors](https://your-scorpion.ru/introduction-to-color-light/) automatically trigger positive emotion, the way loud noise creates discomfort. Based on both function and color scheme, you need to establish standards for photos, avatars, ad space, and other content graphics (image proportions, standard sizes, and their variations across resolutions get nailed down in later sections). Finally, work out the project's expected speed and its behavior depending on internet connectivity. A lot of this can be checked with dedicated [tools](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ER/tools/?q=wcag-20-w3c-web-content-accessibility-guidelines-20).

### Screens

The product's final design — the largest and most labor-intensive section, easily more than 70% of the whole document. By this stage, everything should be not just thought through but genuinely beautiful, since beauty is one of design's key components, triggering pleasure in people through its effect on unconscious associations. Prototypes are worth building when there's real money and time to spend, or when the use case is genuinely unclear (in games, say) — but a prototype isn't a mandatory part of development. If you do decide to build one, it needs to be interactive and answer specific questions. For Photoshop work: push every raster layer into a smart object, keep every size and every font as a multiple of two (a designer should choose sizes by consistent rule, never by eye), and keep all other graphics in vector form, at least for responsive websites — later, you'll turn that into an SVG that can hold a handful of raster images inside it during development. You sometimes end up building many screens: you build a 320px screen, and the client says everything looks blurry on their current phone, so you build a 640px screen for them, and now the developer complains and demands 320px back.

This is exactly the territory where device density becomes unavoidable, and it's worth spelling out properly, since screens vary enormously across manufacturers and platforms.

**Screen size** is a device's actual physical size, expressed in inches (1 inch = 2.54cm).

**Screen resolution** is the number of pixels on the screen — more pixels means a crisper image. Popular sizes are 480×800, 720×1280, and 1080×1920. For a base mockup, a designer is better off starting from 720×1280, or 1080×1920 if building with an eye toward the future. It's fine to design mockups at 360×640px, which scale cleanly up to 200% DPI (720×1280, the HDTV standard) and up to 300% ppi (1080×1920, the 1080p standard). 720×1280 corresponds to 320 ppi, and within that resolution, 1dp = 2px, following the formula `px = dp × (dpi / 160)`. Worth understanding here: if an app is built around 320dp, it'll generally look good across multiple icon densities; built around 360dp or 480dp, text can occasionally fail to fit inside buttons.

It's worth remembering dp's own quirk: if you set a button's height to 16mm, that button will measure 16mm on both a 4-inch phone screen and a 10-inch tablet — and on a genuinely huge tablet, it'll start to look strange. Past a certain size, you need to design separate logic for tablets: thicken elements, widen spacing, and so on. Anything above 7 inches is conventionally treated as a tablet, since it's awkward to hold to your ear as a phone. A dedicated tablet design is genuinely important for exactly this reason — an app that simply stretches its phone content from 3 inches to 12 is a bad experience.

Ppi is the number of pixels per inch of screen, and this value exists both for the screen itself and for the operating system — the two need to match. On my 27-inch Mac, the ppi is 109 — 109 pixels per inch of screen. So on a 109ppi screen, an image reads smaller than it would on a 72ppi one, and the same logic applies across mobile devices. Every device has a native resolution, and that's what design should be built around.

**On fonts:** using the system font is recommended. On Android 4.x and above, that's Roboto; Android 2.x and 3.x use Droid Sans, with Droid Sans Fallback covering Asian scripts. [Noto](https://www.google.com/get/noto/) is also a safe choice. Android's recommended font sizes run 12sp, 14sp, 18sp, and 22sp.

Non-standard density values show up too, even if the vast majority of devices sit at fairly standard figures — a device like the Nexus 5X ran at 420 dpi, and the Nexus 6 and 6P at 560 dpi. Android 6 and 7 also let users change pixel density directly — on the Nexus 5X, for instance, the settings expose 356, 420 (default), 460, 500, and 540 dpi. Among non-standard values, 5.25 inches (2.57×4.57 inches) shows up most often, appearing at both 420 and 560 dpi and corresponding to 1080×1920 (FHD). The practical problems with non-standard dpi tend to be incorrect UI rendering in certain apps, and raster graphics getting pulled from the wrong resolution bucket and scaled awkwardly as a result. At 420 and 560 dpi specifically, you land on pixel values of 2.625 and 3.5 respectively — the same kind of fractional-pixel headache familiar to anyone who's cut HDPI assets at their 1.5-pixel density for 240 dpi.

The screen conversation doesn't stop at Android, either — iOS carries its own version of the same story, and it's worth walking through both together, since specs get handed off across both ecosystems constantly. In 2007, Apple introduced the iPhone with a 3.5-inch, 320×480 display — a resolution where designers worked in plain pixels. With the iPhone 4's Retina display, resolution doubled to 640×960, and a point started corresponding to two pixels on Retina screens. Points exist specifically to make developers' lives easier: resolution kept climbing (1136×1920 across various models, expressed as 320×480 for the iPhone 4/4S or 320×568 for the iPhone 5/5c), and the whole translation between points and pixels is handled by the system itself, invisibly.

320 became the standard baseline width for legibility, alongside 360 for modern HD Android phones — these values come out of a good deal of research, arm's-length reach among the factors considered, even though 320 isn't actually the single most common resolution in the wild; a service like [CSSpixels](http://www.canbike.org/CSSpixels/) makes that clear. When a Samsung Galaxy at 2960×1440 renders a web page, it renders it as if it were 320-wide, since so many sites are still built around that figure — the OS or browser reports a suitably scaled "physical" width to the page, and a responsive site renders at whatever width a designer chooses, since `device-width` itself can be overridden. When the iPhone 6 and 6+ arrived with larger screens, Apple introduced 375px and 414px rather than sticking with 320 — a consequence of the display's underlying pixel grid: a virtual pixel needs to be a whole-number multiple of a real one, or the resulting image won't be crisp. As physical pixel density keeps climbing on modern screens, virtual pixels only grow when the physical screen itself grows — in effect, the greater the distance between eyes and screen, the larger a "CSS pixel" needs to be. In CSS, a pixel's physical size is measured in millimeters, fixed, and differs by screen type; on Retina displays it ranges from about 0.11mm to 0.23mm. Modern HD devices mostly settle around two standard virtual resolutions, 320 and 360, with non-integer multipliers like 2.37, 2.4, or 1.8 surviving only as legacy from older mobile devices and best ignored today. Apple, for its part, ended up with its own small zoo of 320, 375, and 414 — a reality worth simply making peace with.

A related, purely practical handoff detail: when a designer works in Retina and the developer building the layout doesn't, the developer mainly needs the raw sizes out of the PSD — send the mockup at 144dpi instead of the default 72, and most developers can flip between 144 and 72 themselves as their workflow needs.

Font metrics carry their own handoff quirk across platforms, worth flagging explicitly since it trips up almost every design-to-dev handoff at some point. A text block's bounding box in Sketch behaves nothing like a TextView's in Android — line spacing in Sketch, for instance, maps onto Android's `lineSpacingExtra` and `lineSpacingMultiplier` parameters, and the cleanest fix is building text from its baseline and making sure developers know that's the intended approach. On iOS, this is a somewhat easier problem, since Auto Layout lets you build text from the baseline directly, through the `firstBaseline` and `baseline` properties.

Beyond phones, the same density conversation naturally extends into modern breakpoints generally. A great many users have shifted almost entirely to Retina and 4K displays, and hit a kind of culture shock the moment they run into an ordinary Full HD screen by comparison — at 4K, antialiasing in games effectively stops being a visible problem at all. Plenty of people are already on Ultra High Definition (UHD, 4K, 3840×2160px), though most people will likely still be sitting on Full HD for another decade at least. A useful list of the resolutions that show up across cross-platform product statistics: Standard Definition (SD) 720×480; qHD 960×540; HD 1280×720; Full HD 1920×1080; 2K 2048×1152; Quad HD (QHD) 2560×1440; Quad Full HD (QFHD)/Ultra HD 3840×2160; and 4K 4096×2160.

For assets specifically, on Android, icons for a toolbar or menu items break down as: MDPI 48×48 (1×), HDPI 72×72 (1.5×), XHDPI 96×96 (2×), XXHDPI 144×144 (3×), and XXXHDPI 192×192 (4×) — where a 48×48px asset should read as roughly 32×32px, and 48px works out to about 13mm on a 135ppi screen (see the [Android icon guidelines](https://developer.android.com/guide/practices/ui_guidelines/icon_design_menu.html) for more). Sizing also depends on where a button sits in the layout: the bigger the phone, the smaller the comfortable one-handed reach area becomes. At the center of the screen, thumb accuracy runs around 12mm; toward the edges, it drops to about 7mm — which is exactly why buttons near the edges of a screen are conventionally made larger, or given a larger hit target than their visual size suggests.

And on the App Store side of asset delivery specifically: submissions typically need up to five screenshots per screen resolution (PNG without transparency, or JPEG). For iPhone: 4.7-inch at 1334×750, 5.5-inch at 1242×2208 (often sufficient on its own), 4-inch at 1136×640, and 3.5-inch at 960×640. For iPad: 1024×768, 2048×1536, 768×1024, and 1536×2048.

Designing across Apple's own device family carries its own set of conventions worth spelling out too, since it's genuinely its own small ecosystem within the larger one. Input methods stay largely the same across iPhone and iPad; the Apple Watch adds touch plus the Digital Crown, while Apple TV is driven by a remote rather than direct on-screen manipulation — Force Touch aside, the overall feel of getting something done stays consistent with iPhone. The iPhone 6 Plus in landscape starts to echo an iPad, and an iPad's Split View mode reads a lot like an elongated iPhone. A solid rule of thumb is designing for the smallest screen first, and layering in more information as screen size grows; Apple's own minimum touch target is 44 points, and sticking to that keeps you safe. Visual identity staying consistent across every device type matters enormously too — game developers rarely worry over every possible screen size individually, instead scaling the whole game to fit any screen, and that's a reasonable approach to borrow elsewhere. On raw sizes: 320×568pt (iPhone 5), 320×480pt (iPhone 4), 375×667pt (iPhone 6), and 414×736pt (iPhone 6 Plus); iPad runs 1024×1366pt (iPad Pro) and 768×1024pt (iPad). With Retina, 1pt became 2px — so points describe what shows on an iPhone, and pixels are simply those points expressed relative to pixel density. An Apple Watch app is a companion to the main iOS app, and comes in two sizes, 38mm and 42mm, each with its own resolution; the digital crown enables some genuinely interesting navigation mechanics, and while an accelerometer and gyroscope are both present, they're a step down from their iPhone counterparts. Elaborate graphics and Apple Watch don't always mix well either, given the roughly 50MB limit per app. Plenty of users treat the watch purely as a notification accessory, but building genuine app functionality into it can meaningfully lift retention — through the same standard levers that help retention generally: gradually unlocking new content, social features, push notifications and messaging, ongoing communication, and the Hook Model.

For game UI specifically, aspect ratio is its own design decision. Building a project around 16:9 has clear advantages, with the active gameplay area at 4:3 and everything else as set dressing — 16:9 shows all of it, 16:10 crops some of the dressing, 4:3 shows only the active zone, and 5:4 shows that same active zone slightly squeezed horizontally by the camera's aspect. Players run into every proportion imaginable — 4:3, 16:10, 3:2 — and can simply launch a game windowed and set whatever aspect they like; designing around 32:21, roughly halfway between 16:9 and 4:3, is one reasonable compromise. There's a real case for 4:3 too, with the iPad being the main reason. When drawing game UI, a designer needs smart objects configured properly, so that scaling all the way up to 3840×2160 never leaves an asset looking visibly stretched — designing at 1920×1080 (1080p) or 1280×720 (720p) both work, and it's just as reasonable to design directly for 21:9 from the start, moving from something like 3840×2160 (4K 16:9) down to 1920×1080 (2K 16:9), or 5120×2160 (4K 21:9) down to 2560×1080, or 2880×2160 (4K 4:3) down to 1440×1080 (2K 4:3). Designing at 4K and scaling down generally gives the best result.

### Texts

This section holds the copy for SMS, notifications, error messages, and tooltips, and describes the language's style and tone — the mood, and the degree of familiarity used toward the user. By this stage, buttons already need to be scalable and flexible enough to hold text while accounting for baseline conflicts (`overflow: hidden` or `vertical-align: middle`, though in practice this usually ends up as `display: inline-flex`, wrapped in the extra containers a preprocessor generates). A layout strategy needs to be chosen too: how many columns, and whether to build on `grid` or `flex`. The two are almost fully interchangeable, animation included — `flex` makes inverting column order trivial, and otherwise `grid` has largely displaced flex elsewhere. `Grid` tends to need more code; `flex` is the better fit for single-column or single-row layouts.

Never write long copy — 1,000 characters is a good ceiling. More than one comma in a sentence is a symptom of clip-thinking creeping in. People are willing to read a text newsletter regularly if it delivers real value, and that value usually boils down to a simple phrase: quickly find out the latest news, the best films playing nearby, discounts on things they care about. If your analytics show someone scrolling text down to the middle of the screen and moving their mouse across it, that's a good sign the text was actually read. A great deal of effort goes into working out edge cases and character-count limits, and getting real data into an interactive prototype quickly makes checking those limits far faster.

### UI Kit

This is where the founder of Lotus, Colin Chapman, is often paraphrased on the subject of design philosophy — simplify, then add lightness. By this point you have a broad-strokes architecture for the whole project, and if there's no overly precious prototype standing in the way, there are no artificial constraints left either — it's genuinely time to design. This tends to be built as one of the last stages and goes into a portfolio as static images; it covers form fields, lists, toggles, tables, charts, line weights, the color palette, methods for conveying depth, and rules for placement within the interface. By this point, interface icons should have grown into a genuinely universal set, with a guide developed for creating new ones — line angles, corner radii, the underlying icon metaphors. Illustrations and infographics need to fit the overall style, described as a standard brief for illustrators and designers to follow. Every source file needs to be ready for localization — text left un-rasterized, fonts attached. A UI kit isn't just a file showing the states of standard interface elements; it also covers things like notification delay timing and examples of how elements resize across different resolutions and screen orientations.

During development, a designer is expected to debug their own work directly in the browser — devtools are roughly the same, and simple enough, across every browser. Account for how controls respond to swipe, flick, drag, pinch, and un-pinch. All of this together is what's called component-based design (the BEM methodology). Raster graphics should be dropped wherever possible, since vector compresses and scales far better (Sketch and Illustrator both handle this well). Everything described here has to fit within the column grid. Once layout is finished, running the site through [Stylify Me](http://stylifyme.com) is a good way to confirm everything matches the guidelines.

A naming system can be pulled straight from BEM itself. As a matter of plain common sense, selectors and design components should carry names that actually mean something — `id="content"` is fine on the face of it and looks reasonable enough, but `content-about` is the more correct choice. Not `green-text`, but `positive-notification`. BEM sets a stricter bar than that: `categories-service__title` and `categories-service__description` sit at the same level as entities of a shared kind, while `popular-services__wrapper` is the entity that contains them — the double underscore always separates that relationship. There's also a modifier, governing an element's appearance, written with a single underscore, reflecting something like a pressed state or a style change. None of this is hard to grasp or slow to pick up.

BEM's core advantage is the ability to override cleanly. Level zero is the ecosystem, level one is the project, and level two is exactly where local overrides for color and anything else that didn't fit the design guides get handled. In React, overriding across levels gets somewhat more complicated, which is why BEM doesn't tend to get used there.

### Animation model and interactivity

Transitions between interface states and screens need to follow a consistent model — that's what keeps animation coherent and makes it easier for the user to understand where they are. This is the stage to finally decide whether popups slide in from the top of the screen and slide back out on close, or follow some other logic entirely, and at what speed and delay. If a block has more elements than can be displayed on screen at once, you need to decide: a link to a full list on a separate page, expanding in place on a "show more" click, paginated navigation, or scrolling within the block itself (vertical or horizontal). Choosing horizontal scrolling means designing swipe gestures for touchscreens too.

### Requirements

The general quality standards for the product. This covers requirements for materials, the platform, the design, icon metaphors, and the default view (the ideal state of a page). The whole of the design theory and information architecture needs to be spelled out here too, which is exactly what cuts down disputes and small mistakes by an order of magnitude. Don't forget to check the layout itself: how the mockup behaves as font sizes increase, how precisely the design was actually built (pixel-perfect layout is something of a peculiarly local obsession among domestic clients). Block placement, cross-browser behavior, microdata, WCAG2, IVR integration. Spell out that tags like `header`, `footer`, `aside`, and `section` are preferable to `div`. And the small things too — `logo = h1`, with `H1` reserved for the content heading and `Logo` as a `div` on inner pages, hover/active/focus states, and don't forget `favicon.ico` (ideally bundled with 32×32, 48×48, and 64×64 variants inside it) and `apple-touch-icon`, and so on. There's a genuinely excellent front-end developer's checklist [here](http://habrahabr.ru/post/114256/).

*An experienced designer can document everything described above in five to seven working days. Analytical skills are non-negotiable for this role.*

Two favicon sizes are actually enough in practice — browsers handle the rest themselves: a `favicon.png` at 32×32px, and a `favicon-180x180.png` at 180×180px.

The resulting document can fairly be called "project documentation." What comes out of this is a designer who isn't just sitting in an inbox of incoming tasks, constantly justifying to a manager why the product isn't perfect — a designer who thinks through every task, why it exists, and whether it actually contradicts the product's overall direction. Even though the product manager is ultimately accountable for the outcome, the designer carries responsibility for the product's quality, while the manager carries responsibility for the project itself. Fortunately, the pendulum has swung back toward treating the designer as a well-rounded specialist, and the era of "I drew the mockup, so that's how it looks" is behind us. Constantly blown deadlines are usually a sign that the underlying skill to get the work done simply isn't there yet.

And to close: test, test, test — run active experiments in [Google Analytics](https://developers.google.com/analytics/solutions/experiments-client-side) with a multi-armed bandit, never close the GA Debugger or GA Tag Assistant, use a [significance calculator](https://splitforce.com/resources/significance_calculator), keep refining the design and the animations… and test again — UI, JS, performance, test, test. Manage the process, not the chairs.

## Practical addenda: beyond iOS and Android

A handful of reader questions push this article's own ecosystem thinking into territory it doesn't cover directly — other operating systems, documentation tooling, and how far "platform" now extends. They're worth folding in here.

**Designing for less common operating systems.** Sailfish OS, for instance, has no physical buttons on the device at all, so interaction runs entirely on gestures — genuinely pleasant once you get used to it, and worth a look at the Jolla phone as a reference point. App icons there need the formats 86×86, 108×108, 128×128, and 256×256. Android doesn't map onto Sailfish especially well, though prior experience designing for Windows Phone 7/8 transfers more easily. A few of the key conventions: a pull-down menu shouldn't hold more than three items, since a fourth becomes invisible in portrait orientation; since there's no physical back button, it needs a home in the docked panel or the pull-down menu instead; confirm and cancel actions sit comfortably at the top of the screen; and don't forget covers — small images shown in the task list, matching the device's own screen proportions, which can carry buttons tied to your app's functionality. Beyond Sailfish, IRIX (used in aviation), Tizen (wearables), and LG webOS (round screens) are all worth a look for how differently they each handle the same underlying problems.

**Confluence versus a free alternative for spec documentation.** Confluence's real advantage is how much more comfortable it is to store, edit, and browse documentation in — genuinely convenient cross-linking, UML plugins like Gliffy, easy image embedding, and the ability to embed almost any document type (PDF, DOC, XLS, PPT, even Google Docs), on top of full synergy with Jira. Google Docs, in turn, handles comments and suggested edits better, supports real-time co-editing, and has reasonably serviceable tables. In production, the two are worth running together — it lets you sidestep each tool's limitations, embedding a Google Sheet directly into a wiki page for collaborative editing, for instance.

**How far "platform" now extends.** The market isn't just heading toward mobile-first — it's been there for a long time, and by now it's mobile-only. On one enterprise web service, phablets alone account for over 60% of traffic, with the iPhone 5S carrying the most sessions of any single device — which says something about how broad "mobile" really needs to be understood, since it's nowhere close to just iOS and Android anymore. Your product needs to be able to take practically any shape, because Facebook, Instagram, WeChat, Opera, Twitter, and YouTube are all platforms in their own right now too, each building its own technology for a comfortable user experience inside its own ecosystem — Facebook's Instant Articles, AMP, Progressive Web Apps, Android Instant Apps — all of it meant to keep users living comfortably inside the Facebook, Google, and equivalent ecosystems. Choosing the right platforms for your product is genuinely critical: swapping a Facebook sign-up button for an equivalent Google one, in one real case, tripled the number of registrations — different platforms carry very different levels of trust. Messengers are their own platform too, growing fast and generating enormous traffic, with app-store-like ecosystems emerging inside them; in countries like China and Iran, messengers already account for more than a third of all internet use. If your service isn't among the 3–5–7 most frequently used apps on someone's phone, your real "platform" may not be iOS or Android at all, but the large vendors listed above — meaning mobile-first now means having a genuine presence across every platform that matters, not just an app in a store.

**Regional input methods, QR codes included.** Whether a QR code is worth designing for depends heavily on the country you're building for. They never really caught on in the US or Europe, but they're everywhere in China, WeChat especially, thanks to its built-in scanner — used for purchases, links, gifts, even picking the next song off a public screen in some venues, or serving as a ticket for a cinema, a train, or a museum. Chinese payment platforms like WeChat Pay and AliPay built a genuine alternative to POS systems around how quickly they can generate a payment QR code — a buyer scans a code and payment happens automatically, or a seller scans the buyer's code to pull funds from their account, with offline transfers to businesses possible too. The real drawback of QR codes is their graphic design: you can't tell what scanning one will actually produce — a link, or a dancing AR coffee cup — though the visual appearance of the underlying matrix code can be styled into a clear, inviting entry point.
