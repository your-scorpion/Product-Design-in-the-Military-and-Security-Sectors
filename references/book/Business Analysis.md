# Business Analysis

Working with requirements is a critical part of building a product. The product designer, product manager, or business analyst defines the problems together with stakeholders and describes the solution, and the finished requirements go to the development team. In this piece we'll concentrate on the business analyst's role, because it's the analyst who focuses on finding and analyzing information, interviewing stakeholders, spotting new opportunities, and surfacing problems. The business analyst carries the work from an initial idea to a fully formed business need, resolving conflicts in the requirements along the way. You could characterize an analyst as someone who knows how to make sense of information, how to acquire new information, and who holds an exhaustive understanding of the data.

Requirements come in different kinds. *Project requirements* describe how the work is to be done and what the acceptance criteria are. *Product requirements* are purely about the result. When you're developing requirements, business requirements sit highest in the hierarchy, followed by stakeholder requirements, and then the technical requirements for the final solution.

For that reason, the opening move in sprint planning is to understand the business requirements. The level of abstraction doesn't matter — the whole product, or a slice of a single scenario. A clear sign of poor planning is over-engineering and solutions that won't fit the agreed timelines and budgets. The formal excuses for that tend to be the same: the requirements weren't fully understood, they were misinterpreted, the team doesn't grasp the fundamental business problem.

A second important truth: a problem that looks simple always leads to simple solutions, so long as you treat it as simple. If the problem seems simple, it means you don't yet know enough about it. The very first solution that comes to mind is nothing more than a prompt to dig deeper — to uncover the more fundamental problems beneath it and to go looking for more valuable information.

Valuable information is the kind that helps you understand the optimal result you're after — and confirm that the intended solution will actually get you there. Do we have enough resources to reach it? Will the investment be worth the outcome? We work out where we are in our development right now, what result we want to arrive at, and precisely how we'll get there. This is called gap analysis.

So the steps of the work run: assess the need → plan your analysis work → gather information → develop requirements → monitor the requirements and the process.

In their conversations with product management or a designer, analysts offer recommendations, not final decisions. It follows that the requirements should match the need, and the solution should match the requirements. This is very close to OKRs.

OKR goals flow top-down, but every employee can reframe a goal so that it genuinely drives them. Leadership will always be about money and hitting KPIs, whereas an employee has no interest in simply nudging up a number in someone else's bank account — the employee wants to *create* something. The manager, the ruler, will pick a Mercedes; the expert will prefer an Audi. People are interested in different things depending on their profession, which is precisely why they mastered that profession in the first place. Synchronizing different professions is done through metrics and budgets.

A budget is a financial plan set for the year.
**P&L** — the profit-and-loss report: how much was spent and how much was earned, the financial results for a period.
**CapEx** — one-off expenditures, like buying an office or a printer, or a major software purchase budgeted for in advance. Large companies try to move away from CapEx to simplify their balance-sheet accounting.
**OpEx** — operating expenditures: office rent, salaries, small software purchases.
**Commercial** — the cost of marketing and sales, marketing being the acquisition, retention, and study of the audience.

The **total spend** is everything spent in a given period, CapEx and OpEx together.
**Cash in** is how much money you received — from an investor, for example; any inflow of money.
**Sum** is cash in minus the total spend.
**Accum** is how much money sits in the company's account at a given moment, income included.

For medium-term planning we use OKRs, with delivery horizons from a month to a quarter. An OKR is a system for setting and reaching goals within a quarter. The **Objectives** are the core aim of the business, usually a short, pithy phrase — "raise the quality of design," say — and the **Key Results** are the metrics for reaching those Objectives. OKRs almost always break against Goodhart's law: when hitting a metric becomes the goal, the metric loses its value.

Longer horizons — a year, for instance — are assembled from the cascade of shorter quarterly OKRs (four a year). A Unit Objective must, without exception, equal a Global Key Result, and there are the notions of Global OKR → Unit OKR → Team OKR.

OKRs come with rules:

1. No more than five Objectives, and no more than four metrics per Objective.
2. Every Objective must have measurable results.
3. An Objective should be unreachable, yet the team should achieve 60–70% of the original goal.
4. OKRs for everyone.
5. Quarterly goals cannot be changed.
6. Half the goals should come from individual contributors, not only from managers.
7. At the end of the quarter, every employee rates the results of their own work.
8. Everyone knows everyone else's OKRs.

All five Objectives need to be the right ones; a misfire in even one means 20% of your resources are burned with nothing to show for them.

The modern alternative is Unit economics, reckoned per customer. LTV < CAC is unhealthy; LTV > CAC is what you want. All the most heavily marketed metrics come straight from unit economics: CAC (cost of acquisition), ARPU (revenue per user), ARPPU (revenue per paying user), COGS (the cost on each sale), LTV, lsCOGS (the cost of the first sale including lead generation), and fixCOGS (the fixed costs of the business). And on top of all that come the M&A plans. But that's drifting into management — let's come back to the analytical track.

It isn't always obvious why you'd spend time working requirements through so deeply. Often an ordinary conversation with the project manager about the goals and objectives of the business analysis is enough. But that manager must not only bless the work — they have to actively help it along, investing their own time, just as the development team invests its time in consultations and estimates, and just as the stakeholders invest theirs in interviews, approvals, hunting for problems, and settling disputes. Once the analysis plan exists and we've booked the time of everyone involved, the plan barely changes — in the ideal version of the world.

## Working with stakeholders

Stakeholders can be people, groups of people, or companies. They may be *benefactors* — the ones who provide resources, the investors or senior leaders. *Beneficiaries* — the people who use the product or need it to satisfy some need. And the *impacted* — those on whom the product's existence will tell, for better or worse. Stakeholders are also the end users, government regulators, and the development and support teams.

You can work out who the stakeholders are with a simple brainstorm, or by studying the organization's staff structure. But a list of stakeholders isn't enough; what matters is understanding their attitude toward the feature. Who supports rolling it out, and who doesn't? Who will the feature's existence affect? What are the reasons for resistance to building it? Very often stakeholders resist because of requirements no one accounted for. There's no sense drafting new rules of conduct for managers without understanding their process and their working culture. The feature's very reasonableness may raise serious questions, along with fights over the allocation of resources or over how the feature will change a stakeholder's standing once it ships. The reasons for resistance can be legion.

Perhaps staff in remote offices are deeply unhappy with every decision HQ makes and take any change badly. Perhaps the salespeople don't want to sell the *better* product — they want to sell what's easy to sell, a cheap knock-off of the competition. So we find out and account for the influence of different cultures and the political climate inside the organization. Do stakeholders speak freely about their wishes and their problems? What cultural factors can we identify? What competencies will the company gain once the feature is in? And whose influence do we need in order to reach the goal we've set? We work out every detail relevant to needs, wants, and data. Needs can rest on stakeholders' experience and on the state of the industry; wants rest on the number of stakeholders, their diversity, and sometimes on the culture of the company or even of a whole nation.

As soon as we've understood everything important about the stakeholders, we begin preparing for the interviews. We tease out every nuance of the culture. Not everyone speaks perfect English; different cultures have different habits; experience in the industry, in the field, and in the company at a particular level all play their part. Have they shared their experience with anyone else before? If not, they may not be able to articulate their professional experience cleanly. Have they worked anywhere else? If not, they have nothing to compare the processes against.

From the data we want to obtain, we build a hierarchy of stakeholders. Usually the most valuable people are busy with work and unwilling to invest much time in an interview with you, and there will never be enough formal authority to summon people to the table. So you have to understand the motivations and needs of the stakeholders or their leadership in order to invite people to an interview the right way — and to bring an enormous reserve of pragmatism and persistence.

When a face-to-face isn't possible, there's always a compromise to be found: conversations over lunch, analysis of technical documents, group calls, exchanges by email.

## The feature

A feature must be measurable and must have a goal, and goals are set with SMART. We might want, for instance, to improve our product's responsiveness by 15% to better the UX, measured by an NPS survey over the three months following launch. Or, over eight months, to raise the number of orders placed through the site by unauthenticated customers by 50% through integrating partners' tools. Here it's clear what the result affects, and the goal is finite. Goals can change — because a hypothesis went unconfirmed, say, or because better hypotheses appeared, or because the external conditions shifted.

Let's break it down a little further. SMART, as I use it, implies one large goal and several smaller ones. There are various interpretations, but in my practice this approach works most effectively:

- **S** — the goal's direction: an understanding of what needs to be done. Learning to design user flows, for example. That's a direction — raising your qualification, sharpening your skills — and that kind of work sits outside the backlog's tasks.
- **M** — the goal's name, with its result. Learning to test prototypes before handing them to development, say.
- **A** — three questions for pinning down the goal's effectiveness criteria: what result must be reached, what indicator will confirm the goal was met, and what benefit meeting it will bring. That might be a change in the number of post-release reworks, the count of iterations, or design corrections.
- **R** — the goal's weight, given that the sum of all goals for the period must come to 100%. The larger goal is conventionally set at 70%.
- **T** — the final result. A concrete one — not "it got better, prettier, kinder." For a specialist, that can be reflected as progress in a competency matrix.

Every interested stakeholder should be named in a RACI matrix.

You need to know in advance whether the infrastructure to build the feature exists, what benefits building it will bring, and how long it will take to earn back the money spent on building and supporting it. We analyze the goals with SWOT, where the feature's goals are built on opportunities or threats and sorted under S or under W. The SWOT analysis is done before any building begins, which saves a great deal of time later.

We don't touch the business plan in detail, but a feature inevitably tells on the business architecture. We need to know the answers to these questions:

- What resources are available, and which are needed.
- In what order the additional resources must be hired.
- Which tool, and who, will validate quality.
- What can be discussed and with whom, and who is responsible for what.
- What is hardest to build? Those are the risk zones.
- What external dependencies do we have?
- Being able to answer all five "why?"s.
- Preparing the many documents and diagrams we'll come to below.

In the end, we need to understand the business requirements from the point of view of each stakeholder. And don't forget that any stakeholder's time is important and valuable.

## Getting to the insights

In any research, what we want is rare and sensitive information, not confirmation of the obvious. Information like that can come even from a plain analysis of existing documents — at the very least, the context grows clearer. This work is done together with the designer and the product manager, and the aim is good, high-quality information: the unexpected, the rare, the non-obvious — the insight.

When interviews start running too long, focus groups and group workshops are the tools for working with a group of stakeholders. The difference between a focus group and a group workshop:

- In a workshop, conflicts of interest are the norm; in a focus group, we manage groupthink.
- Workshops are better at generating requirements.
- Workshops must be moderated, focus groups facilitated.
- Both call for cross-disciplinary samples.

A sample prompt for working with a focus group: "Cast your mind back to childhood and tell me about the smell of a perfume that stayed with you, for good or for ill."

Observation, too, yields a great deal of useful information. You can observe passively — without intervening — but if employees know they're being watched, the Hawthorne effect is unavoidable. Active observation is observation with questions, asked as they arise. Participatory observation involves getting personally involved. There's also the simulation route, where we artificially create the conditions we invite the respondent into — in effect, the equivalent of a prototype interface test.

At the close of any exchange with respondents, we always check: do the people you're interviewing have questions of their own? Is there anything we didn't ask? And who else should we talk to on the topic we've discussed?

After the interviews we cluster the results into job analysis and/or persona analysis. Personas are more about the indirect stakeholders it's hard to speak with in person — what we need from them is a point of view. Personas help us understand skill levels and working methods, and to build the classic user story. We send every participant a summary of the session so they can check and confirm we've understood them correctly. With jobs, we can work out which roles bear on the project.

A project's lifecycle can be predictive or adaptive. Predictive — everything is planned in detail up front; we know all the requirements in advance and most likely work in Waterfall. Adaptive is about gathering requirements as you go and adapting your work to a shifting environment, as in Agile (SPOD → VUCA → BANI).

## Artifacts

Documentation isn't only solid walls of text. For a front-end developer the primary documentation is the final design in Figma, not a slab of prose in Jira. There's also a wealth of handy diagrams and tables for structuring requirements.

Need to describe a lot of nested problems? Use a **Fishbone diagram**. The problem sits in the fish's head, and the potential causes run along the bones. The tips of the bones hold categories of cause, and beneath each bone sit the causes themselves. Each bone has its own theme — one bone is personnel, a second technology, a third resources — and note that resources ≠ personnel: there can be enough personnel while the processes are inefficient, which leads to a wasteful allocation of resources.

![Fishbone diagram — the causes of not shipping the super-app](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/5085495.png)

*A Fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram: the effect — being unable to ship the super-app — sits in the head, and the causes are grouped along the bones under Design, Processes, Management, and Code quality. (The labels in this source image remain in the original Russian.)*

Next is the **interrelationships diagram**, which helps you see the links between many aspects visually — especially useful in complex situations.

Then the **process flow**, which shows how a business process works, people and systems included. A diagram like this can vividly show how an operator works at an offline point of sale.

![Process flow for a point of sale](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/Group-1.png)

*A process flow for an offline point of sale: the purchase process branches on "credit card or cash?", and card payments branch again on whether the acquiring bank is under sanctions.*

A **capability table** is an ordinary table listing the problems, their root causes, and the methods of solving them. You can enrich it with columns for deadlines, completion status, priority, a link to the Jira task, and so on.

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| The web interface lags on the workstations. | Too much data because of the operation's duration; an enormous profile. | Move to the platform's native technologies. |
| Textures don't load on the network map. | Ordinary textures are slow to upload to the GPU. | Use compressed textures instead of PNG/JPG. |

An **affinity diagram** gathers all the problems and solutions into intelligible groups, from which you can sometimes even build a scenario. Often such a diagram is the product of a brainstorm, after clustering and tagging.

![Affinity diagram](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Group-690.png)

*An affinity diagram sorting ideas under three headings — Motivation (pizza every Friday, perks for seniors, free lunches, upskilling courses, a clear career path), Profit growth (grow sales in Asia, adapt products for the Eastern market, more salespeople in Singapore, close unprofitable projects, replace the commercial director), and Working with users (regular UX research, review analysis in support, social channels, more time on interfaces, UX courses for managers).*

There are nearly always competitors in the market. In competitor analysis we work out which of them is already solving the problem we want to solve. To identify best practices we run pairwise comparisons, and we arrive at a table like this:

| Solution | Votes | Final rank |
|---|---|---|
| Solution 1 | III | 2 |
| Solution 2 | I | 5 |
| Solution 3 | II | 4 |
| Solution 4 | III | 1 |
| Solution 5 | II | 3 |

The principle of pairwise comparison: take Solution 1 and Solution 2 and decide, by some criteria, that Solution 1 is the better — give it a vote and mark it in the table. Then compare Solution 1 against Solution 3, and again a vote goes to one of them. A Weighted Decision Matrix works on the same principle but more flexibly and fairly, adding a weight to each factor.

And if you set out to draw a diagram and end up with an endless tangle of everything, look toward decision tables — sometimes they're far more concise.

## Metrics

A business analyst is obliged to be able to compute the following metrics.

**Net Present Value.** NPV > 0 means the feature is profitable. The formula is NPV = Σ [Period Cash Flow / (1 + R)^T] − initial investment, where R is the interest rate and T the number of time periods.

There's also the **payback period**, and it's better still to reckon annual return — the **Internal Rate of Return (IRR)**.

**Cashflow** is income minus outgoings across a time series. Say we loaded up an expensive advertising campaign to get a project moving, and it then reaches self-sufficiency. The cashflow report is the first thing the C-level asks for; only afterward, if the cashflow results are weak, do they start looking at the projects' effectiveness. So cashflow has nothing to do with the company's profitability.

The minimum viable executive dashboard holds cashflow, P&L, and the balance sheet — in plainer language, the movement of funds, the profit-and-loss statement, and the balance.

## Upstream of the roadmap: raising the money

All of these numbers — NPV, IRR, the cashflow report the C-level reaches for first — eventually get pointed at someone: an investor deciding whether to fund the work at all. Preparing to pitch a project to an investor is a discipline of its own. An investor is really weighing four basic questions: do you genuinely understand the market and your target audience; why won't they lose their money with you; is your business durable; and when, and how much, will the investor earn?

It helps to understand that money landing in your account isn't the end of the relationship — it's the beginning. Communication continues on the terms of the agreement: reports, a part in decisions, help through the investor's connections and knowledge, a hand in the hard moments, and a defined course of events if you fail to meet the obligations you've taken on. But there's no need to be afraid; once the money is in, you're in the same boat. The investor will help you solve problems, will invest further if you succeed, and may even take a seat on the board.

How long does it all take? At the fastest, raising a round takes three to four months; more usually four to six, from the start to money in your account — and it can drag on toward a year. The fundraising itself is quick, a couple of weeks. If you're searching for a long time, everyone concludes that either the product is mediocre or you, as a leader, can't sell and can't charge people with energy. What you want is for investors to feel FOMO — the sense that they're about to miss the best deal of the year. Negotiating the terms takes one to three months and produces a Term Sheet, the list of deal conditions. Once the Term Sheet is signed, the terms don't change; those are the conditions on which the investor enters the company. Signing it almost certainly makes the deal exclusive, and you stop talking to other investors — whereas an *unsigned* Term Sheet is precisely the argument you use to hurry the more interesting investors along.

The next stage is Due Diligence, which costs hundreds of thousands of dollars and is paid for by the lead investor — a hyper-detailed examination of the company, which obviously implies the round has to be sizeable. At least $500,000, so that paying $50,000–100,000 for the Due Diligence makes sense.

Say an investor comes in with a Series A. You'll sign documents for converting the stake, spell out the discount for the early investor, the valuation cap, and the terms for an early exit or an early close of the convertible loan — the option to return the investment in cash rather than as equity. That lives in the Convertible Note, and it's usually a 2× return of the money. You'll talk through the liquidation preference: if the startup had assets, they're distributed in favour of the investor who signed the Convertible Note. As an alternative to the Convertible Note there's the SAFE, on terms friendlier to the owner, or the KISS (debt), though that's for sums under a million dollars. Make absolutely sure the documents cover the company's valuation, the structure of the deal terms and of company governance, the exit conditions, the distribution of money in a liquidation, the closing conditions, the confidentiality and exclusivity clauses, and the penalties.

If a board of directors is formed — usually after a Series A — it seats investors and founders, who make the strategic decisions. Shareholder rights are written into the SHA. Drag-along and tag-along describe who has to join whose decisions, and the right of first refusal (ROFR) lets a current shareholder block investment from another investor. Other documents matter too: the liquidation preferences that govern the order in which assets are distributed if the company is wound up; call and put options, the rights to buy out; vesting, where a shareholder receives their stake not all at once but after certain events, such as a portion after a tranche; anti-dilution protection; and the ratchet — an increase in the investor's stake with no fresh money at all, for instance a percentage dripping from the owner's share into the investor's if targets aren't met by an agreed date.

Any of your work can serve internal ends or outward-facing presentations alike, and how you format and store the data depends directly on the project and on what comes next. If you're on Agile, it's user stories; Waterfall wants use cases. And the development team holds only the information you documented — nothing more.

**Goal map** — very useful at the very start. Every goal should be easily traceable back to the original problems.

**Ecosystem map** — we visualize the tools among the various tools that together form a single picture of the world.

## Describing scenarios

Everyone's favourite, the use case, is a description of how a goal can be reached through a particular process. Use cases earn their keep in complex scenarios with varied, unforeseen outcomes. Their visualization is strictly tabular or solid text. A use case is built on actors and on a description of the context from the business's point of view, and it includes a main path and alternative ones. Sometimes a use case is a full-blown replacement for the requirements, with a complete description of the problem.

| Description | The user finds a partner based on their profile data, their likes on social networks, their content preferences, and the volume of their correspondence. |
|---|---|
| Actor | The user |
| Our benefit | Higher engagement, CSI, COR |
| Triggers | On the "dating" screen, the user taps the "Find a match" button. |
| Preconditions | The user's profile is filled in and they've granted access to their social networks. |
| Post-conditions | The user is satisfied with the partner found and they're talking. Or the user is dissatisfied with the match and restarts the scenario. |

**The user was happy with it.** **The user wasn't happy.** **Alternative scenario — something went wrong.** **Exception scenario.** These are the main, alternative, and exception paths that follow.

Next come **user stories**. Unlike a use case, a user story is a narrative description of a scenario from the user's point of view, built on the principle User → Action → Value. As a *buyer*, I want *to receive goods* at night, so that I can *extend the romantic evening*. And we describe the acceptance criteria:

- Couriers work at night.
- Warehouses are scattered across the whole city.
- A duty operator is reachable 24/7.

Many stories form an Epic. User stories change over time and are not the final requirements for development; a user story isn't about requirements but about user value.

All of this has to fit into Jira, or any other task manager. One arrangement: an Epic describes only *what* needs doing, not *how*. These are business requirements, and they must pass an Epic review — no technical detail, the level of strategy, and an Epic should fit within a single quarter. A Story describes functionality and is written by the product owner, with particular attention to the acceptance criteria; building it shouldn't take more than two weeks, and final designs are essential, since without them you can't estimate the effort. If building it needs two weeks and a day, that's two different stories. Stories should make it easy to write release notes — and remember that 10% of the sprint goes to grooming. On the level below live the tasks, written by team leads for the developers: which API to use or write, which library.

## Caring for requirements

Requirements let us understand which figures we want to reach and by which criteria we'll know the work is done. When stakeholders have questions about a shipped feature, the chief way to resolve the conflict is the agreed business requirements. Requirements can be functional and non-functional. Functional ones describe what the application does; non-functional ones speak to the principles of relieving the user's pain. Set apart from these are the high-level requirements about user pain (the business requirements) and the requirements from stakeholders. A functional requirement might read: enter the conditions of a taxi trip, choose a taxi from those offered. Non-functional ones concern server availability at a DAU of 1,000,000 with peaks at 9 and 18 hundred hours, or using a quadtree to find the nearest drone.

Just how deep should that detail go? Take payment systems, where the temptation is to write a requirement as thin as "the user can pay by card" and leave it there. In practice you usually descend to the level of the payment channels. A payment system distinguishes the *provider* from the *method*: the provider is Stripe, say, and once Stripe is connected you can expose payment by card, AliPay, BNPL, crypto, and so on. A single merchant may run several providers — the first offering card and PayPal, the second Apple Pay, which can be both a provider and a method at once.

Inside the methods sit *channels*: a specific bank. Connect the provider Stripe, let the user choose to pay by card, and the payment goes through, say, FAB. The more legitimate the business, the simpler this tree. In gambling and 18+, the menagerie of providers and methods is enormous, because these are high-risk transactions that banks are forever blocking — if you try to top up on a gambling site and it offers only crypto or wallets, the other methods have just been flagged for strange transactions and blocked. High-risk transactions tend to carry more meat: many processors, different limits on different channels, complex logic for balancing between them, and a rate somewhere around 6–10%. There, the more provider buttons on the screen, the higher the odds of success.

Then there's the question of how to store crypto transactions, which have no approach to the exponent and no ISO codes, even though the original purchase is still in USD. Risk level can be read from the MCC code, which reports the merchant's line of business — and "miscoded merchants" are always high-risk. Currency deserves particular attention: a channel might handle only euros and dollars, while in Nigeria you need to pay in naira with 150% volatility. Either the payment is simply unavailable to the user, or it goes through a conversion that carries its own risks, since you need the live rate. Some build their own converter without a markup via indicative currencies; others use ready-made DCC with conversion at the gateway. But you have to settle upfront who bears the conversion risk, where the hedging sits, and how the limits bleed into usability — set a $600 limit and, converted into Turkmen manat, it becomes something like 2,106.53 TMT, an oddly specific figure the user won't understand, so you need to think through a rounding algorithm. You also spell out the decline codes and their matching use cases: code 51 is insufficient funds, and there's no sense hammering away at other channels after it.

The size of the business feeds the menagerie too. The complexity is necessary because some PSPs aren't licensed to operate in every country — the Amazons and AliExpresses of the world keep their own local providers in each country they're present in. And the regional specificity runs deep: providers generally issue keys per merchant, and in Africa, say, only a few providers will hand over a pool of keys in advance. In every case they won't switch the methods on at their end until onboarding is complete — and onboarding wants a working site with all its policies, a company, licensing information, the client's approximate turnover, and usually something more besides.

Requirements can carry assumptions: an expected load of 100,000 users a day, and the team closing the task in a single iteration. Assumptions should be categorized and re-checked from time to time. Requirements must be exhaustively complete, consistent, detailed, measurable, feasible, and testable. If we're building Instagram, we assume 1,500,000,000 users, enormous read load and heavy write load. On an average day, some 3,000,000,000 photos are uploaded — 3 × 10⁹. There are 86,400 seconds in a day, which we can round up to 100,000 = 10⁵, and that gives on the order of tens of thousands of reads a second — and writes can be a thousand times heavier. One photo weighs 200 kb, and with 3 × 10⁹ photos a day the storage adds up fast. You keep the photo's ID and the date of the post. With a key-value store, the question becomes whether you need two tables — one keyed on the photo, the other keyed on the user. And you back all of it up by the 3-2-1 rule: keep three backups, on two different devices, one of them off the organization's network.

And we validate all of it. Did we guess the number of photos a day correctly? If not, how does that tell on the rest of the requirements? It's vital to keep confirming that the requirements are current, because from time to time they change and no one may think to tell us.

Requirements need to be kept current and monitored, and they have metrics of their own — stakeholder satisfaction, the stability of the requirements, how hard they are to take in. If requirements change, every change has to be documented and categorized: who changed a requirement, and why.

A requirements review happens after each iteration, to understand what was done and what we'll do for the next release. New requirements must be approved by the stakeholders before they go into the backlog.
