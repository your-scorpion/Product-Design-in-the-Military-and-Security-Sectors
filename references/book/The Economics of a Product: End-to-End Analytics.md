# The Economics of a Product: End-to-End Analytics

*Maxim Tsvetkov — 10 June 2019*

---

You have a business — an online store, a game, a service. You have already run targeted and display advertising, decided it was expensive and ineffective, and now your customer count is slowly sliding, the site redesign only made things worse, and you are starting to think about selling the business or accepting investment from strategic players on frightening terms. Then this article is for you. What you need is end-to-end analytics as a business process: it lets you grow both sales and advertising efficiency while tracking every micro- and macro-conversion.

## Competitor Analysis

All of end-to-end analytics reduces to answering a single question: *which channel is best?* Simple to ask, but a correct answer requires answering many others — how many click-throughs came from each advertising channel, how much money was spent, how many were real customers, and what revenue, profit, and ROI they produced. To get there you need to gather precise 360° data: every call, every form submission, every chat enquiry. In effect this is a sales funnel that accounts for user behaviour before the site, on the site, and after it.

To analyse the market you can either buy research or dig through open sources. In the early stages you need to define your lines of business, study competitors, gather your semantic core and its parameters, plot a traffic-growth curve, and estimate and forecast the profit from promotion. All of this fits under the heading "market structure." You need to understand your competitors' value propositions, the trends, and the indicators of success: which competitors are in decline, and which are growing their base? A word of caution about leaning too hard on any of these third-party panels. It is better to run several analytics systems at once and to keep something home-grown alongside them; an internal data-collection system can be split into internal use and separate modules or roles for outside contractors. Store conversion is in fact quite easy to compute yourself — impressions into installs (once you have at least four hundred of them), since an install can happen without a visit to the app's page at all. The data can be trusted, but it pays to be specific about *which* data: features, store presentation, and app-market analytics are not equally reliable. AppMagic lays out market segments well, though they are worth checking against Sensor Tower (which is expensive); revenue figures for successful apps hold up under scrutiny. Day-7 and day-30 retention, on the other hand, diverge sharply from reality, and services like AppFollow are essentially a proxy of a proxy — I would trust them less, and on indexing they plainly lie.

If you want to go deeper, scanners such as `dirb` will walk a site with a dictionary and surface useful information; you can even pass a list of domains to brute-force through `-L`.

Next comes gathering the semantic core, for which the time-tested [Wordstat](https://wordstat.yandex.ru/) shows what queries people take to the internet and lets you plot a sales-growth curve, while AppFollow does the same for queries in the mobile stores. To estimate and forecast profit without historical data, fairly shallow arithmetic often suffices: in season, demand runs at 1,000 packages a month; off season, 300; the pre-season at 600. Assume a 6% conversion to lead and an average order value of ₽1,700. And keep a very simple tally of staff costs alongside: a mid-level developer on ₽100,000 net costs the company ₽147,300. From these inputs you can already build a forecast in Excel or Google Sheets — provided you are not doing three million sessions a day — and ideally you plot the profit dynamics and the cumulative total in parallel.

To gauge whether prospective buyers even have the budget for your product or service, the **BANT** approach is used: **B**udget — whether the prospect has enough money to buy what you sell; **A**uthority — whether they have the competence to make the purchase decision; **N**eed — whether they actually need what you offer; and **T**iming — the time frame in which the purchase will happen.

If you run not a store but a service that has to be monetized, there are three principal routes. Showing ads, which brings a low ARPU (revenue divided by user count) and irritated users. A virtual-currency store, which demands that you keep users engaged and makes offers hard to tune. And personalized offers, which is what we will look at next. To craft a personal offer you look at time since registration, activity over a given period, and seasonality — if it is climbing season, everyone is buying chalk. This can be automated with a simple arithmetic progression: if someone pays once a week, you surface a personal offer every week. There are subtler algorithms too. After registration a user is filed under *Non-payer* and shown a single starter set of offers. After the first transaction they move to *New-payer*, and now, empirically, through varied personal offers, you feel out the average cheque that suits them. If a user never pays again within a month of that first purchase, you have to re-activate them with a steep discount.

Strangely enough, product-efficiency metrics exist even at the pre-project stage. PAM (Potential Available Market) is the entire potential volume of the market. SAM (Served Available Market) is the share you can serve. TAM (Total Addressable Market) is the total target market, usually overestimated on the high side. SOM (Serviceable & Obtainable Market) is what is realistically attainable. The classic chain is PAM → SAM → SOM, and you compute optimistic, realistic, and pessimistic scenarios.

| Scenario    | MRR per paying user | Users who buy | Users   | MRR       |
| ----------- | ------------------- | ------------- | ------- | --------- |
| Optimistic  | $20.00              | 10%           | 12,000  | $240,000  |
| Realistic   | $20.00              | 7%            | 7,500   | $150,000  |
| Pessimistic | $20.00              | 4%            | 4,000   | $80,000   |
| Failure     | $20.00              | 1%            | 300     | $6,000    |

## Counting the Money

You cannot improve what you cannot measure. The classic trap: bids rise, CPA rises in proportion, you no longer fit inside your margin and run at a loss, and you are forced into non-target traffic. Better to launch a new kind of advertising against an audience that has not carried your site's cookie for the last sixty days. Your principal metric for tracking the efficiency of money spent is **ROI** (Return on Investment) = (revenue − investment) / investment × 100%. The higher the figure, the more effective the campaign; once it slips below break-even, things are dire. An aside: you only know a user's true value at a particular moment in time — traffic cohorts are not constant — so it is important to track ROI both in traffic buying and in the product. It also pays to learn early how to decompose ROI into smaller metrics: if ROI fell by 23%, you detail it — the cost per thousand impressions rose 13%, users began buying cheaper items so AOV dropped 7%, and the remaining 3% was lost to a narrowing of the funnel.

One way to build a pyramid with ROI at its apex:

| 1) | ROI = LTV / CPI                       |
| -- | ------------------------------------- |
| 2) | LTV = ARPU_inapp + ARPU_AdsMon        |
| 3) | ARPU_inapp = ARPPU × %payers          |
| 4) | ARPPU = AOV × OPP                     |
| 5) | ARPU_AdsMon = rv_per_user × eCPM      |

Alternatively, the **DRR / ad-cost share** metric = ad spend / profit from advertising × 100%. The lower the figure, the more effective the campaign. Pick whichever you prefer.

You could write a dozen books about advertising spend, but the gist is simple: the market is full of bad traffic — incentivized, fraudulent, click-farm traffic — and real users will by no means always be the ones arriving through your ads. This is easily solved by working only with vetted partners that everyone in the market uses. Yet there are always people who want to find something cheaper, and occasionally they manage to find it both cheaper and better; in those cases you set limits.

While we are on targeting, a caveat worth internalizing: you cannot, for instance, simply target an Instagram audience that has at least ten thousand followers. Easily — no, it is not possible. You can target bloggers and large stores, or take up parsing. But are you even sure the audience you want to reach is actually sitting in the app, scrolling its feed? I rather doubt it.

The steps of the sales funnel are not only attribution but also money: we pay to acquire a user and to return them to the funnel. The simplest thing you can do is classic remarketing — configuring targeted advertising in response to certain user actions. A prospect put an item in the basket, or merely viewed it, or already bought a low-quality product and you know they will need spare parts in a month; the number of purchases made, recent abandoners, users with a particular time-on-site — all of these are potential targets for advertising or for bid adjustments in contextual search campaigns.

The elements of an automated funnel in messengers, for example, run like this:

- A **lead magnet** to capture the lead — a free offer that nudges the user to leave their contacts. Its value must be high and tied to your product.
- A **tripwire** to demonstrate how you solve the pain — a useful paid item at a very attractive price that partly closes the customer's pain. The value should exceed the price, but the pain must not be fully resolved.
- A **one-time offer** for fast lead-to-customer conversion — a prompt to an impulse purchase, a good discount or an extra bonus.
- The **core product** to fully resolve the pain — the company's main product or service, often advertised head-on.
- A **profit maximizer** to lift the average cheque and loyalty — it tries to sell a high-margin item that is hard to sell, offered to those who have already bought the core product.

Then we analyse how the people — or bots — from the acquired traffic behave, chiefly through cohort analysis. Did they complete onboarding, and if so, how quickly? Did they look at several products? Those are short-term indicators. The long-term ones are the familiar metrics: retention at 1, 2, 3, 5, 7 days and onward, sessions, conversion to first purchase, Cumulative ARPU over *n* days (how much a person brought in over their first *n* days in the product), and then ROI, LTV, CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost), and ROMI (Return on Marketing Investment). The last two come down to how much was spent on acquisition divided by how many users were ultimately acquired. Each user should bring in three times what was spent to acquire them; in the case of ROMI we are speaking only of marketing spend. And when working with CAC you must not forget salaries, overheads, and the cost of the acquisition tooling — in many formulas these are omitted deliberately, to understate CAC before a sale of the business.

There is a recurring puzzle here that every operator meets: the gap between the forecast price of advertising and its real price. You run ads in a contextual network, the forecast quotes one price, and the result comes out very different — far higher. The first thing to examine is the cost of your product: with a small median cheque and a low markup, you should not wade into paid search at all. Advertising phone cases, say, takes skill — it is always low-frequency queries with negative keywords. Dig into your CTR: if it is below your competitors', the platform will raise your price. My own median click price runs around ₽25 in a contextual network. Conceptually, the platform pushes to the top the ads that are clicked more often and more dearly, and since at the start of a campaign you have no average to stand on, you will more often land on expensive clicks. CTR — clicks divided by impressions, times one hundred — is the lever. Picture two ads: the first at CPM ₽200 with CTR 1.7%, the second at CPM ₽360 with CTR 1.2%. The platform will choose the second: the CTR is lower, but in aggregate it earns more. So play with the settings — different audience variants, different geo combinations, interesting creatives (the good ones fire immediately, there is no point waiting) — and watch the conversion for each audience.

That same discipline is what good channel management looks like day to day. On a regular basis a competent paid-search specialist should, without being assigned each task: find queries with zero impressions over six months and any 404s; produce quality-score reports; refresh ad creatives; work the search-query report, adding target queries and pushing non-target ones into negatives; switch off ineffective placements; refine and clean the query set; run experiments and use new ad-platform features; request audits from the network; analyse competitors' ads and mine them for ideas to test; and remove duplicates while running cross-negation through the editor.

Now to unit economics. The whole of unit economics answers a few questions: how much, on average, we spend to acquire one customer, how quickly they pay back, and how many purchases it takes to operate in the black. There are settled conventions — in mobile games and apps the unit is a new user; in SaaS it is a paying customer; in a subscription model the unit is the amount of money in a single payment. If a subscription has different tiers, each tier is its own economy; if a product has several sales channels, those are several economies. And tracking unit economics can be replaced perfectly well by ROI or ROMI, where ROI = (LTV − CPA) / CPA, and happiness lies in CPA (the cost of a given action) being less than LTV. For many specialists the core KPI is raising LTV and lowering CPA, and LTV = ARPPU × the average number of purchases per user, which must exceed CPA. We will leave aside the classic case where a salesperson's KPI is the number of sales, so they hand out discounts left and right and erode the margin.

![Unit-economics breakdown.](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/gfhf.jpg)

*CPM is the cost of a thousand ad impressions. A good CPM is considered $2–3 for mobile games; you either measure it per thousand impressions or divide by a thousand.*

To counter the discount-everything reflex there is OKR (Objectives and Key Results), an alternative to KPI. The objectives are what must be achieved; the key results are the metrics and the monitoring of progress toward them. There should be two to five of each, no more. In simplified form, OKR fits into the following table:

| Objectives                                                       | Metrics                          | Projects                                                       |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| The objective and how you will know it has been reached          | Target and current metrics       | Which projects to run, the resource estimate per project, and the deadline |

There is also an approach of diminishing the revenue taken from showing the same ad to the same user across a day — the first impression is the sweetest in money terms. Mediation platforms can help serve relevant ads.

I want to single out the North Star Metric (NSM): the one "mother" metric that reflects the core value the product delivers to customers. NSM, together with OKR and product vision, makes it clear where the business is heading and why. It is a long-term metric, the embodiment of the team's strategic task. For a taxi service, NSM = LTV; for Zoom it is the number of weekly meetings; for Facebook, DAU; for WhatsApp, the number of messages sent by a single user.

Living right alongside it is OMTM (One Metric That Matters): at the intersection of the business's development stage and its business model, you can work out which key metrics belong. The development stages might be Empathy, Stickiness, Virality, Revenue, and Scale.

![Stages and the metrics that fit them.](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/slide_58.jpg)

When working with metrics you must choose ones that satisfy the test of *comparability* — comparison of numbers across time periods, comparison of user groups, our figures against competitors' — and the test of *clarity*: if a metric is unclear to the decision-maker, it is a bad metric (with occasional exceptions). Metrics should also be interrelated; the number of orders and the conversion rate are far more informative together than apart.

The global NSM is about value to the user. It can change with the development stage, the market situation, and so on. The C-level does not always set the NSM correctly with width, frequency, depth, and quality in mind; that level of detail is something the leading product managers, designers, and analysts can supply, while the broad idea comes from the important people, fresh from their year-long sabbaticals. But ultimately a business has one goal — revenue or market share — so financial metrics will always sit somewhere in the hierarchy. The hygiene factor: NSM + OKR + product vision.

So if a company is aimed at growing efficiency — an NSM set by the C-level — the metrics become Payback Period, LTV, CAC, Shipping Cost, and ROAS. If instead it is growth in the number of customers, the metrics are traffic, registrations, retention, and conversion to paying. For that to happen you need to help users invest their time, files, or social life into the service — friending their best friends, say, or uploading files to the cloud — and all of this breaks down into metrics. Take the retention rate, or its mirror the churn rate: this metric shows the result of your efforts on frequency of use, but it says nothing about earnings, so you look to revenue growth — ARPU, Net Dollar Retention.

There is a related question that comes up the moment you ship something new: which metrics matter when releasing a feature? Empirically, I would make it a rule to look first at feature activation at 50% or more with daily usage in mind; from daily usage you can already compute retention; and then link two more metrics — C1, or AvPrice (the average cheque).

Or the metric might be user motivation — say, on an educational project, where different products carry different North Stars. SkillBox is edutainment; GeekBrains is comfortable learning from zero to junior; one practicum keeps the clever ones; courses run by design studios are about quickly banging out a portfolio case. Broadly, the goal of an educational project is to grow the number of students through their motivation to stay on the platform and learn. The motivations you might uncover include: it was a gift; social approval (everyone's learning a language, so I will too); a friend's son studied there and now earns a fortune; a promotion at work; broadening one's competence to raise one's value and income; changing profession; being assigned to it at work; wanting to get to grips with a new field — and much more besides. Each motivation can have its own metric: employment, recommendations, the share of diplomas completed. Want a hard exercise? Work out what metric an SRM — Supply Relationship Management — system would carry.

A word, too, on the development team. A fixed-price model for a project is possible only under strict conditions: a short project, a full specification, documentation, nothing changing, and a team already familiar with the platform and tools. Time & Material applies when the project is in revision or testing, the development horizon is up to six months and the team is five or more, or the project is simply large. A dedicated team, focused on the IT solution, is used when you need a long-term project and cannot hire a team with the right competencies any other way.

## Working With the Audience

Next you will need to improve the interface of your store, service, app, or game, because driving users in is far from the hardest part. Improving the UX means generating hypotheses and testing them — through customer development, prototypes, interviews, analysis of quantitative and qualitative data, A/B tests, and an MVP of the functionality.

When it comes to A/B-testing platforms specifically, if you find yourself unable to come to terms with one vendor, there are alternatives; as a general answer, look at the Adobe stack — Audience Manager together with Analytics and Target — and let the actual task decide.

All of the above leads gently to the question of a 24/7 live report, that is, end-to-end analytics. It is not hard to see that if you get a current report once a week, you make decisions a week late and lose money. Like A/B tests — each of which costs money — you must be able to close a test automatically the moment enough data has accumulated. A test left running is a loss, and Bayes will help you here. Consider the loss directly: you launch an A/B test and turnover immediately drops 3%, and yet such a test still has twenty days to run. This is solved with a canary deployment, a gradual roll-out across the traffic. Nuances arise with the sizes of the control and test groups, where you can see the wrong proportions of an effect.

The first step toward building end-to-end analytics is knowing precisely how much money has gone into advertising, including every traffic-acquisition cost. You need the CPA metric and the logging of every lead enquiry into the company, which in turn yields a better-tuned campaign: you look at the key phrases whose resulting applications are noticeably expensive, separate them from the cheap leads, and each day raise bids on the latter, pouring budget toward them to bring the channel into the black. The result is an understanding of which advertising sources bring cheap, good leads. In the world of games, mechanics test very well: you wire in Firebase, set the events, publish to the stores, and run two or three hundred players through via advertising.

And then there is LTV. Classically, Lifetime Value is how much gross profit one user brings over the entire time they use the product. A pretty definition that is hard to apply in practice; usually LTV is computed at some month from the user's arrival. Many businesses live on repeat sales, especially the large retailers — there is always a split into first and repeat purchases, and every supermarket loyalty card is no more than a way to pay a customer back through conversion to a second, third, or tenth purchase. The simple rule: track CPA and LTV. Conversion, after all, is the sum of every doubt, barrier, and problem on the user's path to purchase.

> You cannot build business analytics on free web-analytics tools.

Growing LTV. In a subscription model, the user first meets the product — installing a mobile app, say. Then comes the *free journey*, when they use the product at no cost. *Conversion* is the first payment, the move into paying status, and the *trial journey* is the period when the person keeps living in the service as a payer after that first purchase. That is one loop. Then the second begins: Activation → Premium Journey → Stop → Renewal. You track where the user falls away and direct the team's effort there.

Computing acquisition cost is straightforward: take the cost of acquiring a cohort, divide it by the number of users in the cohort, and you have CPA (Cost per Acquisition). For example, $600,000 spent over 365,987 users gives a CPA of roughly $1.64. Then, how much you earned per user: take that same cohort and work out how much it will potentially bring in by computing the cohort's future gross profit, divided by the number of users — that is the user's LTV. And here one can argue at length that LTV should be reckoned from gross profit, not revenue — but in Google Analytics we do not see profit, only revenue. Gross profit is the difference between revenue and the cost of the goods or service sold: bought for $790,000, sold for $1,234,427, leaving $444,427 of gross profit.

If all of this frightens you, here is a simple recipe: split the audience into three or four segments and spend ₽1,500 on each. See which segments produced no conversions and stop pouring money there. Where conversions ran positive, spend more; where they ran negative or flat, segment more finely and launch again at ₽1,500. Along the way you assemble a look-alike audience.

Once you have done that exercise you can climb further — into RFM segmentation, which works especially well paired with k-means. You start from users' payments, assigning each user a score for their payment behaviour from 1 to 5. That gives 5 × 5 × 5 = 125 possible segments; 555 are the VIPs, who pay often and a lot. With each identified cohort you build a different manner of communication — for instance: people who have only just started paying, whose interest in paying needs supporting; those who paid only once and need reminding how good life is when you pay; those on the verge of leaving, for whom you switch on reactivation; VIPs on the verge of leaving, on whom you spend every resource to hold loyalty; and plain VIPs, who pay and use the product happily, with whom you communicate and to whom you give gifts. This way you can pick out those who pay much and often, or who became a customer recently and started straight away with expensive purchases. R = 4, F = 3, M = 1 means a recent buyer who buys now and then, but cheap trifles. RFM stands for Recency, Frequency, and Monetary value (the sum of orders, an analogue of LTV) — recency, frequency, and profitability — segmenting customers by purchasing behaviour. With the results in hand you segment:

- Loyal customers: R = 4–5, F = 3–5, M = 3–5.
- Loyal but still shopping around: R = 4–5, F = 1–3, M = 1–2.
- Buys everywhere: R = 1, F = 1, M = 5.
- Ready to leave, needs more attention: R = 2–3, F = 1–5, M = 1–5.
- Already moved to competitors: R = 1, F = 1–5, M = 3–5.
- Moved to competitors and thinks ill of you: R = 1, F = 1–5, M = 1–2.

RFM is not the only lens. When you need to weigh many cohorts of users inside one product at once, ABCDX segmentation divides customers by the ratio of effort and money invested in them against what they give back. **A** pay a lot, complain little, and buy often. **B** pay a lot and need the product, but have complaints. **C** complain, pay little, but need the product. **D** use the product for free and complain a great deal. **X** demand the earth, want a unique product built to their needs, and pay very heavily. Take design studios: you can split them into small (ten landing pages a month), medium (ten to thirty), and large (thirty to a hundred); for us, as a landing-page platform, the small ones fall into category C or D with a negative NPS.

A universal piece of advice, from Sean Ellis, is to run a survey — the **PMF** (Product/Market Fit) survey. Ellis has his own [site](https://pmfsurvey.com/) for distributing it, but you are better off using a tool such as Typeform. The structure runs: Where did you hear about the product? How would your life be affected if you could no longer use it? What alternative would you be willing to use? Have you recommended it to anyone? What is its main benefit? Who else might appreciate it? How else could it be improved? And — do you mind if we come back to you to comment on your answers? Be sure, too, to find out about geography and buying habits. You save the results to CSV, load them into pandas, and analyse; it is best to run the survey automatically every three months and watch how customer sentiment shifts, plus a couple of random calls with customers for validation.

This is also exactly where a designer can help establish product/market fit. The headline is the Sean Ellis metric — the single question *"How would you feel if you could no longer use our product?"* with the answers Very disappointed / Somewhat disappointed / Not disappointed; if more than 40% answer "very disappointed," your fit is in fine shape. The second route is the Lean Canvas paired with HADI cycles, a methodology for structuring the initial version of a product — ideal for SaaS or online marketing. The Lean Canvas describes every part of a product needed to start a business, though it may not work for digitizing an already-running one; it is more a precise description of the structure you intend to build. The steps run: form a user segment without thinking about the profit from each user; create clear communication for that audience, watching Post-Click; identify your uniqueness by assessing competitors' functionality; form the list of features only you have; choose promotion channels from the insights of your first customers; work the unit economics, understanding costs and revenue; lay out the cost structure; form the base metrics; and form a uniqueness that cannot be copied quickly. I usually fill in such a table for each need I uncover in an interview, and so understand which user need we can earn the most money by solving. All of it is hypotheses, and therefore risks — product, customer, and market risks. A customer risk is a problem with the size of the audience or access to it; a market risk is competitors and the conquest of market volume. On the Lean Canvas the customer risks are owned by the "early adopters," "customer segments," and "channels" blocks, the market risks by "unfair advantage," "alternatives," "cost structure," and "revenue streams." And it is worth keeping the Stanford framing in mind: a successful product needs a strong team, an idea, execution, and distribution — and at every stage the cornerstone is sales.

![A filled Lean Canvas.](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Group-2481.png)

Other ways to segment exist beyond these. You can turn to NPS and to k-means (similar points): NPS scores from −100 to 100, dividing users into Promoters (9–10), Passives (7–8), and Detractors (0–6). In combination with RFM and neural or Bayesian networks for prediction, the task should be closed. If recency trends negative the person is a potential churner; if positive, a loyal one.

![NPS distribution.](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/hjhj-1.png)

While we are on measuring engagement: a recurring question is whether you can know for certain that the recipient of an email read it. Read, meaning opened? Broadly, only by embedding a pixel that, when its request hits the server, counts the email as opened; services like SendGrid and Mailchimp expose open rates out of the box.

The general point is that we must be able to gather data in our own analytics system. And inevitably, over time, we hit the system's limits — the number of event parameters, the number of events themselves, the cost of scaling the collection system. So it is important to shape the right event structure in advance: sketch the funnel you want on a sheet of paper, and from it form the list of events you will need. For a starting point, look at the AIDA and AARRR funnel frameworks; AARRR is the best known, with its variant funnels and the corresponding metrics at each step, and you can start from the user's first session. A sales funnel is a sequence of steps, and many systems cap the number of steps, usually at five. An alternative is PULSE (Uptime, Latency, Seven-day active users, Earnings), more for websites, or Google's HEART, which leans on UX.

Those limits are not abstract; they bite in concrete ways. A common one: an online store has enhanced e-commerce wired into Google Analytics, the `impressions` event sends every product through the `dataLayer`, the catalogue grows, and suddenly not all the data shows up. Almost certainly you have hit GA's ceiling of 8 KB per POST request. There are fixes. The top-tier SMB shops send requests in small batches in real time, the moment a product enters the visible area of the screen — jQuery has an `onScreen` property, and you can do it in plain JS — though this is not the lightest approach on resources: pushing a separate event each time noticeably slows page load, on top of the 500-hits-per-session limit. If you have a single-page app the situation is more varied still, and the product catalogue is far from your biggest analytics problem there — that is when you invest in your own data warehouse. You can also send IDs rather than Cyrillic product names to shrink the request. Really, this is a problem to solve with the developers, handing them libraries like Tracking-Events. And what counts as a "top-tier SMB" shop in the first place? You can lean on the SKU metric — the number of stock-keeping units — but it is a weak signal, useful more for identifying competitors, where you take two stores' SKUs and compare them with Kendall's rank correlation. Going by orders per day is sturdier: up to a hundred orders a day is small business, a hundred to five hundred is medium, and over five hundred is large; at a typical 1% conversion, that maps to small at ten thousand visits, medium at ten to fifty thousand, and large above fifty thousand.

What you can collect varies by project, but a usable core stands out: traffic source, geography, day of registration, time since registration, device parameters, socio-demographics, how often the user logs in and pays, and any custom events. From this set you can build segments such as:

- New users vs. veterans
- Paying vs. non-paying
- Visited the promotions page vs. not
- First payment vs. repeat payments
- Activated vs. not activated
- Frequent vs. infrequent log-ins
- Completed onboarding vs. skipped it
- Source 1 / source 2 / source 3

## Offline Retail and FMCG

It is worth pausing on whether all of this applies to physical goods — to analysing offline stores and the discounts inside them — and whether that is even a digital topic at all. In FMCG, analytics is entirely possible. You can compete for the customer's money with more than discounts: with packaging design (a biscuit drawn on a chocolate-bar wrapper creates the impression of buying two things for the price of one), with ecology (people lean toward organic goods made without harm to the environment), and with advertising in sport. Loyalty programmes both bring a person back to the store and let you track their offline purchases; all of this helps you see where to expect a sales uplift and assists with impulse buys. Most likely the offline store also has an online one, and discounts can be differentiated — the online buyers tend to spend more, and online and offline are different audiences.

Offline, it is useful to track a unified product index that shows an item's in-store performance from ACV sales, turnover, the number of receipts, and margin; separately you compute a product's uniqueness, its proximity to other products in the customer decision tree, and how replaceable it is. And in any case a basket is a basket everywhere — you can track peaks, dips, and unusual consumer behaviour. Any goods fall into two types, substitutes and complements. Substitutes are items that can be swapped for an analogue, used when you need to predict how the prices of different goods influence one another: one kind of milk goes up, but there are many milks on the shelf, and sales shift to the analogues that did not rise. A more structured approach is ABC analysis with the Pareto principle — 80% of the financial result comes from 20% of your product line; group A gives you the most profit (twenty products yielding 80%), while experiments are safe to run on group C, of which there are many and whose contribution to revenue is slight. For demand stability, XYZ analysis is used: compute the standard deviation, compute the mean, divide them, and get a measure in percent, then split everything into three cohorts — X (stable demand), Y (medium), and Z (unstable). Combining the two analyses gives nine sub-segments — the aptly named ABC-XYZ analysis — from which you can conclude, for instance, that group AZ goods are bought mainly during promotions, being expensive, and that their price should come down. And ROPO (research online, purchase offline) never goes away: loyalty cards tie online visitors to offline purchases and reveal the effectiveness of advertising.

## Systems

The next important criterion is sales: revenue, profit, and ROI — how much money was really earned. This data usually lives in a CRM. CRMs come in many shapes and hold a great deal of useful information and important functions: automated call-outs to customers, lead processing, the full history of a customer's interaction with the business, and customer data. Any decent CRM has an API, and the popular ones — amoCRM, retailCRM, Bitrix24, Pipedrive — let you work with entities such as deal, lead, contact, company, and order, on which end-to-end analytics is built. Technically it looks roughly like this: order data lands in the CRM along with the `ga_clientID` and `userID`, and then a manager adds the revenue per user by hand. Telephony integrated into the CRM lets that manager see a customer's earlier enquiries at the moment of a call. And Zapier glues everything to everything.

At a genuinely advanced level, we can even determine the customer's country from the ringtone. The ringback sound is usually supplied by the operator of the call's recipient, so from the music you can tell which country the person is currently in and make them relevant offers. Within the CRM you can also listen back to managers' call recordings and analyse how they speak to customers — beyond quality control, you can pull out niche keywords — and you can analyse missed calls and adjust the support team's schedule accordingly.

Depending on the company's maturity, the role of the CRM can be played by a succession of things: Excel → CRM → BPM → BI → PM → RTBM → ~~Excel~~. A Real-Time Business Manager is more of an unattainable goal to strive toward.

On integrations: there is a native one between Google Ads and Google Analytics, but importing spend from Yandex, VK, Facebook, and Mail.ru into GA is done by hand through automated flows. From there you can pass the needed `ga_clientID` into the CRM and use connectors to load the UTM-tagged data from the advertising sources into a separate database. There will be many sources — typically Yandex.Direct, Yandex.Market, Google Ads, Google Merchant Center, Facebook, VK, and Criteo.

Another important step is call tracking. Not every call is a lead — calls may come from current customers about technical problems, or be plain spam — and that is solved with tagging: a manager marks in the CRM, by hand, whether a call was spam or a lead. A separate phone number for each advertising source makes the analyst's life easier. The pricier option is dynamic call tracking with phrase recognition, which helps trace online advertising and understand which keywords and sites are working. In reality both methods are combined, but only dynamic call tracking yields full statistics for end-to-end analytics.

The approach for pulling, transforming, and loading data is called ETL, and Dataflow is for shaping the architecture of the stream — where the data is taken from, where and when it is processed, and where it is loaded. Take a case: on the site a user sees a phone number and calls; the call is instantly logged in the CRM with its date, an ID, the phone number, and a `userID`/`ga_clientID` from the dynamic call tracking. The clientID is still, even now, a simple and reliable way to identify a user and then follow them by session — if only as a browser. From this data the lead is matched against other channels, a manager closes the deal by hand, and we get the revenue per customer. On some sites — especially car dealers' — you will notice the phone number hidden behind a little eye icon; that is a budget implementation of dynamic call tracking, saving on the generation of email addresses and phone numbers for each user. From a UX standpoint it is a very debatable decision, but data-driven design sometimes demands sacrifices.

## Conversions

The case above skips a step: the moment, before the call, when the user first arrived on the site. And this is the most common problem of all — working out which channel to credit with the first touch that began the sale. The first case is a direct visit (the user simply typed the address into the bar), where we have no idea where they learned of the company. The second: a customer arrives by advertising and looks at prices, then drifts into another retargeting base and returns by a different ad — at which point the first ad and its source are counted as loss-making. The first case calls for multichannel analytics, the second for associated conversions.

Accounting for multichannel behaviour is a serious matter of attribution models. Suppose a customer wants to buy an air conditioner: they enter a query, click a Yandex.Direct ad, and land on our store. They settle on a brand and leave. A week later some app increased their browsing depth, so our ad for that conditioner — with a link to our store — caught up with them; they click through and leave again, returning later from plain organic search. So: first Yandex.Direct, then a Facebook ad, then Google Organic, and all of these channels together drove the sale — the first let them choose the brand, the second warmed their interest, the third converted. That is multichannel: accounting for every touch of the site before the purchase. Questions arise around Post-Click and Post-View, hard to count in a world without cookies and SKAd.

The real fun begins when you choose the attribution model by which the sale is credited to a channel. By default the Google Analytics and Yandex.Metrica counters build reports on the last interaction with the site — the most common model, but not the best. Last Click is evil, and the default in Metrica; it cannot measure the payback of advertising channels with the customer's earlier touchpoints in mind, and works only for assessment when the chain of interactions was initiated by an ad click. Another model, last non-direct click, credits the conversion to the last non-direct channel, ignoring all direct visits. There is also first interaction, which credits the first visit to the site — and the first touch is usually the most expensive.

![Attribution models compared.](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/Frame-61.png)

Google Analytics has a "multichannel funnels" report that shows the touches before a conversion plainly; in Yandex.Metrica you can do this through the Logs API. A lifehack: to determine the traffic source you sometimes need to ignore the UTM tag — if an ad is running on Facebook but the tag says Google.

![Multichannel funnels in GA.](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/coversion.jpg)

*Multichannel funnels in GA.*

The plumbing behind all of this is the UTM tag, and it is worth getting right, because attribution is only as honest as the tagging beneath it. There are three mandatory parameters: `utm_source` for the traffic source, `utm_medium` for the type of source, and `utm_campaign` for the campaign name. A tag missing the medium — `?utm_source=yandex.ru&utm_campaign=shop_conf&utm_term=cart` — is broken; `utm_term`, which carries the keywords, is not mandatory. Hand-placed tags are where errors breed, so use a dedicated URL builder; and check the obvious, always — I recently fixed quick links for a large retailer where a marketer had swapped the anchor's position, writing `site.ru/#anchor?utm` instead of `site.ru/?utm#anchor`. The `utm_medium` channels follow market conventions: `social` for social posts (not banner creatives); `cpc` for cost-per-click, which marketers adore because it is safe — no clicks, no spend; `cpa` for cost-per-action; `cpm` for cost per thousand, which the ad platform loves; `cpv` for cost per view or visit; `cps` for cost per sale; `cpl` for cost per lead; `cpo` for cost per order; with `email`, `qrcode`, and `sms` raising no questions, and `bloggers` and `podcasts` for their respective links. By entry type, in GA: direct visits read everywhere as `(not set) | (none)`; search as `organic`; mailings as `email`; social as `social | social-network | social-media | sm`; paid search as `cpc | ppc | paidsearch`; display as `display | cpm | banner`; other advertising as `cpv | cpa | cpp | content-text`; third-party links as `referral`; and an affiliate programme as `affiliate`. The rules themselves are simple: UTM tags are always lowercase; the source always names the domain; before you create a new tag, check a hundred times that it does not already exist; and for Google Ads, auto-tagging is used together with UTM. Keep in mind, too, that eCPM is a *calculated* cost per thousand — you pay per click under CPC, but the platform wants its profit and reckons it through CTR — and that the second-price auction softens this: if a competitor bid ₽100 and you bid ₽200, you are charged not ₽200 but ₽101.

You can have fun with associated conversions as well. This is the multichannel approach for identifying the channels at the start, middle, and end of the interaction funnel before a goal is reached, and it is used to track the ROI of channels that look loss-making under the ordinary model. The U-shaped model, for instance, gives 40% of the weight each to the first and last channels and spreads the rest evenly across the remaining touches; the simpler linear model distributes weight evenly across all channels. The same multi-site logic is what you reach for when, say, you run a network of promo sites for high-margin goods and a prospect drifts between them leaving different applications on each: you want a single cookie (one browser) across the whole network — which also repairs the cookie-death problem, since it will be refreshed often — and a consolidated report showing each campaign's cost across the different attribution models, killing any campaign that is loss-making under all of them, and from there moving on to Markov chains.

The choice of model has an enormous effect on the analytics, because it changes the resulting revenue, profit, ROI, and even the order count. So as the business grows you should develop your own model of dynamic attribution, built by hand out of the same BigQuery, accounting for the time and distance between touches or the time spent on site — streaming data into BigQuery, or exporting from GA 360, so there is no pre-hit sampling. But taking the raw data on user actions and assembling it into sessions yourself is a senior-level task.

## Tools

For almost the whole article I have used GA as the example, since you almost certainly already have a counter installed and historical data. But here is the bad news: Yandex.Metrica and Google Analytics are not very good at building analytics up to the moment of sale. Metrica will not let you pass cost information across all advertising channels correctly, and in Google Analytics it is hard to pass data in from a CRM. There is the Measurement Protocol, which handles the job of pushing data into GA, but a product return has to be entered by hand — to say nothing of the absence of call tracking and the sampling.

Before any of those pipes carry trustworthy numbers, it helps to know the measurement gotchas that quietly distort them. Both GA and Metrica build their session and time metrics from `pageview` hits: a hit goes to the server on each page load, "time on page" is the difference between hits across pageviews (excluding the last page), and the session ends with the last hit sent. Past 500,000 sessions, GA samples; the session window defaults to thirty minutes but is configurable; and if the same goal is reached several times within a session, only one completion is counted — which is why goals are better hung on events. So when traffic differs twofold between Metrica and GA while entry pages and behavioural figures match, suspect the session counting and a spike in visits from an "undefined" source, which is either a swarm of robots or a duplicated counter snippet. To check Metrica's install, open `http://your-site.com/?_ym_debug=1` and the console, where you should see the counter number and the data being sent; several pageviews mean the snippet is duplicated. The same time logic applies in GA, and it is not the most precise measure — in Webvisor, time is the difference between the first and last recorded action, which is more accurate, and you can raise precision further by hanging an event on scroll. When figures wander from one Metrica report to another, remember that different reports show different things — "Popular" is about views, "Entry pages" about visits, so a wander through several pages gives each page one view in the former but the first page several views and a corresponding depth in the latter. There is one underlying database, and the Logs API and the web UI both read it, but the UI can lag; sampling and bugs are other reasons API and interface diverge, so take days on which the statistics have settled, since Google likes to recompute data two or three weeks back. When goals fight a redirect, the gtm-sonar and Stop Light browser extensions help.

A few more practical knots from the same territory. Metrica cannot show the search phrases by which a user arrived from Google organic — no one can determine Google search transitions except Google itself — but install the GA code, add the site to Search Console, and link them under Admin → Property, and the "Queries" report will show what you need. If you lack GA 360 but want to export into BigQuery, proxy the traffic through a dedicated server in GTM when you only need new data; for historical data, Python with JSON authentication on a cron job, where you can run into request limits. And if a subdomain's `collect` fires but the view sends no data, check how the filter is set — it should be by host, `param\.subdomain\.ru` — try sending a test hit, and if it lands in the real-time report, dig into the site and GTM settings, while if it does not, dig into the counter; verify the counter ID, that Tag Assistant recognizes it, and that GTM is published — or you have hit the 500-hit ceiling, whose exit is buying 360 and requesting the cap be raised to 2,000 per session.

You will have to learn to combine data into a single pivot and visualize it. This is fairly simple: the data is a combination of visit parameters and "hits" (transactions, events), all exported into Excel, Power BI, Data Studio, or BigQuery/Azure and combinations thereof. You choose the storage into which information from every advertising channel will pour — CRM plus counters plus call tracking — then build reports and show them to leadership. The idea is to analyse data on the side of services like Power BI, Tableau, or Adobe Analytics, and store it in the likes of BigQuery, ClickHouse, a spreadsheet, or Excel. The scheme, in short: Google Ads (UTM) → site + Google Analytics → Google Data Studio.

If the goal is simply a stack that the business can open at any moment, including from a phone, as cheaply as possible — then Tableau or Amplitude, if the refresh speed is acceptable. You can set up Yandex DataLens + AppMetrica, or Google Data Studio + Firebase, and get close to real-time updates. For something truly cheap, Firebase + BigQuery + Data Studio works, though it is awkward even for marketing tasks; you can also pull data from AppsFlyer via Data Locker into ClickHouse + Power BI for visualization (which is paid). The self-hosted alternatives are Apache Superset and Metabase.

Ordinary sales funnels will not be enough for you: user behaviour is non-sequential, full of returns and dashes between pages and platforms. If you have a multiplatform product, it exists on several platforms with identical functionality; cross-platform means present on different platforms but with possibly differing functionality. And the conversion platform does not necessarily equal the delivery platform — people may search for a product on an iPad and buy from a desktop.

A funnel is a visualization of the customer's progress: acquisition → activation → retention → referral → revenue, with each stage carrying its own metrics (an email opened, a return visit, a minimum revenue generated). The classic GA funnel shows none of this. You need a system in which you can build a graph of the sequence in which features are used. On graphs you can see a looped scenario caused by poor UX (the user makes a circuit of the features and closes the app) and the time spent at each stage; graphs also let you better track the users who fell away, segment them, and hand them to qualitative research. I build my graphs in RStudio with igraph, and use networkX for analysis.

![A typical step funnel.](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/funnel.jpg)

*A typical step-by-step sales funnel.*

To sum up, what should ultimately take shape is a pipeline along these lines. For end-to-end analytics you need data sources — at least two, a CRM and Google Analytics. To check lead cost, UTM tags come from Yandex.Direct and, by your chosen model, the sale is credited to the last non-direct traffic. All of this goes to a data export in BigQuery, your own SQL Server, or ClickHouse — and ClickHouse even has an internal visualizer of its own. A chain for real-time analytics is entirely feasible: Azure/Unity/AWS/AppsFlyer → Kafka/Alooma → BigQuery → a dashboard. Draw up such a plan and begin building it. Market conditions will of course make their own corrections, but the analyst's duty is to try to organize the whole process of collecting and storing data. Or something more complex, when the business needs to track changes in everything over time — then you can go to anchor modeling, where a separate table is created for each entity. Say there is a listing with twenty parameters; for it you create a special table with slices by update, which is far better than the venerable Kimball. Taking a daily snapshot is also an option, stored as a separate history on a separate server. In anchor modeling it is a multitude of small tables — but you still need the snapshots.

## Raising Money From Investors

All of which circles back to the choice raised at the very start: selling the business or taking investment. And the first instinct of most founders — to lead a pitch with profit — is the wrong one. The moment you start talking about profit, they will ask "how much?", and however you answer, the next line is "why so little?" The reason is that a dividend model does not interest investors; they want it sharp, immediate, and large. From an investor's point of view, an investment is money placed to multiply income and preserve capital, and a small passive yield is a perk of investing, not its aim. From which follows the counter-intuitive fact that investors are interested even in loss-making startups. What they want is numbers and facts, market analysis and quantitative research, insights from respondents, intersecting trends, and at least three competitive advantages. And in the more everyday case where you are presenting results to stakeholders, keep opportunity cost in mind: money spent on one project is a project not launched, and a project with no growth that still consumes support resources will be shut down.

Investors are not all alike, and I divide them into three kinds. *Strategic* investors buy a small share and test how good the company's technology is with a view to buying it outright. *Investment funds* put money in early and wait for growth, then sell their share at the new price. And there are *business angels*. The routes to money differ accordingly. There are indirect investments — through a broker or an exchange, NASDAQ being where every startup wants to be. The internet writes a great deal about IPOs, but going public that way is long and very expensive — millions of dollars — taking in a financial-statement audit, a roadshow, the building of an order book, and much more. Large startups usually choose the stock exchange; smaller ones receive money privately from a specific investor to whom they sell part of their stake.

There is a distinction worth holding firmly: *cash-in* versus *cash-out*. In cash-in, the investor's money passes inside the company and becomes the company's — it pays salaries, rents the office and servers — and in return the investor receives shares created for sale, which is nearly every deal in digital; if the shares are already distributed among shareholders and more money is wanted, the company cannot take shares back from them, but it can issue more. In cash-out, existing shares are sold and the investors take their earned profit. Each time you want to sell shares, the company's capitalization is valued. The arithmetic is fairly simple: the company is valued at ₽4,000,000; I, as the investor, want 20% for ₽1,000,000; after the purchase it is valued at ₽5,000,000 — the post-money value — and the company issues new shares for the deal. As for the journey of dilution: you launch a startup and it is worth nothing, because no outsider has valued it, though as owner you hold 100%. Even if you poured in all your money and five years of your life, it is still worth nothing without an external valuation. You decide to raise; an investor values it at ₽1,000,000, you agree on seed financing of 5% of that million, you receive ₽50,000 and own 95%. The MVP succeeds and you make first sales; you need more for growth, an angel arrives, you issue shares and sell 30%, your founder's share falls — as does the first investor's, diluted below their original 5% — and a smaller founder share is a risk of losing control of the company.

Choosing an investor, then, is not merely a matter of drinking together; it breaks into stages. First, work out whether you need investment at all, and if so, how much and for what. Then compile a list of potential investors; make contact with the ones that interest you and pitch them; prepare the documents they require; conduct the negotiations; work through the Term Sheet (the list of deal conditions); sign the agreement; and continue the relationship afterwards. The investment depends on the project's phase. Seed financing is small sums, because the project is early and the investor is spreading bets across many startups; then there is a Series A as the start of a startup's growth into a corporation, or the path to an IPO — and each stage carries different expectations of how much money is on the table. Business angels can even be your own family, with a couple of tens of thousands of dollars. The rough bands run:

- Pre-seed: under $150k
- Seed: $10k–$2M
- Series A and Series B: $1M–$30M
- Series C and above: $40M+
- Private equity and IPO: hundreds of millions of dollars

If all you hold is an MVP, you are talking about Pre-seed and its sub-$150k. From the investor's side, to recoup, they must invest in twenty startups understanding that only one will fire; if an investor has $1,000,000, it is split across twenty, so the investment in your product is just $50,000. The same $50,000 can come from FFF — family, friends, and fools or founders — and Bezos got his first round from his parents; hence the attention to metrics like CRL (Commercial Readiness Level) and MRL (Manufacturing Readiness Level). A little larger are business angels or syndicates of them, with investments up to $100,000 — and again, from your own circle these can be your b2b clients, the top managers who have such sums. When you have grown a little — a team with a RACI matrix, sales, a financial model — you can look for up to $1,000,000. Venture firms put in $500,000 to $5,000,000, but those are later stages with a strategy and customers; private-equity funds will give up to $50,000,000, but their aim is a controlling stake; and strategic investors give the most — from $1,000,000 to $100,000,000 — because they simply want your technology. Some invest for dividends and need to see net profit and stability; the reverse also exists, the venture appetite for profit from the growth of capitalization, where speed of growth matters and all revenue is reinvested into growth with no dividends. Another motive is to take part of your company and sell it later (the investment banks). A strategic partnership is about mutual benefit. There is even grant-style, non-repayable financing — money that is not returned but must be used for its stated purpose. The crucial thing to understand is that investors are not just money. Their involvement in your processes varies — some only want to buy your shares, while others want strategic partnership (angels among them), aiming at the deliberate growth of your company toward a future merger. The optimum is Smart Money: with the money come connections, introductions, knowledge, experience, even technology.

And how does an investor work out what the company is worth and what they will take out at the end — a finger in the air? Usually one can speak of sensible rounds only after steady sales, with revenue from around ₽100,000 a month; at that stage they invest ₽2–10 million for 7–10% — so the maximum conceivable valuation is ₽100,000,000, but in fact it will be ₽15–20 million. For a seed stage you need revenue of roughly ₽2 million a month, at which point you are given ₽15–30 million for 20–25%, and beyond that the more strategic players invest hundreds of millions of roubles to scale sales — that is for Russia. In the West they give around $1,000,000 for 10–15%; at seed, $1,500,000 for 10–25%; at Series A you can count on up to ten million for 15–50% (the 50% being what strategics expect, financial investors more modest); Series B enriches the company by up to twenty-five million for 15–30%; Series C brings sums up to $50,000,000; and the final stage, the IPO, is any sum at all for 50%.

Valuation itself is done on the basis of comparables with multiples. A multiple is a coefficient: you take a company like yours, look at its capitalization and revenue, divide, and get the multiple — the EV/S formula (Enterprise Value / Sales). On top of that a 25% discount is applied, so it turns out your startup is worth three years' revenue; a country discount is added too, which for the CIS was 30%, making 30 + 25 = a final discount of 55%. Other formulas exist — EV/EBITDA (Enterprise Value / EBITDA) and P/E (Price / Earnings). If the multiple runs from revenue, it is three to ten annual turnovers; ARR/MRR (current subscription contracts) likewise gives a multiple of three to ten on the company's current value. In exchange for the money, the investor takes a share. At the start you own 100%; you bring in a developer as co-founder who takes 50%, leaving you 50% plus one golden share; then the first outside financing arrives from a wealthy figure who takes 5%, and an option pool is carved out for employees; next a business angel takes 15% and everyone's share shrinks; and so on through every round, with venture capitalists taking shares, employees exercising options, and an outside CEO arriving to expand the option pool by another 6–8%. The largest share of the money at IPO ends up with the venture capitalists, and the founder also receives a fine sum — that is the rosy picture; in reality the dilutions are sharper and the founders are left with roughly 15% by the IPO. The only question is what the company is worth at that moment. All of this is written into the cap table — an internal document in the West, a public one in Russia.

A note on the financial reporting that sits underneath all of this. P&L is computed for the whole period up to payback, the first year month by month and everything after at lower detail; you subtract costs from revenue, get the balance, and arrive at Free Cash Flow. But the deeper question is usually about the basic finances of an IT unit — a company or department — which at minimum should compute revenue, gross margin, commercial expenses, operating expenses, EBITDA, EBIT, and net income, broadly and per product. You should not rely on P&L alone: you also need a cash-flow statement, a forecast balance, IFRS reporting, and efficiency indicators — PB, PBP, ROI, DPBP, EBIT, EBITDA. For a company already operating, add the balance sheet, revenue (YoY growth), and gross profit, plus operating cash flow, which speaks to the overall success and growth potential of the business — the sum of money a company can earn over a period, and which depends on rising EBITDA and falling working capital (NWC). Track revenue and gross profit YoY/QoQ monthly. Working capital itself includes deferred expenses, loans and credits, and customer receivables. The EBITDA margin = EBITDA / gross profit, or EBITDA / revenue, with a target before shareholders around 20–30%, and marginality = EBITDA margin / PBT margin (a reminder: marginality is the share of profit in the total value, and EBITDA is profit before taxes, interest, depreciation, and amortization). Revenue per employee for an outstaffing model is revenue, EBITDA, or gross profit divided by headcount. And EBITDA itself = purchases × (average cheque − COGS) − acquisition costs − first-sale costs − fixed COGS. Counting the purchases: users × conversion × repeat purchases, so 1,000 users × 4% × 2.2 = 40 × 2.2 = 88 purchases at the end of the period; acquisition costs, 1,000 × ₽72 = ₽72,000; first-sale costs, with first-sale COGS including lead generation at ₽65, give ₽925.6. For a healthy business the core criteria are scalability and marginality — and you often see marginality fall as a crowd of people is hired, with the hirers failing to grasp that tripling the headcount does not automatically triple EBITDA.

How secret R&D affects all this is a fair question, because EBITDA is the whole pot of profit before taxes and before interest on loans. Such spending is capitalized expenditure, hard to count in IT (people instead of factories); but British, Swedish, German, and French companies do not enter all of a new product's development costs into their reporting — so spend on new development does not touch EBITDA until capitalization. Say there is $45,000,000 in the bank, $20,000,000 for a new product, the rest operating capital topped up by current products. The moment the new product ships, all $20,000,000 of its costs are divided proportionally across a set term, month by month, and only then appear on EBITDA — so within that many months the product must earn back at least its $20,000,000. And when raising money in exchange for a stake, to raise $1,000,000 for 10% you need a valuation of $10,000,000. Early on it seems the higher the valuation the better, since you give away a smaller slice — but that is only a good idea if it is your last round. If you plan several, ask yourself whether you can be worth ten times as much in five years; when you read that company N raised at a $400,000,000 valuation, always ask how it will be worth $4,000,000,000 in five. Overvalue yourself and take $1,000,000, and they expect a $10,000,000 return in five years — and an overvalued first round means a second round must justify the previous valuation as well.

Finally, the kinds of cost, since they thread through every formula above. At the top level, unit economics distinguishes three:

1. **COGS** (Cost of Goods Sold) — costs per sale: logistics, packaging, storage, inspection, cashback, advertising.
2. **1sCOGS** (First-sale COGS) — costs of the first sale: a discount, a promotion, a small fee to the media buyer, free first delivery, "refer a friend."
3. **fixed COGS** — the business's constant costs: office rent, salaries.

So the first formula is users × (ARPU × marginality − CPAcq) = marginal profit, or, expanded, EBITDA = users × (conversion × repeat purchases × (average cheque − COGS) − CPAcq) − (users × conversion × 1sCOGS) − fixed COGS; the second, for the customer, is customers × (ARPPU × marginality − CAC) = marginal profit. And, roughly, an investor will expect parameters of this shape: COGS + OPEX + CAPEX + taxes = 70m AED; FCF (pre-funding) of 1.5m plus funding received of 20m; FCF and cumulative FCF of 25m; with gross profit and net profit each expressed in millions of dollars against revenue. Draw the plan, and build it.
