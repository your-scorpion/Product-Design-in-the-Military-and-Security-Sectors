# Hyper-Casual Ergonomics

*Maksim Tcvetkov · Product Design and Security Lead*

---

The foundation of UX is an uncomfortable truth: people want to rest. They do not want to work their brains. Naturally they also want a good product, good service, and low prices — but none of that decides anything if the customer doesn't trust you. Every one of us routinely uses services that are neither the cheapest nor the best available, and we do it for a single reason: we trust what feels familiar and safe. There is a stubborn belief that a business earns that trust slowly, over years, starting from the day it opens its doors. What follows is, in large part, an argument that this belief is only half right — and that the other half is where the interesting work lives.

It is hard to overstate what brand trust is worth. People will happily overpay for a brand, because they know precisely what they are buying: the assurance that everything will be fine, that they won't have to strain, that their brain can spend its energy somewhere else. That assurance is the core value of every premium service. It rests on the company's genuine underlying value, translated honestly to the customer, layered on top of good UX. People are actively searching for this. And when your North Star — the single guiding purpose of the company — is reflected in what the customer actually needs, rather than merely patching some isolated pain, your customers stop being customers and become advocates. They pay more, they recommend you to friends, they defend you in online arguments, and they forgive your mistakes.

Consumer behavior has shifted underneath all of this. Fifteen years ago, everyone watched television, ads and all. When a viewer liked a product, they phoned a salesperson. If they doubted the quality, they hunted for opinions among acquaintances, extracted details from the salesperson, consulted their acquaintances again, and went around that loop until they felt safe. That model of selling used to be fine. It is not fine now, because a wave of technology has rewired how people behave. Your customers understand something they didn't before: they do not have to rely on your company as a source of information. In the modern world only two things are guaranteed to be within arm's reach of almost everyone — a toothbrush and a smartphone. Through the smartphone a customer can now retrieve any fact they want, and, more importantly, decide for themselves which advertising they are willing to see. Power has passed from the company to the customer. On paper this looks like a catastrophe for business. And yet these same people will cheerfully pay ten dollars for a jar of soda made down the street by a kind old woman. How does that happen? Brand.

Brand carries a second, quieter virtue: it holds on to your people. As an employer you attract exactly those who believe in your North Star, and once a company is a band of like-minded people, service quality rises on its own — without thick rulebooks and systems of fines, which work only briefly and leave consequences behind them.

So it all comes down to two things: trust in the brand, and a lighter cognitive load for your customers. If the ambition to become that kind of company has taken hold, the how rests on three pillars. First, know exactly what your North Star is, and write it down. Second, share your values relentlessly, inside the company and beyond it. Third, live your goals and your ideology rather than framing them. Every decision should be built around the values you actually hold. You must be able to state the idea in a single sentence. And if there is a wonderful person on the team — clever, funny, rarely at fault — who does not share the company's values, that person still has to go. Defending the brand from the inside is one of the sharpest ways to set yourself apart from competitors.

There is a humble measurement habit that makes all of this legible: residual analysis. Say you run a campaign to lift the brand's image. You survey people before, launch, and survey again, then compare the answers. In practice it is a plain table in which some number of respondents rated a statement about the brand as true or false. You compute the frequencies, then the residuals — the gap between the frequencies you expected and the ones you observed — deviation by deviation, for every column and every row. Where the residuals cluster is where perception actually moved.

## Casualness

So: your customer owns a smartphone, refuses to think, and you need their loyalty. Games have a genre built for precisely that person — Hyper-Casual. Ultra-short sessions, primitive gameplay. What separates it from merely *casual* games is the business model: Hyper-Casual titles are monetized purely through advertising. Thirty to forty seconds of play, then an ad. This is the genre we will try to map onto the classic development of digital services.

Ordinarily you discover a need in three moves: interviews, then a persona, then an experience core. But if a client wants to cover the entire paying audience on a market in one stroke, the Hyper-Casual approach can genuinely deliver it. The cleanest example is Tinder. Swipe left, swipe right — a Hyper-Casual mechanic. Its defining traits are worth naming precisely: fast conversion of the user into an install or a CTA (call to action); constant, visible progress; a single finger sufficient to operate the phone; content consumed in queues and on transit; a timekiller people actually want.

Solving a problem like that demands real fluency in ergonomics. Ergonomics is the scientific discipline that studies the interaction between a person and a system, with the aim of improving the quality of that interaction and the efficiency of the tasks performed inside it. The better a product's ergonomics, the better its *snackability* — the quality that makes something easy to consume in small bites and hard to put down, equal parts stickiness and delight. Teams usually chase that feeling with sound and animation, but that is only simulation. UX quality has to be the load-bearing factor, because it acts directly on the receptors, the central nervous system, and the effectors — the organs of movement.

Since we are talking about digital, remember that any computer system offers two things: a way to display information and a way to control it. Take a computer mouse. It is a physical object; you can feel its buttons, you can see its little lights. But it is only a manipulator for controlling information, information that reaches us through our analyzers — the sense organs that relay signals to the brain. What sits on the monitor we perceive only indirectly, through an information model that the person sees. Perception then converts that information model into a conceptual one. And from there, given the situation, comes a decision, and then the execution of the decision.

It is worth walking briefly through the principles wired into us. The brain wants to rest, so it leans on *involuntary* attention wherever it can. Involuntary attention fires regardless of intent: a plain reaction to motion, to external stimuli, to surprise, to novelty, to objects that contrast with their surroundings. There is a second kind — *post-voluntary* attention — that grows out of voluntary attention. Here the goal of the activity is held in mind, an image of the desired future, but at minimal cognitive cost. Driving is the textbook case. An experienced driver and a novice drive very differently; for the expert, every action has dissolved into an automated ritual. The brain need not strain, and the person is content.

An experienced driver, then, is one who *knows*, *remembers*, and *can* drive. Memory is the umbrella term for the cluster of skills that fix, store, and reproduce previously acquired information — the substrate from which experience is built. An external stimulus captured attention; the person fixed the information in memory. Memory does not preserve a hundred percent of everything in full detail — only the volume needed for what we actually do. Few people could reproduce the exact view from their own window on demand, yet that view has been familiar since childhood.

Memory comes in voluntary and involuntary forms. When we dredge up an English or Chinese word, that is a voluntary effort, and nearly every language-learning system is built on it. Involuntary memory interests us far more, because it holds skills close to innate — skills that demand minimal effort. Memory is also short-term, long-term, and operative (working), though the modern field has largely retired the term *short-term* in favor of *working* memory. Working memory lives in close quarters with sensory memory — the visual, iconic kind — which captures the world through the senses and holds it for a vanishingly short span, under 0.4 seconds.

But capture and storage are not the end of it. The next step is reproduction: the actualization of elements of past experience. Tinder's card-sorting is a deliberate appeal to experience we already possess — we all know how to deal out cards, so the moment the app opens, the brain locates a matching mechanic in the interface and the reproduction of old experience begins. When we recall a situation by force of will, it distorts, and it distorts a little more with each act of recall. The brain does not want to haul the requested data up from its depths, so it simply fills in the gaps — which is exactly why people are prone to overestimating themselves. The conclusion writes itself: the best move is to take a proven, well-made interface pattern and reproduce it in your own product, having first made sure the mechanic is all but native to your audience. The simpler, the shorter, the more "innate" it is, the more casual it will feel to the user.

Navigation is another instructive example. Not every city gives its streets unique names; some give them several. In many places a street name is really a name-plus-block-number — you find this in India, in Germany, in Japan. But every locale has its key landmarks: a metro station, a red post. People have long since learned to navigate by them. As a designer you have to supply four things: orientation ("to the east," "further left"), a description of the action ("turn," "make a U-turn"), confirmation ("you'll see a red fountain," "the Kremlin will be on your right"), and error correction ("if you see a mosque, you've missed the turn"). Only then can you build an interface or a mechanic that leans, organically, on behavior patterns that already exist.

This reflex — reduce any repeated action to a single, thoughtless gesture — does not switch off when you close the design file. It leaks into how you run your own machine. Confronted with the trivial daily friction of juggling two Steam accounts, the instinct is not to click through login screens twice but to collapse the whole thing to one keystroke:

```bat
@echo off
TITLE Select Steam account
taskkill.exe /F /IM steam.exe

cls
echo(
echo                                      Select your account
echo                            =======================================
echo(
echo 1) "Main"
echo 2) "Secondary"
echo(

CHOICE /M Select /C 12

If Errorlevel 2 Goto 2
If Errorlevel 1 Goto 1

:2
set username="Main_Username"
Goto end
:1
set username="Secondary_Username"
Goto end

:end
reg add "HKCU\Software\Valve\Steam" /v AutoLoginUser  /t REG_SZ    /d %username% /f
reg add "HKCU\Software\Valve\Steam" /v RememberPassword /t REG_DWORD /d 1 /f
start steam://open/main
exit
```

One number, one press, the ritual automated, attention freed. It is the thesis of this entire essay rehearsed on a quiet afternoon.

## Finding a niche

We now know a good behavior pattern from a bad one. But making a good product is not enough — you have to make it competitive, and that is where the discipline of Competitive Intelligence (CI) earns its keep. Translated bluntly into everyday terms it means competitive reconnaissance, but the substance is a *process*: gathering, analyzing, distributing, and monitoring information about market conditions.

It is fair, at this point, to ask whose job any of this actually is. From the outside it can look like marketing's territory, when in practice the labor is shared with the analyst and the product manager, and the honest answer to "who owns research" could fill a dissertation. The disciplines overlap heavily in their tools, their methods, and their internal clients — the real distinction is *who they serve and what they are after*. Marketing always wants to know how to sell. Product analysts know what users do inside the product. UX researchers know why users do one thing rather than another. Chase the differences a level deeper and they sharpen: market research asks why people need our product at all, a question aimed outward — at the market, at demand, at the future — while user research asks why *these particular* people like or dislike *our* product, here and now, a question answered from inside the company. Market research (the *market research* proper) sizes the market, works out which communication channels can tell the target audience you exist, and estimates the cost of acquiring customers; the various flavors of user research tell you what your users want from the product itself. It sounds tidy. It stops sounding tidy the moment you descend to concrete methods. There is a tempting shorthand — market research is quantitative (the *what*), user research is qualitative (the *why*) — and it is simply wrong. Both draw on qualitative methods, the one-to-one interview borrowed from psychology, and on quantitative ones built from primary and secondary data. Marketing has its own scaffolding in the marketing mix: product, price, promotion, place. And there is a useful pairing worth holding onto — *useful* and *usable*. UX researchers, as a rule, do not run Product Discovery; they specialize in UX testing.

With that settled, CI turns outward. You study the market, your direct competitors, your indirect ones — the JTBD (Jobs To Be Done) competitors, the other products people hire to close the same need — your potential users, and the channels that generate leads. You watch features, prices, reviews, target audiences, roadmaps, and technology stacks. All of it is available if you are willing to look: sit through a competitor's sales demo, crawl their sites, dissect their advertising, track their A/B tests through instrumentation, monitor social media, comb the review sites (TrustRadius, Capterra, G2Crowd), read the forums, the chats, the job boards. The sources are many.

Raw findings need a frame. One of the simplest is four blocks: competitors; products, with their metrics and advantages; the industry, with its trends; and clients. Work those four aspects and analyze your rivals against them and you keep the company in competitive shape. First analyze the market, then compare the findings to your own products on the numbers, then isolate the openings for growth, then ship the changes — an interpretation, really, of the classic HADI cycle of hypothesis, action, data, and insight.

Sizing the opportunity has its own vocabulary, and the base of it is the PAM–TAM–SAM–SOM ladder. **PAM** (Potential Available Market) is the total potential: if you teach iPhone courses in a remote northern town, PAM is every one of the 200-million-plus iPhones sold worldwide each year; for a taxi service it is everyone on earth who takes short rides. **TAM** (Total Addressable Market) is the total target market you could serve — the iPhone courses offered across all of Russia and the CIS, where capturing ten percent would make you the leader; for taxis, the specific countries you operate in. **SAM** (Serviceable Available Market) is the slice you can realistically reach — you live in that northern town and help pensioners with their iPhones, and there are perhaps thirty thousand pensioners there. **SOM** (Serviceable Obtainable Market) is the share you will actually win: you will never reach every pensioner, and not all of them own an iPhone. Two habits keep these numbers honest — talk to people who know the industry from the inside, and do the unit economics.

Competitors must not only be found but segmented, and the frameworks for that are long established: the Gartner Magic Quadrant, the Forrester Wave. The standard cut is four quadrants — innovators, leaders, niche players, and those with a large market share. A young market has no giants; it is mostly niche players. Its signatures are recognizable: no firm association between brands and the product, no obvious leader, the first trickle of investment, a customer count that climbs only gradually. If you find yourself on a market like that, go work at a startup.

A developing market looks different — crowded with SMBs, the small and medium businesses fighting over customers, where the middle of the SMB band sits somewhere around ₽10 billion in annual revenue. Once some of them break away into the lead, you can start calling it a mature market. And when the leaders have climbed so high they can no longer be caught, the market is aging. On an aging market the smart move is to join a strong player, where there are more resources and more room to experiment.

That quadrant picture — a square carved into four — is popular and easy to pick up. But it is only good for organizing what you already know. For learning something genuinely new, reach instead for the Cynefin framework.

## Prototypes

Let us come down from the high-altitude reasoning to work done with the hands, beginning with the selection of ideas. An idea, on its own, is worth nothing — and yet a product's success is hidden precisely inside the idea. Without execution no idea leads anywhere, but executing a *bad* idea merely burns resources. To generate ideas you have to use a great many services and play a great many games, but thoughtfully: analyzing, taking notes, stealing like an artist. You should not only solve your own problems and enjoy yourself, but keep a log of interesting solutions and screenshot the patterns that work. Liked an onboarding? Go through it five to seven times, dissect every small thing, map its flow.

That is how you surface the core mechanic — the foundation the whole product stands on. In Super Mario the core mechanic is jumping and running; in shooters it is movement, resources, and firing; in an antivirus it is find-and-cure; in a camera it is looking at the world through a lens and pressing a button. Hyper-Casual UX is intuitive from the first seconds, engagement is instant and requires no tutorial, and the session is deliberately short — five or six minutes at most — inside which you can show ads or steer the user toward the target action.

There is a subtler question buried in "surface the core mechanic," which is how you decide *which* features matter to users in the first place, and one clean answer is simply to have users rank them. The method has a name — MaxDiff. It is a plain matrix, two columns and a single answer per screen: you hand the respondent a list of names, features, or products and ask them to pick the one that matters most and the one that matters least. These are long surveys, and they pair well with ODI or with Kano ("how would you feel if…"). One caution matters above all others — the respondents must be homogeneous. Run it and the ordering emerges: a first pass might yield *Salary > Brand/Team > Distance from home*; a second screen, mixing old answers with new options, might yield *Adequate management > Salary/Remote work > Flexible schedule*; and step by step you assemble a picture of which factors truly carry weight. The alternative when you want priorities is Conjoint; if the goal is not to prioritize but to *categorize*, that is the Kano model. For MaxDiff I use displayr.com.

Mechanic in hand, it is time to prototype, and prototypes come in two grades: quick or high-quality. Quick prototypes are, by design, low-fidelity — they include only the happy path (the golden scenario) or the core mechanic, and they test a single metric, CPI (cost per install). Invest more time and effort into a higher-quality prototype and you can test a range of product metrics, retention among them. A quick prototype is roughly four times cheaper; a proper one is expensive, and if you bring in a developer, that prototype runs about $2,000 and a couple of weeks.

Before building anything, run the checklist of what you actually intend to learn: whether there will be monetization (in-app advertising, a paid product, in-app purchases, a subscription); which platforms; which countries; which metrics.

That first question — monetization — deserves real care, because choosing the wrong model is expensive. **In-app advertising** is the monetization model of social networks: a feed, with ad units woven into it. The units can be anything, or they can be relevant ads built on the user's own data. That data can be shared with other platforms — in which case the advertising "chases" the user across the web — or access to it can simply be sold. When you are testing a business, target the United States first: the money is there and the audience knows how to pay. Plenty of countries have vast audiences — India, Russia — but a lower CPM (cost per mille). China exists too, but launching a prototype into the Chinese market is anything but quick. Whatever you choose, day-one retention should be at least 50 percent, with 30 percent the acceptable floor, and seven-day retention around 8 percent — a minimum of two sessions a day, three minutes each. You will need a great many users, and the return on investment will take its time. If the product is not viral, you will have to pour money into acquisition. The model suits countries that have historically not paid much: India, Brazil, Russia.

A **paid product** is bought once and forgotten, like a set of dumbbells for the home; upgrading to a new version costs money, the way it once did with Microsoft Word or Photoshop CS. The global trend is a migration from this model to subscriptions, and subscriptions bring in more. Still, the paid model fits nicely if you are shipping an MVP (minimum viable product): you take the money first and refine the product afterward. A subscription is easy to abandon after a month or two of an MVP; a one-time purchase is far harder to walk away from. It also gives salespeople something to play with — discounts, different prices for different clients, selling the product in components, paid license renewals. Under an SLG (sales-led growth) motion, where a salesperson builds a relationship and talks the client into signing, it is exactly right.

**In-app purchases** — donations, in effect — are the backbone of the streaming industry and of casual games. You accumulate a great mass of freemium users who use the product for free and never pay, sometimes 99.9 percent of the whole audience, and much of the craft lies in converting a sliver of them into payers and shaping recurring cycles of payment. The payback period is very long, and everything hinges on the whales, for whose sake you will occasionally rebuild the product outright.

**Subscriptions** are a monthly charge, like a utility bill, comprehensive car insurance, or a gym membership — Spotify, Netflix, Apple Arcade. From the consumer's side it is far better value to buy Spotify for ₽169 than to own eight tracks outright. The company's central problem is that it must keep the appetite for renewal alive — new series, new courses, new content, retention held steady. The cheaper the subscription, the more mass-market it becomes; and mass means no whales. In exchange, revenue is easy and predictable to forecast. Whales do exist inside subscriptions, mind you — ultra-premium tiers, lifetime access, purchases nested within the subscription. Just remember to build in an easy way to cancel, or you have made a roach motel, and to warn the user before the switch from the free version to the paid one, or you have committed forced continuity. This kind of selling sits close to PLG (Product-Led Growth), where the product itself generates the leads.

The thread running through all four models is that you must not be afraid to raise the price. The economics have to close; cash-flow gaps lead to very ugly consequences. The ugliest cousin of the cash-flow gap is a chronic one — an employer that consistently carries a significant average monthly accounts-receivable balance — and while it is a marginal case, the levers are known: insist on prepayment, be willing to decline the work, move to electronic document interchange (which quietly disposes of the "we lost the paperwork" argument). The genuinely effective remedy, unpleasant as it is to say, is to work less with large federal-scale clients in the CIS — itself hard, because as a business grows the quality of its client base tends to fall. Failing that, there are factoring, a bank guarantee written into the contract, and deal insurance. Back on the pricing question: if LTV (lifetime value) is very small and customers churn out of the subscription quickly, sell six months of subscription up front and you will cleanly cut the unprofitable ones. And if the market holds rivals flush with investment or deep financial reserves, the standard differentiation story kicks in — competitors charge for every visit to the doctor, so you charge to cure the illness turnkey, with a schedule and reminders to take the pills. Selling packages usually beats one-off payments. Do not fear raising the price; think instead about the value of the product.

To build a Hyper-Casual experience, the team itself has to be thoroughly "casual." There is a designer, a developer, and a manager — and the designer and manager can each run several projects at once. A quick prototype takes three to five days, a complex one two weeks. One prototype in fifteen or twenty will fire; the rest are thrown away. The one that fires has to be polished, over the following months and through fast iterations, to an ideal state.

Fast iterations of that kind come from a sharp, un-burned-out team and a sensibly organized system of assets and code. Fast prototypes and agile do *not* equal chaos — and the surest proof of that is how a mature team treats its unglamorous work. The business will want new features in every release, but a complex product outruns your ability to catch every bug; the bugs pile up, and each new feature only multiplies them. Convincing the business side to spend a couple of iterations on refactoring is its own art. Academically, Scrum and Agile already provide for technical sprints — the trick is to stop equating a sprint's goal with "a feature." The goal can be the improvement of an internal metric: raise automated-test coverage, or close down technical debt, or improve the features you already have. Try forecasting the velocity of feature delivery with a growing technical debt and without it. Or measure a user-facing metric — NPS (Net Promoter Score) — and show the graph sliding downward release over release.

For the business to believe an NPS figure, it has to be checked against a benchmark. Use a two-sided test, with a 0.025 confidence interval in each tail; if all you need is to prove your NPS is significantly better than the benchmark, a one-sided test will do, and the bar drops from |Z| > 1.96 to Z > 1.645. A worked example makes it concrete. Suppose you have 135 promoters, 47 passives, and 38 detractors, for n = 220. The promoter share is p_pro = 135 / 220 = 0.61 and the detractor share is p_det = 38 / 220 = 0.17, giving an NPS of 0.61 − 0.17 = 0.44 (that is, 44). The variance of a single respondent's score is p_pro + p_det − (p_pro − p_det)² = 0.61 + 0.17 − (0.44)² ≈ 0.59, so the standard error of the estimate is √(0.59 / 220) ≈ 0.052. Measured against a benchmark of 0.40, the difference is 0.44 − 0.40 = 0.04, and Z = 0.04 / 0.052 ≈ 0.79. Below 1.645, so the result is not statistically significant — you cannot yet claim your NPS beats the benchmark. The one-sided p-value in Excel is `=1-NORM.S.DIST(Z,TRUE)`.

## Marketing and analytics

Prototypes come off the line in a flood, so how do you tell which one succeeded? Look at the metrics — the marketing ones (CPI, CTR, conversion) and the product ones (one-day and seven-day retention, session length). If your service is monetized through advertising, that will almost certainly depress retention. The headline measure for an ad campaign is LTV > CPI (with CPI × 1.5–2.5 as the working band): how much money a person brought in, and whether the profit from them covers the cost of acquiring them. Ignore the consulting crowd on this point and evaluate not by individual people — which is usually impossible anyway — but by large groups at once, cities or whole countries, driving CPI down by every means available. And do not forget attribution — where each person came from — which is AppsFlyer and Adjust.

You test the prototype in the US, through Facebook, on iOS — but before any of that you fix, in advance, which metrics you are aiming to move. The target metric might be the number of applications for a product, in the case of financial products, or the headline marketing metric, user-acquisition cost. Ahead of the target metric it pays to compute the leading, or proxy, metrics that correlate directly with it; the same logic governs acquisition, with CAC (customer-acquisition cost), leads, and virality.

How those targets get set in the first place is worth pausing on, because in real life they cascade. Quarterly planning runs in stages. Top management first defines the Global KR — say, an objective to grow revenue by 50 percent, which we intend to achieve through retention of 63 percent — and then the goal is handed down to the directions and, in turn, to the people. The marketing unit will say, plausibly, that it can do nothing about revenue directly, but it *can* lift retention, and it commits to that — reframing the target as, for instance, a 79 percent increase in the number of payments, and detailing exactly how it will get there: plug in new advertising channels, raise prices, work on loyalty. The other teams appraise and detail their own goals the same way, and beneath the units the same story repeats for the teams, until each individual person uses their own skills to move the number. The goals themselves are set to S.M.A.R.T.: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant (matched to available resources), and Time-bound.

Metrics sort, broadly, into three categories. Business metrics: how much money was earned. Proxy business metrics: clicks on ads, satisfaction with the UX, the sentiment of reviews. And DS metrics: F1 score, ROC AUC. Proxy metrics demand caution. If the target metric is a purchase and the proxy is the number of visits to a page of discounted goods, then the visits may be plentiful and still be the wrong visits, off-target every one. Likewise for retention you can treat daily active usage, time on site, the interval between logins, and churn as proxies — while none of them may bear directly on retention at all.

This is also the right lens for judging the promotions you see in shops — "spend ₽1,000 and get a chance to win a prize." You evaluate them by comparing the churn rate before and after the promotion launches. If it worked, you should see a shift right at the ₽1,000 average-check boundary, which is a sign the promotion probably landed; from that shift you can isolate the core "promo" audience and reckon what you spent to attract them against what you earned. Or you can run a permutation test on the significance of the coefficients — sampling without replacement — to be sure the effect is real. The purpose of such promotions, as a rule, is to lift revenue by drawing in new customers or reviving lapsed ones.

Facebook makes the metrics easy to pull — take the SDK, attach a credit card to the ad account, and go. There is no need to fuss over targeting; specify only new devices and a minimum price per impression. Build a few creatives and spend from $50 a day. Facebook comes first because it learns faster than anyone: in three to five days you have a picture. The networks are slower. If Facebook does not suit you, Google Ads works too — create a Universal App Campaign (UAC), set a target CPI slightly inflated, upload the creatives, set a daily budget, and your prototype is out in the world. In my experience Google Ads runs at weaker quality but keeps install volume at an acceptable level, and there text creatives work well. A third route is the ad networks, where playable ads have proven themselves; the temptation there is to lean on misleads, on deception, on provocation, on women's bodies in swimsuits, all of which risks the Streisand effect (not always a bad thing), and all of which rests on each individual specialist's conscience. Video creatives work well universally — Hyper-Casual interaction can be read as gameplay, so you record it, lay music over the top, and you have a functioning ad.

One correlation is worth naming plainly before you scale anything: if a Hyper-Casual audience skews 80 percent male or more, scaling an already-successful title at a sane install cost becomes very hard.

When you analyze the ad campaigns, keep an eye on eCPM (Ad Revenue / Impressions × 1,000), the money earned per thousand ad impressions in the app. eCPM also speaks to how relevant the advertising is: the more relevant the ad shown to a user, the more clicks it draws and the higher the revenue, and mediation platforms handle that job well. Remember that a glut of advertising ties directly back to retention, so run A/B tests to find the ideal ratio of ad volume to session length. If you need to carve out a slice of revenue — a 10 percent platform cut, say — the arithmetic is simply total_revenue / 100 × 10. Two further measures matter here: impressions per session, the number of ads a person watched in one sitting, and the engagement rate (engaged users / DAU), the share of a day's active users who watch ads at all.

And the king of the metrics, LTV, which you can weigh against the cost of buying a user. You know the price of an install, but it is often hard to reckon how much advertising one specific user watched and how much money they brought in — the more so when you are running several ad networks at once, across several payment methods. Ad-based LTV is, for that reason, genuinely difficult to compute. The relatively workable solution is the by-definition model: lifetime × ad ARPDAU (average revenue per daily active user).

Do not lose sight, either, of the quantitative growth metrics — DAU, WAU, MAU (daily, weekly, monthly active users), the count of new users, installs, ad revenue earned — while the qualitative ones tell you about the user: retention, churn rate, sessions per user, session length, conversion to payment (the in-app kind). Suppose you want to raise ARPU (average revenue per user); to do that you need to know which of the linked metrics to raise. If you run an online store and you change the payment screen, that bears directly on ARPU and ARPPU (average revenue per *paying* user). And if you would rather not wait months for the results of a change, you define the leading, proxy metrics — here, perhaps, the number of items added to the cart, or the number of times a promo code is used. When it is conversion you need to track, the (A)AARRR system serves well, especially in its opening (A)AA.

The financial metrics are what a financial model is built on. CAC, conversion, and LTV all have to be tied back to hypotheses; for tools, ueCalc or plain Excel is a perfectly living option. The one thing you must surface is the contribution margin — how much, in the end, you will earn.

## In closing

The Hyper-Casual approach to building a service, then, comes down to four moves: defining a convenient interaction mechanic that needs no tutorial; finding the niche and the market you are walking into; iterating fast with an experienced, active team; and reading the metrics simply, then choosing the right decision *on* those metrics. It resembles growth hacking, with a few modifications of its own. And an approach like this — one that fuses the best methods from across the digital market — lets you test unconventional hypotheses by the dozen, uncovering niches for business growth that classic analysis would never guess at. Or would guess at, but too late.
