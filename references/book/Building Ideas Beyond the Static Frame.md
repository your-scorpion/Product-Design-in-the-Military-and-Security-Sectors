# Building Ideas Beyond the Static Frame
### Storyboards and shooting boards, for advertising and film

*Maksim Tcvetkov · 11 February 2016*

**Storyboards and shot lists for advertising and film.** Every story is a narrative. People used to imagine stories, picture them in their heads, and an artist would simply visualize them. Once film production became an industrialized process, a sharp need emerged to visualize the exact visual sequence a director had in mind. That's the market storyboard artists occupy. The forerunners of the modern storyboard are the comics of the 1920s. The first storyboards appeared at Walt Disney's studio, and today they're an inseparable part of making a film.

A **storyboard** is a film's sketch and framing — a sequence of drawings that serves as a production aid. At the storyboard stage, some scenes that read well on the page can be cut once they turn out visually uninteresting. It should also be clear at this stage which episodes are technically difficult to pull off and better dropped. The storyboard is built on the script, the cinematographer's notes, and sketches of shot composition, angle, framing size, dolly-ins, and pans. Nuances have to be accounted for too — a character's costume, or the fact that night scenes are always shot by wetting down the pavement, walls, and statues and adding a haze of rosin smoke so the figures stay visible. All of this gets worked out in more detail at the concept-art stage, but the storyboard has to convey the overall mood. The cinematographer needs it to picture the lighting scheme for a scene and plan the camera's movement; the director uses it to judge how well the sequence of shots hangs together. In advertising, the art director builds the storyboard, since someone has to judge whether the story actually comes together — and for advertising, more than 20 shots is rarely done.

If you're building a storyboard for a film, the first thing to settle is the format: 4:3, 16:9, or 2.35:1. For panoramic (360°) storyboards, a 2 to 2.5:1 ratio is used. Every frame always gets a number, and space is set aside for comments. A professional storyboard template also breaks things down by action, dialogue, and effects. The storyboard has to show camera placement, shot size, and frame movement (when indicating movement, the arrows shouldn't run past the frame's edges). Comments should use the industry's own [terminology](https://festagent.com/ru/dictionary/). 16:9 is the usual choice, with the frame's width about 1.78 times its height — the 1080p aspect ratio that's the standard for American television.

A **shooting board** and a storyboard are essentially the same thing, but the difference is that a shooting board more often holds only the key scenes — it reads more like a table describing the visuals, the audio, and the text. So: a storyboard is a sequence of sketches for visually planning scenes before shooting. Working on a storyboard is where the shooting board and the "shootingboard" proper come from. A shooting board holds every mise-en-scène and every camera move, the lighting, the camera's movement, where objects sit — the fine details that production will need later. It carries the director's instructions, the lens characteristics, camera framing size, and shooting methods, along with every character's line and every note. Phasing (animation) gets added too, since not everything can be conveyed in words. A shooting board saves time, and time is money — and in Hollywood, all the money has to be paid back after the film wraps, since the budgets are privately financed.

Shooting boards come in a few kinds: special-effects shots, action sequences (fast, active motion), stunts and pyrotechnics, complex camera movement, montage sequences, crowd scenes, and opening/closing sequences.

As the artist on a production, your job is turning the director's idea into something clear, beautiful, thorough, and detailed, keeping all of the above in mind. As Ollie Johnston put it, what matters is drawing *clearly*, not cleanly. The idea for a film is born in the loop of director, cinematographer, and storyboard artist, and what comes out the other end is a storyboard that serves as the guide for the whole team. From there a shooting board gets built, plus a polished version of it for the client and for the artist's own portfolio (careful rendering, plus a sense of montage). At some stages the client will end up preferring a colleague's work over yours — you have to live with that and accept it as simply how the industry works. The industry keeps making adjustments: the marketing plan gets rewritten every six months, concepts and models get redone by the hundreds. Ideas don't get written once — they get rewritten at every stage of production, and that's waiting for your idea just as much as your colleague's.

*A shooting board (not a storyboard!)*

Since an idea reaches beyond a single static shot, it's built from episodes, episodes from scenes, scenes from shots, and shots from individual mises-en-scène. This is the foundation the American industry stands on — the shooting board underlies everything, and sometimes the script itself gets written after the shooting board, since a screenplay, unlike literary fiction, isn't written so much as *constructed*. A great deal of time has to go into working out the idea and how to get it across to the audience. First the idea is created, then the events get broken down by episode, and only at the end do the dialogue and the characters' actions get written. But it isn't all rosy — there isn't much room to get carried away creatively. In most cases the production designer will hand you blueprints, and your job is simply to sketch over them, conveying the shot's mood. In this sense, the screenwriter isn't really the film's author at all — the idea comes from the director, the producer, or whoever else is holding the money. And yet storyboard artists carry the design along with them, and that design can change fairly often, sometimes shaping a shot's whole quality. This is the secret of the American film aesthetic: shots change often, and every shot carries exactly one meaning. That sequence of meanings builds a continuous narrative, with a clear sense of where the character sits in the scene at every moment — which is what holds the audience's attention without overloading them. This is also where the huge budgets come from, so that nobody's afraid to redo anything. The storyboard's endpoint, you could say, is the move into building the animatic.

Standards can't be ignored, either — credits, for instance, are often fitted to a 15-second timing, working out to 89 seconds plus 500ms of silence for transitions; or four 10-minute story episodes within a one-hour series, plus 90 seconds for the open and close, dictated by American TV's ad load. A black screen always goes at the end of the video.

## The foundation of any work

It's always conflict. Its visual expression is contrast — and contrast is everywhere: in the lines of a form, in horizontal and vertical lines, and how they're set against each other. Film's visual components are line (texture), form (dynamics, weight), tone (the ratio of black to white in a composition — everyone reacts to tone in roughly the same way), composition/mise-en-scène, space/perspective, color, and movement/rhythm. Rhythm can express the tension of a scene. A good starting point is a moodboard — photos, sketches, mood boards for getting the director's sign-off. In a sequence of shots, there's often one key frame that sums up, or briefly states, the meaning of that stretch of the piece. Keep the overall shape of your idea or film in mind, but don't forget to focus on the details either.

## Rules exist to build a system. The rules:

**Character.** How do you build a good character? A character has external visual traits — surroundings, appearance — and internal psychological traits, temperament and emotion. A character embodies the story. What matters is making the character a real person, not just a bad guy or a smart girl. The easiest way to do this is to base a character on people you actually know — pull your material from the world around you, so keep analyzing everything that happens near you. Every character carries a conflict. The protagonist is usually the most "normal" one — in Steve Jobs' story, the protagonist is arguably Apple itself. In production you'll need to build a character lineup — a rundown of the characters where, looking at the lineup, you immediately understand what kind of character this is and how they'll relate to the world. Every hero needs a problem they can't simply live with. Develop each character separately, but the finished characters should depend on one another, the way Batman and Superman do.

**Background and environment** — the film's mise-en-scène. It's organized starting at the storyboard stage and wraps up at the layout and animation level. Shots that follow one another in the same environment shouldn't carry a strong tonal break, since that can make the seams between them obvious. Avoiding visible seams is also helped by consistent light direction, matching focus zones, and continuous background motion (a crowd, islands in the sea, street traffic, and so on). One of the most common techniques is the 180-degree rule — the fundamental idea behind narrative continuity. If a character starts moving left or right, that direction of movement has to stay consistent the entire time the character is visible on screen.

**Dialogue.** The main rule for building a dialogue scene: never cross the axis line — this keeps the audience's sense of where things sit geographically in the frame intact. Dialogue follows its own laws: if the character on screen, in close-up, is looking right to left, their conversation partner has to look left to right, and the framing size and eyeline both have to match. The most convenient way to bring in additional figures with their own backgrounds is to have the character turned partway in frame rather than looking straight into the camera — with the line of the character's shoulder pointed toward the background.

![Camera positioning example](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/566.jpg)

When the camera moves, there should be visible elements in frame whose relative position shifts as the camera moves. And moving around the subject against a smooth backdrop creates the impression of a small shift in point of view.

A common mistake when shooting video is placing the character in the center of the frame and pushing in while keeping an equal distance from the subject to all four edges of the frame — the frame ends up narrowing toward the subject from all four sides at once. The correct push-in keeps a constant distance from the subject to only two of the frame's edges. This is achieved, whether tracking or pushing in, by choosing the point of focus in the composition ahead of time — usually the character — and holding its position at a fixed distance from two adjacent frame edges, while the relative distance to the other two edges is allowed to change. This lets the subject's image gradually grow (or shrink) within the frame without ever looking like the frame itself is squeezing in toward it.

In any case, when building advertising, we're working with the viewer's feelings, not with push-ins, pull-backs, or speed changes. Every successful director works with the viewer's feelings.

**Comics.** These are defined, first and foremost, by non-standard panel shapes. A page is the unit of storytelling. The work starts with understanding what panel shape you need to build your visual message at each stage of the story, and you have to be sure that when the panels come together, they form a solid, coherent page layout. You need to know how repetitive or unpredictable the sequence's rhythm should be — will the reading be linear, or unconventional and choppy? Will the panel shapes stay stable, or lean into more diagonals? Action scenes are built on diagonals; whether the lines all point the same direction or scatter chaotically doesn't matter — a tilted (Dutch) angle always reads as dynamic. Beyond that, comics and storyboards are identical in how their frames connect. There are limits, though — getting a laugh on a comic page is much harder than in film, where the movement itself is often what's funny.

Take the square format as an example, considered the hardest to work with. Within that space you can include characters that convey a sense of their defining traits. Since composing within it is difficult, cropping characters is acceptable. A popular solution for a square frame is arranging objects in a circular, pyramidal, or oval layout. It's critical to establish the main subject of the frame and position everything else to reinforce it.

![Square-format composition example](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/566-1.jpg)

Scene rhythm matters across every kind of sequential imagery there is — emotion reaches the viewer through how shots connect to each other, not through any single shot alone. Once you know these technical points, you can break them — and breaking them is how you create.

**Technical points:**

*Extreme long shot* — used to show the environment, the setting of the character's action.

*Wide shot* — shows the character full-length.

*Medium shot* — offers a broad view but trims out elements that could get in the way of the shot's meaning.

*Close-up* — lets you feel a character's traits and their reaction to a situation more clearly. From this angle, the character's reaction matters far more to the audience than whatever provoked it. There shouldn't be too much empty space above the character, and in wide and medium shots you can't crop off the character's head or the lower part of their legs — cropping is only allowed above the knee.

*Extreme close-up* — here the audience becomes one with the person on screen. This isn't the only way to achieve that effect, since there are other techniques too, like a first-person point of view (known as the subjective camera).

![Shot types in cinematography](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/gif_wip_your-scorpion_starwars-2.gif)

A sketch's starting point is always the horizon line and the camera's placement. With a high horizon line, the audience looks down at the setting from above; a low horizon has them looking up at what's happening. A low horizon line is used more often, since it requires less fuss with perspective, shows off distant scenery nicely, and works better with silhouettes — and camera-angle variety is worth keeping in mind too. There's a small trick for placing objects in frame: if an object is bisected by the horizon line, other objects will end up bisected at a similar height as well.

No article on this subject would be complete without a few practical techniques that will save your nerves, and your management's money. Tools for structuring ideas and connections include XMind and MindMaster. As for localizing your idea: write your own story in English yourself, and hire an editor whose native language is English.

Production discipline like this isn't unique to film, either — the same instinct for staged review shows up in book publishing, a pipeline built on its own sequence of specialized roles. A proofreader reads for errors, and ideally there are two of them, each reading the manuscript at least twice — the first pass happening right after the draft comes in from the author. A layout artist typesets the book, and the layout needs its own second proofread afterward, since hyphenation plugins tend to make a mess of things, leaving stray dangling prepositions and the like; a final reconciliation pass follows that. And one rule holds firm throughout: the advance/signal copy never goes to the author. It's the same underlying principle as a shooting board or an animatic — a checkpoint built in before anything ships, so mistakes get caught while they're still cheap to fix.

To close, I want to bring up one of my favorite lines on this subject. Spielberg once said, in *Directing the Film*, that holding onto the sense of a screenplay's prose — hundreds of words of it — in his memory was genuinely difficult, and that breaking it down into pictures and assembling those into a storyboard turned out to be a far simpler, more natural way for him to work. And keep this in mind: the best joke, or the best story, is always unexpected yet predictable — and the worst is the reverse, expected yet unpredictable.

*A closing, practical footnote on the camera side of things, since storyboard and concept artists often end up shooting their own reference photography: if a camera seems to be focusing poorly, the issue usually only shows up on DSLRs, and it's fixed through calibration. Set up a tripod and a target, focus on it without stabilization, and trigger the shutter with a timer — if the target comes out sharp, the equipment isn't the problem. If it doesn't, you're dealing with front-focus or back-focus, which a service center can correct through calibration in about an hour. Two other common culprits: the AF/MF switch on the lens itself, and the focus-area setting, which should be set to manual. (Lens stabilization, for reference, works through a gyroscope plus lens elements that shift without moving relative to the housing.)*

*If instead every shot comes out darker than it should, the fix is in ISO, aperture, and shutter speed. If the issue is how much light the camera is actually capturing, exposure gets corrected through shutter speed and aperture, with ISO acting more like a signal amplifier. Aperture: the higher the value, the less light gets in. Shutter speed governs image brightness — 1/8000 lets in little light, 1/100 lets in a lot. ISO: the higher the value, the brighter the photo. A shot coming out dark is most likely a low ISO, a fast shutter speed, or too closed an aperture — or, sometimes, an older camera that simply doesn't handle high ISO well. And on high ISO's main drawback, noise: shooting in the dark with ISO pushed up and little available light means a lot of noise; at high ISO with adequate light, there shouldn't be noise visible to the eye. If ISO is already low, use a longer shutter speed instead — and don't let the sensor overheat.*
