# Basic Rules for Book Typesetting
### A working reference for Cyrillic/Russian-language typography — the rules that carry straight into English, and the ones that are specifically about Russian

*Maksim Tcvetkov · 23 December 2013*

This isn't an essay — it's a plain set of rules for typesetting. Some of it is borrowed from books and articles, some comes from my own experience and my colleagues'. There's dogma here that's non-negotiable, and there are simple recommendations too. This cheat sheet will help you check just how professionally a piece of work has actually been done. These rules can be broken — on a part-title page, say — but for ordinary body text, they're worth holding to.

A quick note before diving in: a good portion of what follows is universal typesetting practice, the same principles you'd find in any serious English-language reference on the subject. But a real share of it is specifically about Cyrillic and the conventions of Russian-language typesetting — quote nesting, the decimal comma, formal address, and so on — and those are marked clearly as such below, since they don't carry over into English the way the rest does.

# Rules for body text

- The optimal line length shouldn't exceed 60–65 characters (80 at the absolute most), with 30–40 lines per page; the line count across a two-page spread shouldn't differ by more than two lines, and the last page of a chapter (where chapters start on a fresh page) should look at least half full. On the web, text should fill 60–70% of the page's width, with paragraphs separated by a blank line.

- No widows or orphans, top or bottom.

![Widow and orphan lines](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/stroka.png)

- Punctuation always stays at the end of its line; commonly accepted abbreviations aren't split across lines; and single-letter prepositions or conjunctions never get left dangling at a line's end. *(In Russian, single-letter words like "в" or "и" are genuinely common and genuinely awkward left stranded — this rule matters far more in Russian typesetting than in English, where single-letter words at line-end are rare to begin with.)*

- An ordinary page should be filled with text 100% down its full vertical measure, with the last line sitting exactly on the type area's bottom edge. An incomplete page is only acceptable in rare cases — where a non-drop-in heading begins on the following page, say.

- Text should fill at least a quarter of a chapter's closing page (roughly 10–15 lines of body text). Where a closing page is fully filled instead, the text shouldn't reach within four lines of the page's bottom edge, so the page still visibly reads as an ending.

- Text set as scattered "tiles" is harder to read than text set as a continuous ribbon.

- All subscripts and superscripts are set at 6pt.

- Every paragraph indent needs to match, regardless of the point size used in different parts of the text. Indents for footnotes, epigraphs, sidebars, and the like should equal the body text's own paragraph indent, which typically runs at one and a half times the type size.

![Paragraph indent](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/abz.png)

If a document is set without a first-line indent at all, the final line of a paragraph should instead be left visibly incomplete, so paragraph breaks stay legible.

- A paragraph's closing line should, in length, exceed the paragraph indent — a minimum of roughly three characters plus a punctuation mark is the usual floor, and ideally the closing line should run about one and a half times longer than the indent itself; it should never fall short of that by more than that same 1.5 ratio.

- Where a paragraph opens with an em dash, the spacing after that dash across every paragraph on the page needs to be identical and fixed, so the first letters of those paragraphs line up on the same vertical axis — the normal word-space during typesetting is equal to one character's width. The dash itself counts as punctuation; an en dash, separately, is reserved for numeric ranges.

- The same fixed-spacing rule applies after numbering or bullets in a list, so the first letters of each list item line up vertically too. Where a list runs past three items, numerals are the better choice over plain bullets — and in general, bullets paired with Cyrillic text tend to look a little awkward. Long dashes work better, or the bullet's size can simply be reduced.

If round, bold bullets are genuinely necessary alongside Cyrillic text, they need the support of other bold elements nearby to not look out of place.

- Use numbering for a genuinely long list.

- Letter-spacing (kerning pairs aside) needs to stay consistent across the whole text — you can't shrink inter-letter spacing to compensate for word spacing within a line. Tracking (kerning) should only ever be used as a last resort for expanding or condensing a line, when word spacing alone can't do the job, and it shouldn't deviate from the norm by more than 0.02 (this figure can vary somewhat by typeface).

- Leading across a page should stay consistent for every line set at the same point size — though each point size naturally carries its own leading value. In imposition work, leading needs to match across both pages leading up to the sheet's foot. Leading depends on the typeface and typically runs 1–2pt larger than the type size; in Adobe products, the "Auto" leading setting works out to 1.2× the type size, though from experience I'd avoid going below 1.6× in practice. Leading also scales with line length — the more words a line holds, the more leading it needs.

![Leading example](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/interl.png)

- On quote nesting *(this next section is specifically Russian typographic convention — the nesting order below is not how English handles nested quotes, where a double quote holds a single quote inside it instead)*: internal quotation marks are set as "lapki" — Cyrillic-style low-high marks — with the opening mark as a low pair and the closing mark as a raised pair: text «quote „quote within a quote"».

![Quote nesting](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/kav.png)

- The recommended primary quotation marks are guillemets, « and » (called "yolochki," or "little fir trees," in Russian) — the marks used most often in Russian typography. Even for a phrase in a foreign language, the classic Russian guillemets are still the recommended choice; plenty of us were taught to use the Polish-style quotation marks in school instead, but guillemets remain the standard. When one quoted phrase is nested inside another using the same mark, only a single closing mark is placed at the very end: text «quote «quote within a quote». This is really a technical convention more than anything else — the low-high "lapki" marks are equally acceptable for nested text. Example: «"RICH" is a delicious juice!» the marketers claimed.

It's worth flagging that the » character sitting on the "2" key is not actually a quotation mark at all — it's the symbol for arcminutes and inches. If a sentence opens and closes with two different quotation styles, the two should genuinely differ in appearance — guillemets on the outside, "lapki" on the inside. Beyond two levels of nesting, "lapki" cover every further level regardless.

- Percent (%), degree (°), arcminute ('), and arcsecond (") marks are only ever used together with the number they attach to — used without a number, they should be spelled out as words instead. These marks are never spaced away from the number they belong to, but are separated from surrounding text with a regular word space. Where a mark applies to several numbers at once, it's placed only after the last one. Where degree, minute, or second marks attach to a number containing a decimal fraction, they go after the final decimal digit — and decimal numbers within the same context should carry the same number of digits after the decimal point throughout. In expressions like °C or °K, the degree mark isn't spaced away from the following letter.

![Percent, degree, minute, second marks](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/EMK.png)

- If you've decided to set text vertically, it should either be functional text (captions, copyright lines, running headers) or dictated by the format itself — a book's spine, or a narrow vertical banner. Vertical text can also be used deliberately, to heighten contrast, dynamism, tension, or rhythm. Either way, stick to the convention: horizontal text runs left to right; vertical text runs bottom to top; diagonal text runs either left-up-right or left-down-right, with every letter in the line holding strictly to that single line.

- Leader lines deserve their own attention — they should be thin (no more than 25% black on screen, or 0.25pt on paper) while still tall enough to read clearly.

# Headings

- In multi-line headings, every line needs to carry real meaning on its own — where possible, avoid ending a heading on a function word, and prepositions, conjunctions, and adverbs shouldn't end a heading line at all.

- Hyphenated word breaks aren't allowed in headings, except within genuinely multi-line ones — and even then, the result has to read as a coherent unit of meaning.

- Never place a graphic-design element between a heading and its text; every article needs its own heading.

- Where a heading is set in all caps, word spacing should run slightly wider than normal (letterspacing/tracking applied). For headings set at a large point size (roughly 20pt and up), letter-spacing should be pulled in slightly instead.

- A centered heading's line length shouldn't exceed the type area's width minus two paragraph indents (maximum heading line length = type area width − 2× paragraph indent).

![Heading width formula](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/header.png)

- Leading between heading lines should run around 110–120% of the type size; where the heading is set in all caps, that leading increases slightly (120–130%).

- The space above a heading should be larger than the space below it, and the total vertical space a heading occupies together with that surrounding space is best kept as a multiple of the body text's leading.

- At least four lines of body text need to sit above a heading. Three is acceptable, but only if that last line runs at least two-thirds the width of the type area.

- At least three lines of body text need to sit below a heading. Where a footnote follows too, two lines below the heading is acceptable, provided the footnote's first line runs the full column width.

![Heading spacing example](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/2.png)

- In genus-numbered headings ("Chapter 4," "Section 2," and the like), letter-spacing applies only to the generic word itself ("Chapter," "Section") — never to the number that follows, Arabic or Roman.

**Rules for a book's table of contents:**

- Contents-page text is set 1–2pt smaller than the body text's own point size.

- The hierarchy of headings is shown through emphasis and indentation, with indents kept as multiples of the body text's paragraph indent — or, where indentation levels multiply, as multiples of half that indent instead.

- Page numbers are set flush right, following a line of dot leaders spaced evenly apart; regardless of how the heading itself is emphasized, both the page number and the leader line are always set in regular (non-bold) weight.

- Where pages from a different book are appended — as a preview of the next installment in a series, say — page numbering should stay unified across the whole volume, with those extra pages placed before any back matter. The books themselves may differ, but the format belongs to a single edition. As an alternative, those "foreign" pages can carry their own Roman numeral sequence instead.

Fully justified text carries a lot of potential trouble — cramped or overly loose lines, too many hyphenated breaks, an overly short closing line in a paragraph. Because of this, even in Cyrillic typesetting, a dangling preposition at a line's end is tolerated when the text is fully justified. Justified setting like this really only tends to be needed for books — newspapers are typically set flush left instead.

# Tables

- Tables are usually set a few points smaller than the book's body text. For instance: 12pt body text pairs with an 8 or 10pt table; 10pt body pairs with 6 or 8pt; 8pt body pairs with 6 or 8pt. Where a table is genuinely enormous, the point size drops to 6pt and the typeface switches to a sans-serif across every table in the book.

- Column-header text should run horizontally and be centered.

- Where a header's width is narrower than its column is tall, the header is set vertically instead. The general rule for tables throughout: stay compact.

- A table's header row is set 0.5–1pt smaller than the table's own body text, in regular weight. An alternative approach keeps the point size the same but sets the header in bold instead. For a large table, the header's size shrinks by no more than 0.5pt.

![Table example](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/table.png)

- Body-text rows are set flush left, or full-measure justified in a wide column. Header rows are centered, though the leftmost column may be set flush left. Numbers are centered while respecting place value, or set flush right; numbers should always be set in tabular figures. Flush-right setting is almost never the right call otherwise.

- Table rule lines run 0.5pt thick; where part of the table needs emphasis through rules, that line thickens to 1pt. The space beneath a table runs 1.5pt. As the example above shows, rule lines create real visual noise — which is why it's become standard practice on the web to drop rule lines entirely for simple tables. In print, vertical rules can sometimes be dropped too, with a single rule shifted to sit just before the first value in a row instead.

![Simplified table without rule lines](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Group-47.png)

- Numbers in tables need to align by place value; ordinary text aligns flush left. InDesign's table mode can even auto-insert the spacing narrow digits need to align correctly on its own.

Ideally, the numbers themselves align flush right against each other, while the block of numbers as a whole still sits flush left relative to its header and column boundary. But where the numbers in a column represent genuinely different kinds of quantity (a count of milk cartons next to a cat-to-dog ratio, say), flush left is the right call — the same goes for IP addresses and other inventory-style identifiers, which always align flush left.

- A "Table No. …" caption is set no larger than the body text, in bold, centered — and at a point size distinct from the book's other headings. The gap between the number and the caption text runs 4–6pt; the gap down to the table itself shouldn't exceed 10pt.

- Where a table doesn't fit on one page and is tightly bound to its surrounding text, the fix is: write "Continuation of Table No. …" in italics at the top right of the following page, with columns numbered, so the header on that next page only needs to carry the column numbers.

- Numbers are set in a monospaced (tabular) typeface.

- Notes go directly after the table, separated by 6–8pt of space and set a couple of points smaller than the body text; the word "Note(s)" is set in italic with letter-spacing applied.

# Phone numbers

*(This section is squarely about Russian telephone-number convention, worth understanding as such rather than a general typesetting rule.)*

- For international readers, Moscow phone numbers should be written as +7 123 555-55-55. The leading 8 seen in domestic Russian numbers is a Soviet-era long-distance access code, still used today on landlines — but someone dialing home from abroad can't reach it that way, so any number intended for an international audience needs to lead with the country code instead.

- Mobile numbers are conventionally written as +7 123 123-45-67, or 8 123 123-45-67.

- Parentheses mark the optional part of a number. A correctly written example: (+7 812) 555-55-55, used where the city isn't otherwise clear from context.

# Abbreviations

- Abbreviations come in a few kinds. Letter-initial abbreviations are shortened words built from the first letters of a full name (comparable to "MSU" for Moscow State University). Compound-shortened words merge parts of two or more words together. Unit abbreviations shorten by initial letters (comparable to "min" or "Mbit"). Abbreviations are never hyphenated across a line break.

*(A number of the specific abbreviation conventions here — no periods within compound Russian abbreviations, letter-case rules for former-name-derived unit abbreviations — are Russian orthographic convention specifically; English abbreviation style, e.g. for "etc." or "i.e.," follows its own separate rules.)*

- Abbreviations are set without internal spacing and without periods; compound-shortened words and unit abbreviations are set the same as ordinary text.

![Abbreviation example](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/PIC_6.png)

- Where you run into letter-abbreviated physical units that never originated as a person's surname, they're set in lowercase without a period. Unit abbreviations are separated from the number they attach to with a thin (narrow) space.

![Unit abbreviation spacing](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/test.png)

- Indices and exponents appearing within abbreviations are never spaced away from the marks they attach to (kg/mm²).

# Punctuation

- No period follows a heading or subheading set apart from the body text — though a subheading that runs on the same line as the body text is allowed one. No period follows an image caption either, or a table's heading or its internal text.

- A period or comma is never spaced away from the character before it; a comma used as a decimal marker isn't spaced on either side. A semicolon, colon, exclamation point, or question mark is likewise never spaced away from the preceding letters.

- *(Decimal and grouping convention below is specifically Russian/European, the reverse of US English practice.)* Decimal fractions are separated from whole numbers with a comma (0,3). A space separates digits marking different orders of magnitude within a single number: 20 023.

- A hyphen is never spaced away from what comes before or after it — except where it appears inside letter-spaced text, so long as it isn't functioning as a line-break hyphen there. Its job is joining words together.

- An em dash within running text is spaced on both sides with a thin space; in a numeric "from–to" range, the dash isn't spaced away from the numbers at all (125-199).

![Dash usage example](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/promo-sprite-1.png)

- At the start of a line of dialogue, the dash is set off from the words that follow with a thin space. Quotation marks are never spaced away from the words they enclose.

# Illustrations

- Images are placed right after the text reference to them, or on the following page. I place illustrations so the top edge aligns with cap height and the bottom edge sits on the baseline.

- In a modular layout, illustrations should be rectangular. Where an image's background is close in color to the paper itself, it needs either an outline or proper color correction. Where an image has had its background removed and is cropped along the bottom, draw a rule line along that crop edge.

- A caption is set beneath the image, in the same point size as the body text or one point smaller, usually italic and centered. Where an edition's headings are set flush left, captions should follow suit — with the second and later lines indented and aligned to the first letter of the image's own title. The most common failure here is an illustration that isn't actually tied to anything on the page and ends up functioning as pure decoration instead.

- A caption is separated from its image by 6–8pt; the space above the image and below the caption runs 1–1.5 lines.

- Illustrations sit above a block of text — placing one between a heading and its text is incorrect. In full-width text blocks, an illustration is placed to the right.

# Grid

The rectangle is the grid's basic unit — information gets structured through filled and empty rectangles. The most visually prominent elements — headings, illustrations — belong at the center, in the corners, or along the edges.

A grid needs margins, and it's good practice to give the horizontal and vertical margins different values, with the bottom margin larger than the top. Text is aligned to the top or bottom line of the lowercase letters, ignoring ascenders and descenders.

# Common corrector mistakes

- Avoid gerund-derived nouns, formal participial constructions, and long chains of genitive-case nouns. *(This one is Russian-grammar-specific — the genitive-chain problem is a distinctly Russian bureaucratic-prose failure mode.)*
- Address the reader informally rather than formally, by default. *(Russian distinguishes an informal "ты" from a formal "вы" — English has no equivalent distinction, so this is a Russian-specific style call rather than something with a direct English parallel.)*
- Hyperlinks embedded mid-text create visual noise — place them at the end of the text, or as a standalone button instead.
- A slash (cat/dog) is usually better replaced with "and" or "or."
- Don't leave a dangling preposition at a line's end — acceptable in English, not acceptable in Russian.
- Where you run into a construction like "made the top-10" using a mixed numeral-suffix form, spell the number out in full instead.
- Decide up front whether you're using the letter Ё, and stay consistent. *(Ё is a distinct Cyrillic letter that Russian writers frequently substitute with plain Е in casual writing — there's no equivalent choice in English orthography.)*

## A few practical additions from readers, on adjacent ground

**Dates, times, and numerals.** For dates, the everyday format is DD.MM.YYYY HH:MM — in the US, month comes first instead (MM.DD.YY). Printed matter is often recommended to separate time with a period rather than a colon, though I use a colon myself, since a period tends to confuse readers; in tables specifically, I do switch to a period, purely to cut down visual noise. ISO time formatting is also worth knowing, since it comes up occasionally: hours like 11:30–19:45, or a break window like 13:00–14:00. As for numerals generally — spell out numbers zero through ten as words, and use digits above ten; where a single paragraph mixes numbers on both sides of that line, write both as digits for consistency, the same rule applying to ordinal numbers ("first," but "35th").

**On writing style more broadly**, beyond the typesetting rules above: strip out unnecessary adjectives, use fewer pronouns (there's rarely a need to keep clarifying whose object something is, or who a phrase refers to), and drop throat-clearing filler like "in short," "as it turns out," "by the way," and similar interjections. At a higher level, text splits broadly into creative and functional registers — pick the one that actually suits the piece. People generally want to read about real difficulties and real experience, not a tidy success story; text should be clear, concise, and honest. It's worth running text through a spell-and-style checker as a matter of course (the Russian-language [Yandex Speller](https://tech.yandex.ru/speller/) is one such tool). Skip bureaucratic phrasing entirely. If you're reaching for humor, either write it for the roughly 10% of the audience who'll actually get it, or leave humor out altogether — jokes engineered to land with absolutely everyone tend to curdle into the lowest common denominator of comedy.

**Extracting every image from a Word document into one folder, quickly.** Rename the .docx file's extension to .zip — the archive will contain every image inside a Media folder. Alternatively, save the document as HTML and the images will land in an accompanying folder. A third option: save as PDF and use Acrobat Reader's image-export function (labeled Export All Images or Export PDF Images, depending on the Acrobat version) to pull every image out at once.

**Formatting foreign brand and model names in Russian**, car names especially (Nissan Patrol, Lada Kalina, and the like), doesn't have one universally agreed answer. From a UX-copywriting standpoint, it's often cleanest to just keep such names in their original Latin script. Where Cyrillic transliteration is genuinely wanted, I personally don't hyphenate names like these — you'll find guidance from Gramota.ru (Russia's standard-setting language authority) recommending both declension and a hyphen (as in "Ладе-Калине," "Ниссаном-Патролом"), but that reads as overreach to me; I prefer the plainer "Ниссан Патрол" and "Лада Калина" instead.

**Whether a page can open with a single-line paragraph, indent-free.** I generally skip the first-line indent where a page opens with a centered heading — it keeps the layout looking cleaner. From a pure reading standpoint, a paragraph indent signals a pause, and there's little reason to open a page with one.

**Formatting a wrapped signature line — a date plus a place name that overflows onto a second line, in a poem's closing line, say.** Where the place name wraps entirely onto its own second line, the comma after the date gets dropped — the line break itself makes the boundary between the two clear enough without it.
