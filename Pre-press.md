# Answers to the Main Questions About Printing

> A working designer's field guide to prepress: how CMYK and Pantone actually behave, total ink limits, resolution math, black generation, dot gain, color profiles, monitor calibration, screening, trapping, spot colors, file preparation, imposition, and a long tail of production edge cases.


As it happens, I currently spend a great deal of time re-preparing other people's layouts for print. Building on the questions my colleagues raise most often, I decided to write an article after which you should already understand for yourself how to solve printing problems.

## How Printing Works: CMYK and Pantone

Printing uses the CMYK color space — a subtractive color model. CMYK consists of four colors: cyan, magenta, yellow, and black. It is important to understand that the hardware numbers of CMYK do not define a color unambiguously; for that there is Pantone, an exact color. It is used so that the same color does not differ in shade between print shops. Ready-made Pantone fan decks exist, against which print shops check the color that comes off the press. A Pantone is not a color number of the form `#djdjdj`; it is usually given as something like "Pantone 1967," which looks opaque. By certain tricks you can learn a Pantone's color in this form: C 96, M 0, Y 72, K 0. Recognize CMYK? But CMYK and Pantones are different things. CMYK is a set of commands to the plotter telling it how much of each ink to lay down, so CMYK profiles are device-dependent, tied to inks and paper. A Pantone is a finished color from which you cannot deviate a single step. Pantones have their own color scheme, the PMS; Pantones are already built into Photoshop and Illustrator, but on your monitor they will almost certainly still display incorrectly. (There is a dedicated [Pantone and Spot Colors](#pantone-and-spot-colors) section below that goes much deeper into how to choose, mix, name, and verify them.)

## Ink Sums and the Total Ink Limit

In printing you also have to deal with the concept of the "ink sum." Put unscientifically, the inks mix during printing, and to keep the mixture from turning into dirty colors you must not exceed a certain ink-sum limit. This sum usually ranges from 280 to 320; the print shop should give you the exact figure. Example: if you poke a CMYK image with the eyedropper you might get C 83% + M 95% + Y 87% + Key 85% = 350%, which will print dirty because we have ended up with more than 300% ink. To prevent this, convert the image through a color profile that caps the ink sum — the TIL (Total Ink Limit). If a print shop says it has set its equipment to, say, 370 TIL, that should give you pause: large TIL values often expand neither the gamut in the shadows nor the contrast, and a large amount of yellow in a saturated black lightens the picture. Printing with a TIL below 300, on the other hand, trims the gamut in the deep shadows. BUT — for an inkjet printer, TIL = 400 is normal. As for harsh reality, newspapers are laid out in CMYK with RGB images and written with the print shop's profile; at TIL = 240 on coated paper that is a perfectly adequate approach. If the TIL is capped but you need a deep black, you have to play with combining colors — for instance, mix black in proportions of 60-45-45-100, which totals 250: not bad.

For exactly the same reason you cannot make a solid area of 4 cm or more in black ink alone; it is important to build it as a composite color. Offset cannot distribute a single ink evenly over a large area, because non-parallel rollers produce uneven pressure. Safe values for a black solid are taken to be 60% 50% 40% 98%, and the lightest 6% 5% 5% 7%. As you can see, black dominates, to smooth out the color shades. The most capricious solids in the industry are the black-greens — thank our not-yet-vestigial instincts for spotting detail in the green forest thicket.

One more important point: some print shops cap the range on a single ink at 2–98%. I prefer not to work with such shops, since proper equipment can reproduce 1% and even 0.39% (1/255 in 8 bits). Clipping everything below 2% at the RIP strongly affects the quality of skin tones, which drop sharply because of the abrupt tonal boundaries.

Sometimes you need the opposite extreme — an unusually *low* ink sum. A backlit light panel, for instance, may demand total ink no greater than 150%, yet Photoshop will not let you set anything below 200% via *Edit → Color Settings → Custom CMYK*. Photoshop is simply not the best instrument for lowering the ink sum that far; build the appropriate profile in an external tool instead, and check with the print shop whether they have compensation curves for click panels, since those are backlit and behave differently.

## Resolution: DPI, PPI, and the Math

**DPI** is dots per inch. This parameter depends on the job the image will do: the closer a person will view your layout, the higher the dpi must be. For viewing from half a meter, use 300 dpi — the classic figure quoted in every set of technical requirements. In that case each dot is 1/300 of an inch (0.0846 mm). If you want some other dpi, these values help you orient yourself:

| DPI | Dots per mm |
| --- | --- |
| 50 | 2 |
| 72 | 2.8 |
| 75 | 3 |
| 100 | 4 |
| 150 | 6 |
| 225 | 9 |

Do not mistakenly think more dpi always means better quality. A high dpi does not always yield a better final result, while the file size grows. Within an article on the *basics* of prepress, remember the first rule: a print image should be 300 dpi. With practice you will learn for which purposes other values work. So we have an image that must fit into centimeters or millimeters. In the end we have three interrelated quantities — size in pixels, size in centimeters, and resolution. Resolution is a measure of the sharpness of detail in a raster image, counted in pixels per inch (ppi). For viewing on a monitor this value can be anything, since it does not affect quality; for print it is an important parameter, so until the output device is known, a digital image's magnitude can only be judged by file weight.

You cannot work this out without math. Make a 10×10 cm image at 300 dpi in Photoshop. In Image Size you see 1181×1181 px, assuming Pixels/Inch (switch it if it is set to Pixels/cm). Count: 1181 px corresponds to 10 cm at a resolution of 118.1 px/cm, i.e. 299.974 px/inch (dpi). Or 1181 px at 300 px/inch gives us 3.9366 inches, i.e. 9.999133 cm. A certain mismatch, no? If you read the TIFF 6.0 spec, you learn that the file records dimensions in pixels and the resolution, while linear dimensions are a computed parameter. But Photoshop is not perfect, and its linear-dimension precision for centimeters is two decimal places, so it shows 9.999133 cm as 10 cm (the error depends on resolution and image size and can reach 0.1 mm). And the magic: if you change the dimension from 10 to 11 and immediately back (without resampling), the resolution reads not 300 dpi but the fabled 299.974. The difference plays no real role, since the RIP cares only about the image's linear dimensions and its pixel count. But if you are a perfectionist, use 254 dpi — that is 100 px/cm, so in Photoshop, with a size-entry precision of 0.01 cm, at this resolution 0.01 cm = 1 px. Remember that perfectionism is the intent to achieve a knowingly unattainable result.

This same effect is why whole numbers turn fractional on export — a 600×600 mm canvas becoming 600.45 mm in a TIFF. You specify the size in millimeters, but a raster file is made of pixels, and pixels are indivisible. Convert 1/300 of an inch into millimeters and you get the file's resolution. If it is critical to output a TIFF with no fractional numbers, set the resolution in dpm rather than dpi; in my view that is excessive perfectionism for print files of that size. The general idea: specify a whole number of pixels per millimeter — 254 or 512 ppi.

To compute an image's size in inches from its pixel dimensions and ppi, Photoshop does it for you, but the general values are: at **600 ppi**, 3000 px ÷ 600 = 5 in and 2000 px ÷ 600 = 3.33 in; at **300 ppi**, 3000 px = 10 in and 2000 px = 6.667 in; at **150 ppi**, 3000 px = 20 in and 2000 px = 13.33 in; at **72 ppi**, 3000 px = 41.67 in and 2000 px = 27.78 in.

To measure the real print size of an image in Photoshop, first make sure the resolution is 300 ppi with the Resample checkbox off (it governs recalculation of the size). Then take the Ruler Tool, measure the region of interest, and read the dimensions in the Info palette. To find the scale you need, take your required final size, multiply by 100, and divide by the value from the Info palette — and mind the units. Example: required leaflet height 297 mm, current Info value 440 mm; 297 × 100 = 29700, divided by 440 = 67.5%. Enter that scale in Image Size after switching the field to Percent and ticking Resample.

When stating dimensions, by the way, the order in Euler coordinate measurement is always W × H (width by height).

A small trick: want to see an image in a PDF at its original size? In Adobe Acrobat (*Preferences → Page Display*) set the resolution to 72 dpi instead of the default 99, and the PDF on screen will at once occupy the right number of web pixels.

We also often meet these cryptic symbols in technical requirements: Eurostandard, Dot Gain 15%, Black Generation Light, Black Ink Limit 98%, Total Ink Limit 300%, UCA Amount 0%, and so on. Many ignore all these baffling squiggles and, with an innocent smile, apply the Fogra 39 or 27 profile — Fogra 51 at best — without even knowing whether European inks and type-1 paper will be used. So what are we ignoring? Let us work it out.

## Black Generation: GCR and UCR

As we know, of the 400% ink available in the CMYK scheme, no more than 320% can be used in print, and that only if just one color is at 100%. With flexo or single-color solids we can easily control the ink sum, but in photographs you can meet all 400% in use. How to cope? Simply lightening the whole photo is not the way out in 99% of cases. We already know the fix is to apply a color profile that lowers the TIL — and here is where the magic of GCR and UCR happens. That "magic" is called the methods of black generation during color separation.

If you go to Photoshop's color settings and choose Custom CMYK, a separation-settings window opens where you can specify the maximum total ink density and much else. First the fundamentals: the Separation type — GCR and UCR (Gray Component Replacement and Under Color Removal). These procedures were born to shift values like C45 M45 Y45 toward K45; as a result we save ink.

It is important to account for the fact that ordinary **process** inks are not perfectly transparent, and the order in which they are laid down plays a role in the final result. This question usually surfaces at the separation stage, where it is well known that M+Y and Y+M are far from the same thing — the first is a "pioneer" red, the second a crimson. Good shops monitor this two-ink overlay (the binary) deliberately when printing offset. The most problematic ink is yellow: in offset it is semitransparent and light, and can lighten the final print.

**UCR (Under Color Removal)** replaces CMY inks with black ink, acting mainly in photo regions where the total ink percentage is very high. The output has little black ink and beautiful bright colors, but you risk losing contrast. Crudely, UCR is an image painted in three CMY inks with the shadows underscored by black — replacing CMY with an achromatic color in the shadows.

**GCR (Gray Component Replacement)** replaces CMY inks with black mixed in with a small coefficient of the process inks. The output is an image printed in black and colored with the three CMY inks. For most work GCR is preferable; it is on by default, preserves good contrast and detail, and in slang is often called "heavy" black generation.

Why does choosing GCR unlock more options? Because UCR is one variant of GCR, and items like "Black Generation" and "UCA Amount" are simply not needed in UCR. I advised working with GCR, so let us look at all the settings. **Black Generation** becomes active under GCR. Choosing the **Light** setting means a black-ink content in the base color of no less than 40%. The values, with explanations:

- **Light** — 40% and up; much CMY, minimum black (minimum black stability, maximum gamut).
- **Medium** — 20% and up.
- **Heavy** — 10% and up.
- **Maximum** — 0% and up; much black, little CMY (here even one-percent "dirtying" CMY is replaced by black). A fine way to save expensive colored ink.

UCR has no obvious way to set the amount of black, but in practice it is close to Light at 60%.

![The Custom CMYK separation-settings dialog in Photoshop](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2012/09/Screen-Shot-2016-06-23-at-17.33.48.png)

Two related parameters you will meet in profile descriptions are **Black length** and **Black width**: roughly, the point at which colored inks begin to be replaced by black, and the strength with which the amount of black increases — both working by the image's tone. Black generation exists in the first place because of the crooked black-generation algorithm Photoshop uses; in the ECI profiles I recommend, black is generated roughly as in Photoshop at GCR Medium with UCA Amount −20%.

## Dot Gain

Next, in Custom CMYK, note **Dot Gain** — the increase in the area of the printed elements as the paper absorbs ink. The printer prints in ink dots, the ink spreads on the paper, and the result differs from what was expected. Dot gain is unavoidable, regardless of the blanket's quality, but it can be compensated. It depends on the paper you plan to use, on the ink's properties, on the equipment — on everything. The paper choices are Coated, Uncoated, and Newsprint. Dot gain of 20–25% for offset is fine; for coated, 15% is acceptable; for newsprint, 35% is the maximum. Bear in mind that saturation and contrast depend on the printing method: on coated paper both are three times higher than on offset, and on an Epson WT7900 three times higher than on coated. For maximum black contrast, designers order a 2D or 10L laydown on the black ink, which increases both dot gain and saturation.

Beyond the obvious mechanical dot gain there is optical dot gain. Black on paper absorbs more light than yellow; the more light reflected back to the viewer, the smaller the dots seem through perception.

Photoshop automatically adds compensation when converting RGB to CMYK, accounting for coated/uncoated paper. Illustrator does not compensate for vector shapes, so it is normal to expect a slightly darker picture in print than in Illustrator — but no more than 2% if the printer's compensation curve is set up properly.

**Black Ink Limit** — the cap on black ink. You can leave it at 100%, but 98% is better.

**Total Ink Limit** — the cap on the sum of all inks, usually 300%. In essence, how much ink a given paper can hold; more ink means a better gamut.

**UCA (Under Color Addition)** — available only under GCR, it governs the extra ink that remains after CMY separation in neutral regions: the addition of CMY inks in gray (black) proportions into shadow areas. Zero by default; raising it increases the density of neutral regions. Crudely, it is used so that shadows do not turn too gray under Heavy and Maximum.

## Saving and Exporting Files

Remember that the above describes converting an image to CMYK — the final stage of layout preparation. Save to uncompressed TIFF (byte order under Windows) or to JPEG without compression (quality 12). You must be sure the image's dimensions will not change further and the quality stays at 300 ppi. A raster image is processed in the knowledge that sRGB cannot convey some CMYK shades — and even Adobe RGB cannot fully do so — but for print preparation use Adobe RGB (1998) with the profile embedded.

Images at 300 ppi can weigh a great deal. The accepted fix is ZIP compression: it does not distort the image, the photo's quality is unchanged, and the result will please you (a PDF exported one way weighs 591 MB; after Distiller, 137 MB). Also, there are still print shops on the market whose RIP behaves very badly with JPEG compression. ZIP is no panacea: the more unpredictable the neighboring pixels in an image, the worse the compression result.

When laying out a catalog in InDesign with RGB JPEGs placed, you do not need to convert each raster by hand. On export to PDF, choose the Output tab and set **Convert to Destination (Preserve Numbers)**, then specify the required CMYK profile below; after Export, everything is converted to CMYK, images included. Keep in mind, though, that modern digital and wide-format equipment such as HP Indigo can give a better result from RGB than from CMYK — if the printer accepts PDF/X-3 and PDF/X-4 on input, you can probably feed it RGB for a better result. This applies to photos only; spot colors and raster brand elements are better converted to CMYK in advance, if you want, say, a true 100% magenta rather than CMYK (1%, 79%, 0%, 15%).

![The InDesign PDF Output tab with "Convert to Destination (Preserve Numbers)" and the CMYK profile set](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Screen-Shot-2019-01-09-at-9.29.21-AM.png)

If you work on a Mac and graphics copied out of Word or Excel come out garbled — through copy/paste or through PDF — generate the PDF not with Apple's default Quartz PDFContext but with the Adobe Mac PDF Plug-in or similar; thanks to Adobe Distiller the problem should resolve. And to convert an InDesign PDF into a PowerPoint presentation, there is [pdf.io](https://pdf.io/).

This is all well and good — now you know which parameters govern which processes and can more or less control the result. At the print stage, extra factors come in: aniloxes, the material, the mounting tape, the polymer. The designer's job is to know about this and to make the print shop's work as easy as possible by doing good separation.

### A practical aside: nonlinear gradients and banding

A print shop may warn that the attached gradients "will be nonlinear." Gradients are drawn in a linear representation — start 0%, middle 50%, end 100%. If PostScript does not support native gradients, they are output as a set of bands; moreover, exporting gradients from some programs also breaks them into bands. But that is raster, and raster gives no trouble. The deeper question is how the color value at a given point of a native gradient will be recomputed, and whether the results will match — and the answer lies in 8-bit versus 16-bit precision. The range 0–255 is 8 bits; you can dial a thousand variants across 0.0–100.0%, which is already 10 bits. On top of this sits the nature of our color perception: a linear gamma at 8-bit coding gives excessive precision in the highlights and insufficient precision in the shadows. So, formally, converting 8-bit(g=1) → 16-bit(g=1) → 16-bit(g=2.2) loses fewer gradations than 8-bit(g=1) → 8-bit(g=2.2), and the losses fall in the highlights, where linear data has surplus precision.

Take an example: many clean gradients under Fogra 39. *Assign* the print profile and the linear gradients become nonlinear; but *Convert to* it and the clean gradients are not preserved — dirt is added — though linearity is kept. To compensate for this, DeviceLink is used. There are many further color-reproduction nuances besides — linearization, profiling, gamma. Want to avoid the problem? Bring the shop a TIFF, or even a PDF out of Photoshop: 16 bits throughout, with surplus precision over the whole range. Above all, do not bring a Photoshop EPS with DCS applied (EPS-5).

That was the clever, theoretical answer. In practice you will have to deal somehow with the nonlinearity and the resulting striped gradients. Make the gradients raster and add noise to them. The best way to add noise: create a layer filled with gray (CMYK C50 M50 Y50 K50, or RGB 128/128/128), apply Add Noise, and set the blend mode to Soft Light / Overlay / Hard Light. Hard Light adds noise into channels with no current color; Soft Light / Overlay do not. **Use Dither** (8-bit/channel images) works similarly. When you increase an image's contrast, the number of color gradations exceeds 256 and you begin to see bands on gradients; dithering adds noise at contrast boundaries, and after several transformations unpleasant artifacts can appear. So work in 16 bits from the start, and tick Use Dither in Color Settings only when finalizing your work.

## Color Profiles and RGB-to-CMYK

There are, as far as I know, three main separation standards: SWOP, Japanese printing, and the Eurotriad. The chief difference between them is the inks used — the red of the Eurotriad is not the red of Japanese printing. This matters when you take ink percentages from foreign sources; manufacturers observe all the rules strictly within each standard. That is for offset. For digital printing there is always a conversion from the CMYK profile to the print device's profile; the Adobe profiles do nicely as the input profile, but it is always better to check with the shop which profile to take. If you need every kind of printing, build a master layout in Adobe RGB and convert it for each print method.

As for a universal profile: Fogra 39 for coated, Fogra 38 for offset, or ISOcoated_v2_300_ECI. SWOP is the print standard for the United States and Asia (and for newspapers in China), so it is better to use ISOcoated_v2_300_eci.icc from eci.org, or at least Euroscale (up to Fogra 27). Converting from CMYK to CMYK on the print shop's side is a perfectly good solution when you cannot convert from RGB to the required CMYK yourself — and the common objection that on an uncalibrated monitor you "saw it differently" proves little, since by that logic you could abandon Pantones too (they live in the States and use different pigments).

You can build your own ICC profile straight from Photoshop without learning new software — it is not especially hard. Go to *Edit → Color Settings* and choose Custom CMYK from the dropdown, then configure to your needs (say, Dot Gain 18%, GCR, Black Ink Limit 97%, Total Ink Limit 310%). The main popular ICC parameters: **Ink Colors** (choose the ink set for the paper type — Coated or Uncoated); **Gray Ramp** (the role of the different inks in generating neutral gray); **Dot Gain** (compensation for the dot's size change on paper); **UCA Amount** (replacing black with color); **Black Generation** (the range of color that can be replaced by black); **Black Ink Limit** (the overall black cap); **Separation Type** (UCR removes the lower colors, GCR replaces the gray component).

![Building an ICC profile through Custom CMYK in Photoshop](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/shot_170714_101847.png)

That said, Photoshop builds separation profiles poorly. I would look toward [Color Toolbox](https://www.heidelberg.com/) and [i1Profiler](https://www.xrite.com/), with [ArgyllCMS](https://www.argyllcms.com/) as the more budget option. (And if you do stay in Photoshop, you will additionally want ColorLab to find the monitor's gamma function and ICCInspector to compute the color coordinates of the monitor's primaries.) A more accurate profile built through specialized software gives a better RGB → CMYK conversion for specific equipment — and that is precisely why "stock" Photoshop separation can lose much of an image's saturation. For printing on mugs or phone cases — that is, transfer and fixing in a heat chamber — people often open a profile with Profile Editor, GretagMacbeth, ArgyllCMS, ColorLogic's CoPrA, or colorAnt and make manual edits for the specific situation; i1Profiler has had its day, and even ProfileMaker is better. Different RGB spaces have different starting gamuts, and converting them into the same CMYK gives different results; the better the RGB primaries match the dominant wavelength (Color Dom λ:nm), the better the result — you can, for instance, shift green from 533.3 nm to 535.9 nm and get a new balance between the colors. For illustrations destined for print, ECI-RGB v2 paired with the matching coated profile is not a bad choice. But saturation is achieved more by print technology and photo work — multiple ink passes, Pantones, the Perceptual and Relative Colorimetric intents, work on the CMYK black channel, and printing on a good Epson onto glossy photo paper — than by profiles alone. There is little point preparing an image for the "large" gamuts (eciCMYK v2 / FOGRA59, FOGRA53) and preserving the saturation if you then print offset or coated with the smaller Fogra gamuts (39, 45, 47).

## Monitor Calibration and Color Management

Is it correct to use a monitor's sRGB-emulation mode? Categorically not. Monitors have a requirement of 160 cd/m² for the luminance of white per square meter; the device cannot track conformance to that parameter, and you will not get the sRGB gamut you wanted. The only correct way to see an image in true sRGB is to use the full gamut with properly configured color management. You also must not assign the sRGB profile as the monitor's profile, since the monitor has entirely different factory parameters that do not coincide with sRGB.

For calibration I recently worked with the i1Pro 2 spectrophotometer and the EFI ES2000 — quite acceptable, and they did much of the work for me; the EFI ES2000's software is a little less "familiar" than X-Rite's. If you order the service from someone, try to find a technician with a relatively new instrument. Simply ask them to set brightness to 100–160 cd/m² for the luminance of white, D65, and Gamma 2.2 or Gamma sRGB — or to the lighting conditions, if you work at a print shop. And do not forget to "warm up" the monitor before the technician's visit. To check the technician's work, create a full-screen black-to-white gradient and look for posterization or missing shades: in the light part of the gradient you should be able to read the difference between L100 and L99, and likewise in the dark part.

## Screening: Linature, Screen Angle, and Dot Shape

Beyond CMYK at 300 dpi, three further factors determine print quality: spatial frequency, dot shape, and rotation angle.

**Linature, dpi, screen (spatial frequency).** A little history: rasterization was first used in the mid-nineteenth century. There were no MacBooks then, so an image was reproduced with a glass plate engraved with many fine lines, placed over a sheet of photosensitive paper; light was projected through the photograph and exposed particular regions. Dark areas became sets of large dots, light areas sets of smaller dots; the large and small dots created the effect of tones, and this principle still works, with small changes. So yes, a printed picture is made of dots (not to be confused with pixels!). Even software rasterization in assembly works, broadly, the same way.

The printer's main problem is how to print hundreds of grays with one black ink and millions of colors with four. To print a digital image it must be converted to binary form, since a printing device has a binary output. That is done by the RIP (Raster Image Processor), which converts pixels into dots. One mark of a good RIP is the presence of the Adobe PDF Print Engine (APPE); PostScript is a dance with a tambourine.

**Screen density (spatial frequency)** sets the density of the grid and thus the detail of the final image, measured in lines per inch (lpi); the higher the lpi, the higher the quality — though you cannot jump above the equipment's capabilities. Spatial frequency depends on the printing technology, the document type, and the equipment's characteristics. Universal values: for high-quality advertising, top-class art reproductions, and luxurious print in general, 300 lpi is recommended; informational bulletins will tolerate 80–100 lpi; newspapers 133 lpi; consumer magazines, catalogs, disc covers, and glossy mailbox advertising 150–175 lpi. As always, get the details from the specific shop. With glossy outdoor materials, remember that gloss reflects light and seems lighter, while matte absorbs light and seems darker.

Say we have a 300 dpi image with 256 gradations of each color. Presses are often tuned for 175 lpi with a factor of 1.7, yielding the desired 300 dpi (175 × 1.7 = 297.5). But that does not make other dpi values bad — such numbers lead to moiré, yet sometimes the imagesetter result can be even better than 300 dpi. A small rule a guru once whispered to me: to preserve detail, the best value is 300 dpi, but if especially smooth object edges matter, 360 or 600 dpi. 300 dpi is the most universal value only because the main quality criterion is image detail.

In real life, offset varies despite all the theory in articles and textbooks. For relatively good printing at a high linature, the equipment should manage 150–175 lpi and the image should accordingly be 240–350 ppi. But for black-and-white engravings, scan at 2400 ppi and 1200 ppi, retouch, output experimental films and plates, make proofs, and compare with the original, and the difference almost always favors 2400 ppi — even though everywhere it is written that scanning above 1200 ppi is pointless and only inflates the file. For engravings the offset plates need a resolution around 2500 lpi, so with some luck you may get to work with 5000 and even 12000 dpi. For scanning I use NAPS2 and recommend it.

The ink itself in offset is mixed in the order KCMY, by ink viscosity: black is the most viscous, yellow the least; the most viscous ink is laid down first.

The abbreviation dpi is read thus: 300 dpi tells the output device that each dot in this image should occupy one three-hundredth of an inch (1 cm = 0.394 in). The number of dots that fit on a horizontal inch indicates how many of the 256 shades per CMYK channel can be printed. With black, dot 0 means white and dot 256 means black — dot 120 is a neutral gray, up to 256 shades of one color per cell. A screened image can have no halftones: ink is either present or absent, like transparency in a GIF. Ink thickness is not adjustable; we can manage only dot size. Hence the definition of screening: turning pixels into dots, the goal being to make the result invisible to the eye, creating the illusion of smooth, continuous color.

**Screen angle.** The picture is a grid, and the grid can rotate at a given angle, each CMYK color rotating its own way. The screen angle is the set of angles at which the lines of screen dots sit relative to one another; it determines whether the grid in the final image will hurt the eyes (moiré).

[![Dot tilt angle](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2012/09/untitled-1-300x151.png)](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2012/09/untitled-1.png)

By default the rotation for grays is 45 degrees. For all CMYK: cyan 105, magenta 75, yellow 90, black 45. In practice you may meet a ±7.5-degree rule with angles of cyan 7.5, magenta 67.5, yellow 82.5, black 37.5. The four CMYK dots together form a kind of rosette. With improper orientation of the screen structures, moiré appears — and even if you try hard and pick the right angles, the colored regions will still sin with "rosettes." Moiré can be visible not only at the edges of design elements but on solid filled areas too. The angles work on this principle: the eye reacts to a rotation angle provided it does not coincide with a horizontal or vertical line. 45 degrees is the most acceptable compromise, but if the final layout needs to emphasize diagonal lines, the 45-degree angle can reveal jaggedness (I met this often early in my career). Yellow is least susceptible to jaggedness.

**Dot shape.** At low spatial frequencies the dot shape is easy to make out — take almost any mailbox flyer, look closely, and you will see those dots, that awful moiré. Mostly round dots are used; for crisp drawings, square. There are many other shapes — diamonds, ovals, and more. By default the dots change shape: round in light and dark areas, square in the midtones (square dots give better detail). Only undisputed, experienced, clinically fanatical print professionals may alter this algorithm.

A reader once asked why scans of old photographs show the print dots described above, and whether they can be removed. They must be removed, or you get moiré; plugins with Descreen functionality will help, and to make the plugin's work easier, scan the photo at 600 dpi.

A more conceptual confusion is also common: a tradesperson tells you "300 dpi CMYK is not enough" because your layout did not account for lpi and ppi. Resolution is counted in dpi, ppi, and lpi — all inches. The mix-up between dpi and ppi is understandable, since sources disagree; both usually denote an illustration's output resolution. For files it is customary to use ppi (pixels per inch), for devices dpi (dots per inch). A requirement might read: at 1200 dpi, 85–110 lpi linature, digital-image resolution 150–200 ppi. Digital plotters mostly run at 720 dpi. **lpi (lines per inch)** is the screen linature — the number of dot rows transferred to the plate per unit length. The discrete signal is doubled, so at 75 lpi the print resolution is 150 dpi; correcting for poor human eyesight you multiply by 1.5 rather than 2, giving 112 dpi for 75 lpi. Different linatures mean different tone-reproduction curves, and color can lighten considerably. Linature is a formal technological parameter: anilox rollers at 1000 and 1500 lpi give similar optical density and dot gain on a solid. Quality bands by linature: luxurious quality 300 lpi; ordinary quality print such as covers or art catalogs 170–200 lpi; ordinary magazine quality and flyers 133–170 lpi; offset paper (books, forms) 85–120 lpi; newspapers 75–133 lpi. In theory. In practice shops often claim 200 lpi rollers when the equipment manages only 175; you can verify this only by visiting in person — I once went and adjusted ruined rollers with my own hands, or the result was a horrible moiré. Rollers differ too: traditional ones hold 3.1 cm³/m², newer ones 5.4 cm³/m². The best way to avoid such problems is not to work with shops that deliver low-quality runs.

## Trapping and Overprint

**Trapping** is the gap between color boundaries — visually, additional colors at the edges of contrasting objects. The crutch is needed because of poorly set registration in the press, from misalignment of the plates. I make trapping 0.2 mm in my layouts; the general rule is that trapping equals half the screen linature, though exact values depend on the job. Often it is enough to use the standard *Effects → Pathfinder → Trap*. By hand, assign the object a stroke (inside or outside, no matter) and set it to overprint; you may lower the stroke's opacity so the contour is unnoticeable. This works for general cases, but small white text on a black CMYK, for instance, is more than hard for printers, and the trapping above will not help.

[![Trapping example](http://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/6.png)](http://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/6.png)

![Trapping a stroke with overprint — the harder case of fine detail](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/6-1.png)

A frequent overprint question: is 20% overprinted on 60% a result of 60%, or 80%? Overprint does not sum, unlike Multiply, which sums black — in practice, overprint of 20% over 60% = 20%. And for holes in a varnish the trick is overprint 0% over 100% = 0%, yielding a spot color. There are two kinds of overprint: OPM 0 and OPM 1 (in Illustrator, Standard overprint mode and Illustrator overprint mode). They differ in how they handle CMYK colors with zero values: with one mode, if the upper object has yellow = 0, the corresponding percentage of yellow in the object below is removed; with the other, the color of the object below is left untouched. And if Pantones display oddly in Illustrator, turn on Overprint Preview to emulate Lab; otherwise the editor emulates CMYK for the Pantones.

## Pantone and Spot Colors

A Pantone is a guarantee of the needed shade, with no variability — and some Pantone shades cannot be reproduced in CMYK at all. Pantones are chosen from catalogs, where a special Pantone Matching System (PMS) number is given for each shade. You must account for the paper type, since coated and uncoated behave differently: coated keeps the image bright but leaves a glossy surface, while uncoated soaks up the ink for a less bright result with a matte surface (the catalogs account for this). There is also the SWOP/EURO split — SWOP is the print standard for the USA and Asia, Euroscale is used in Europe. The catalog itself should be no more than a year or two old, since the colors fade. If feedback from the shop says "assign a gold," an experienced specialist knows even without the catalog that this is Pantone 871–874. There is an old joke that if you get a bad client, or want to torment a printer (which I do not recommend), specify the company color Pantone Reflex Blue — its shade variability is well above 0.05 dE — and drop that Pantone onto a tint screen.

Choosing a Pantone from the catalog, you choose a spot color formed not from CMYK but from a pre-mixed ink added separately to the press. Any Pantone is described at once by RGB values, an HTML hex code, and CMYK values, which lets it be reproduced both in print and on a monitor; very often a Pantone is chosen from an existing web color — first the RGB, then the closest catalog match. Once you have the name (of the form PANTONE 3561 C), send the corresponding chips to the printer for calibration: these are perforated tear-off paper squares, three per solid Pantone color, which also let you show the client the exact color. All the popular software ships with Pantone libraries — Adobe Photoshop, InDesign, QuarkXPress, and the rest. In Illustrator and Photoshop the libraries live under *Window → Swatches → Open Swatch Library → Color Books → PANTONE*. To convert a Pantone to CMYK, use the presets under *Color Books → PANTONE color bridge CMYK PC* (for coated) or *CMYK UP* (for uncoated); ignore the common internet advice to use *Edit → Edit Colors → Convert to CMYK*, which ignores the compensation formulas for different papers. Pantones can be mixed: there are formulas for the proportions to mix base Pantones into a given shade, and the fan deck shows the percentage ratios. On a bad monitor the colors display incorrectly, of course. Hand the shop CMYK + one color and discuss it with production; the shop will say they lack that Pantone and offer an alternative, and you insist on your chosen Pantone — let them buy it (€20/kg, silver and gold €40), though purchasing will stretch the deadline. My own criterion for choosing a shop is, at minimum, equipment that can print in seven inks.

![Pantone libraries and the color-bridge presets in Adobe software](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/Untitled-2.png)

**Why do Pantones display incorrectly on screen,** their color differing markedly from the fan deck? If a color profile were enough, there would be no need to update the fan decks every year. Use the profile for your press (ideally something like an HP Indigo 5500), since RGB, CMYK, and Pantone are separate worlds. All Pantones display incorrectly on any uncalibrated monitor — the shade is usually muted, since Pantones are quite bright and saturated. Only about 60% of the fan-deck colors can be reproduced in CMYK. Pantones come as C (coated) and U (uncoated), used by paper type; uncoated absorbs the ink (which alone makes correct on-screen rendering impossible — absorbing makes the color duller), and Pantones often fall outside the monitor's RGB gamut, all on the assumption that Pantones are conceived as opaque (solid) colors. For example, PMS 287 and PMS 300 look identical on screen, though only the first is the blue of the American flag.

**Using a Pantone in a photo.** Painting a halftone image with a Pantone such that it matches the reference color 100% is an unsolvable task — you must account for trapping (ink overlay) and the tone change when a spot ink is screened (autotype tinting). If the task is simply a Pantone with transparency on a colored background, use overprint; if the background is dark, print it over a light Pantone, and achieve a smooth transition from Pantone to ordinary ink with trapping. Technically you make a mask: select the full range of the needed color in the Channels panel with Ctrl-click on a channel, then Ctrl+Alt/Shift-click another channel to subtract/add to the selection; check the selection edges very carefully, accounting for the ink-laydown order and trapping; create a new channel and assign it a Spot color, which it is vital to verify in Adobe Acrobat Pro. From Photoshop's point of view any spot is a solid color with no ink beneath it; it cannot mix base spot colors in a proportion we set — such operations are done in other software.

![A spot-color channel built from a masked selection, verified in Acrobat Pro](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/Untitled-1.jpg)

**The blackest Pantone.** Any deep black is a mix of black with other colors, so casts are inevitable. A good choice in most cases is 433 C in two layers, with a deep anilox. And if the shop says varnish will darken the black, do not believe them — varnish always lightens dark colors.

**Marking up a Pantone as a separate channel in InDesign** (when printers ask for it): go to the Swatches panel, choose New Color Swatch from the burger menu, set Color Type to Spot and Color Mode to the needed Pantone library (usually PANTONE Solid Coated), pick the Pantone, click Add (repeat for more than one), then Done — and replace your Pantones with the ones added to the Swatches panel.

![InDesign: New Color Swatch in the Swatches panel menu](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/Untitled-1.png)

![InDesign: choosing a Spot color type and the PANTONE Solid Coated library](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/8654698789.png)

## File Preparation, PDF, and Imposition

**Preflighting a layout** before sending it: use Adobe Acrobat Pro, *Tools → Print Production → Output Preview*. Watch the "Separations" item, which lists the colors; if you find a color that should not be there, you can switch off the display of particular colors and see which elements to recolor, and the "Show" menu lets you check for RGB elements. You can also check for exceeding the total ink with the "Total Area Coverage" checkbox. In Illustrator you can quickly convert all colors via *Edit → Edit Colors → Convert to CMYK*, then in the Swatches palette click "Add Used Colors" and delete any stray Pantones that appear.

![Acrobat Pro Output Preview, showing Separations and Total Area Coverage](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Screenshot_1.png)

**Removing transparency from a PDF** (when the shop complains): use PDF Optimizer (older Acrobat) or Save As Optimized PDF (recent versions) and mind the Transparency Flattener settings — better still, re-export from the program where the PDF was laid out rather than re-saving the finished PDF. The old Export/PostScript route in Acrobat Reader used to be relevant, but lately one meets PDFs that Acrobat cannot export to PS at all. C'est la vie.

**Converting all fonts to curves in a large PDF:** in InDesign, draw a transparent rectangle on a new layer placed below everything, ungroup all objects so text in groups is not lost, then go to *Edit → Transparency Flattener Presets* and create a new preset (the shop may adjust these). Then *File → Export → Adobe PDF (print)* with General → Standard → none, Compatibility → Acrobat 4 (PDF 1.3), and Advanced → Transparency Flattener Preset set to the preset just created. You can plan transparent rectangles on a new layer in advance when building master pages. Create Outline also turns everything to curves, but risks distorting strokes, losing style tricks, and rendering complex vectors strangely. In Acrobat Reader, alternatively: add a watermark with opacity at 0% and any text, then *Advanced → Print Production → Flattener Preview*, tick "convert to curve," and apply — an archaic method.

![InDesign transparency-flattener preset settings for converting fonts to curves](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/Screen-Shot-2016-06-22-at-08.52.15.png)

**Saving PDF/A** (the archival standard, per GOST and ISO 19005:2005): PDF/A embeds fonts and strips hyperlinks and multimedia, so the document opens anywhere, anytime. There are subformats such as PDF/A-1a and PDF/A-1b; in Russia PDF/A-1a is requested more often. There are many nuances to account for when creating and saving, but they are easily fixed and edited. Saving is simple — *File → Save As → More Options → PDF/A*; recent Acrobat versions offer to save with archival support directly, and Preflight should verify the document and tick the conformance box.

**A PDF sent for rework, with no source and no fonts**, that turns to garbage in Illustrator: the simplest path is to save your PDF as PostScript from full Acrobat (not Reader); the resulting PS should open in Illustrator without font trouble (though not guaranteed). Or use PitStop to make most changes through the Acrobat interface, or convert everything to vector (see the fonts-to-curves method above), or in Illustrator use *Object → Flatten Transparency* for 100% vector plus outlined text.

**An EPS that will not open** (Illegal operand error — can't open file): usually it helps to create a new document and bring your file in with Place, then *Object → Flatten Transparency* at defaults and delete the clipping mask. Or run the ancient chain Acrobat → re-save as PDF → open in CorelDRAW → save as .ai.

**Imposition.** I do not recommend doing imposition in InDesign; there are dedicated Acrobat Pro plugins (Quite Imposing, Quark Imposer, etc.) and standalone programs for imposing from PDF (Signa, Preps, etc.) — most often I have worked with Preps. Still, in InDesign it is done via *File → Print Booklet* (InBooklet in CS/CS2); the print settings hide under Current Settings. The layout types: **2-up Perfect Bound** — a book, split into several signatures each with its own imposition, then bound (ideally a sewn binding, which is reliable and convenient; thick books are usually sewn, but it costs more); **Consecutive** — a concertina booklet, with spreads built from single pages accounting for the folds; **2-up Saddle Stitch** — for small editions folded and stapled after printing. Remember the page count must be a multiple of 4 (the staple), though multiples of 2 (thermal binding, spiral) and 16 also occur.

![InDesign Print Booklet imposition settings](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/print_setting.png)

To **un-impose** a book with no source (say 800 pages): if there were not too many pages, I would place it in InDesign and re-output page by page, with guides solving the exact positioning — but with 800 the first thing that comes to mind is PDF Toolbox, then Quite Imposing → Shuffle Pages (Quite Imposing can also cut, as I recall). Or in Acrobat: duplicate the file, *Document → Crop Pages* to keep only even/odd pages and extract page by page with *Document → Extract Pages* into separate folders; with Total Commander rename the even files page1/page2/page3 into 001/003/005 (using counter parameters for one batch) and 002/004/006 for the other; then *File → Combine → Merge Files into a Single PDF*. There is also a SplitPages.js script for Acrobat, dropped into the Acrobat JavaScript folder.

![PDF Toolbox, used to take apart an imposed PDF](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Screenshot_1.png)

For **packaging dieline dimensions**, ideally you do not set them in Illustrator at all — they are placed by a structural designer on the dieline in a specialized CAD such as Esko ArtiosCAD or Arden Software ImpactCAD (CorelDRAW also has a dedicated tool). If you would rather not leave Illustrator, there are plugins; I used [CADtools](https://www.hotdoor.com/cadtools) years ago, and there are surely others. Or place them by hand: stick + stick + stick = an arrow.

## Special Production Cases

**Bleeds.** When exporting a multipage booklet, it is better to keep bleeds on all sides, even where they overlap at the spine.

**Marks on packaging.** The colored squares you see are registration marks (printer's crosses); they help align the separated forms and control color. When a run starts, the first stage is makeready — a kind of press calibration to align the registration marks. More often on retail packaging you will meet color bars, used to check ink purity and density and how the inks mix (minimally, CMYK plus 50% overprints); there are also ink-density scales for monitoring dot gain and image brightness, and a radial "star" target that controls many parameters at once.

[![Print control marks](http://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/3d.jpg)](http://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/3d.jpg)

**Barcodes.** For EAN-13 I would not recommend inverting or recoloring — only phone-camera scanners will read an inversion; for UPC, inversion is acceptable. My experience is that any experiments with barcodes can spell trouble. Light colors (including red and orange) suit the spaces between bars; bars need dark colors — black, blue, green — and composite colors are unfit for barcodes. Even if a code scans on tests, ancient retail equipment far from large cities may not read it, and glossy surfaces and cellophane hurt readability too. Mind the color pairing: black on red *may* be fine, since lasers read with red light, but red on white will certainly not read.

![Barcode color combinations that are almost certainly readable](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/ok.gif)

![Barcode color combinations a scanner is guaranteed not to read](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/saila.gif)

**Flexo.** The key things are ink order and trapping. The most common ink orders are CMYK and YMCK; Pantones are trickier, but the gist is the same — making a flexo layout, know which ink will cover which and write it down for the printers.

**Glowing, very bright colors.** There are many ways to solve so vague a task. Pantones made from fluorescent flexo inks are prepared like ordinary Pantones, and much depends on the color combinations in the layout itself — their contrast and ratio.

**A saturated yet semitransparent color over a dark background** (say, red Pantone 032 C over a dark gray plaster texture). Semitransparent ink looks bright only on white paper; varnish play almost certainly will not fix it. But high (letterpress) printing such as Z-G UVALUX 032 C is bright and saturated in itself; look toward UV printing, where the same pigments look more saturated than in offset thanks to the thicker ink layer.

**A good color proof.** At minimum the shop needs un-ruined equipment, a RIP, and a spectrophotometer; at best, a color-control system calibrated against the Brunner scale, and Prinect Image Control. If you print with water-based inks, you can check for foam in the system — good shops add a defoamer.

**Non-standard paper format, saving cost.** Paper can be ordered or cut to any format; most offset machines are tuned for A2 (sheets of 620×940, 720×1040, 500×700, and a few mutants), and I would impose to that size. Printing on an A1 machine is cheaper, but not everyone has such equipment. For a book, the format in publishing is a fraction of a sheet — e.g. 70×100/16 means 1/16 of a 700×1000 mm sheet, i.e. 170×240 mm.

**A large convex plastic part.** I would look toward plastic forming (vacuum forming) — that is how plastic car parts and signage are made.

## Resetting Your Color Settings

If you got confused and knocked all your Photoshop settings astray, there is a simple way to put everything right. In Color Settings, just choose Europe Prepress 3 or Europe General Purpose 3 — the only difference is the RGB profile: sRGB for poor monitors (understanding which part of the CMYK gamut you lose), Adobe RGB for fine monitors with the matching gamut.
