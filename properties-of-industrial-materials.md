## What expensive design actually is

The industrial designer's core task is to help a client beat a rival in the market through the visual and functional uniqueness of the product being developed. Plenty of domestic companies make design no worse than their competitors and then rest on that — yet a significant part of a product's uniqueness is its design. Look at Apple, at Blizzard: they pour enormous sums into design and art and receive colossal profit from a grateful public in return. They make a confection — charismatic, beautiful, flawless — the kind of thing you want to pick up and hold. These companies understand that a high-quality visual presentation is a mandatory part of a successful product, and that excellent goods lead to higher profit. But design is not colour, a grid, and a pattern on the box. All those typographic and conceptual embellishments are more accurately called decoration. So what is real, expensive design? What sets it apart, and what are its signs?

Design is the sound ergonomics of products and services. Look at Apple again. When the iPhone appeared, the market changed — and it changed because excellent design came first. Apple gave the market nothing revolutionary; they simply won the war on the design front rather than the technological one. Apple's designers know exactly how to provoke the right emotional response, and that is the key concept of design. Emotions and impressions are what people value in other people, in places, in annual events. Giving a person the right emotions — not drawing a pattern, adding ambient lighting, or chasing pixel-perfect alignment — that is design. People love choosing things that look and feel great, things you can pick up and say: now *that's* an object. Only a designer who accounts for the tactile sensation, the weight, the sounds, the masking of technical imperfections, the evenness and tidiness of every small detail — everything that makes a product feel expensive — can earn that emotional response. It isn't about tonnes of creativity and new ideas, nor about clean, obedient adherence to guidelines, but about the skilful assembly of already-existing emotions and associations into your product.

## Cost, price, and value

Picture two pens: the first costs 80 ₽, the second 800,000 ₽. Does the second write ten thousand times better than the first? I doubt it. Yet a market for premium pens exists, because the demand exists. A product has a *cost* — what it takes to produce and market it. It has a *price* — the sum at which it is offered to the consumer. And it has a *value* — a measure of what the consumer believes it is worth. Value is precisely about emotion, about what that pen will tell the people around its owner. The functional component is identical; the designer simply played with materials. The cheap pen is acrylic; the expensive one is gold and enamel, and it will not be thrown away once it stops writing.

You can play in the opposite direction, too. Polystyrene wine glasses feel and sound entirely different from real glass — especially if the polystyrene was glued together from crumb and shavings dissolved in petrol. Glass can serve a camera lens or a beer bottle; it is the designer who gives the material its character. Even gold, which everyone associates with wealth, can sit inside a microchip, where it becomes a practical engineering application. Ceramics were used back in Ancient Greece, yet today they evoke kitchen stoves, high-pressure valves, and materials for extreme conditions.

## The senses: sound and smell

Perception is shaped even by the frequency of the sound an object makes when struck. Acoustic characteristics lend charm, by analogy with the smell of new things — and both are levers the designer can pull. Sounds and vibrations can also be deadened; that is damping. Bronze and glass ring fairly high when struck, which makes them suitable for bells. Rubber and polymers sound dull and vibrate at low frequencies, so they are used to quiet noise. Lead, metal though it is, also absorbs sound and is sometimes used in building cladding for that reason. In monolithic buildings assembled from reinforced concrete and foam blocks, noise is suppressed with solid barriers and distance. Foam-concrete partitions are fairly useless for soundproofing because they are not dense; for the same reason, cotton wool does nothing to block sound, and neither do egg cartons. Mineral wool is good for reflected noise, while classic thick reinforced concrete and brick are good against airborne noise — but not against impact noise.

Smell deserves the same attention, because it can be engineered into a product to raise its perceived value. The particular smell of an old house or the metro, the one that pulls childhood nostalgia out of you, is most likely creosote: railway ties were impregnated with it, and it also protects wood from rot. Historically the wooden ties were treated with tar, antiseptics, and oil as well, but the fungi win in the end, which is why ties are now mostly concrete — and why that smell is fading. A new car interior or a freshly finished apartment smells different precisely because the materials are different.

## What nature already knows

Begin with biological structures — that is, with nature's inventions. The first living organisms were very soft, because hard materials are more brittle and ill-suited to the needs of growth and adaptation. Nature uses almost no hard materials at all; only large creatures have skeletons and shells, and even then nature wraps the skeleton in a mass of soft tissue.

Nature has roughly 4.5 billion years of experience designing structures, while we humans, in our modest wisdom, try to make everything out of hard materials. It is not surprising — a lens must be fixed precisely in a microscope, brake pads must sit symmetrically, stone is easy to turn into a weapon for killing game, and so on. The designer's task is to overcome all this bulkiness wherever possible, and much of this chapter is about how different materials can deliver lightness and comfort instead.

## Why things resist load

When a material or structure resists a load, it can only do so through an equal and opposite reaction. If a five-storey building presses down on its foundation, the foundation presses back on the building with the same force. Force must be balanced; everything must be in balance. This is an extremely important rule, true of any structure.

Tie a rope to a stone and pull, and the stone resists. But how can an inanimate object resist? Under any force an object stretches or compresses — you know this from any animation course — and hard objects like stone deform too. The eye cannot see it, but it happens at the molecular level, where the strong bonds between atoms compress and stretch, creating the necessary resistance, and those bonds spring back once the force is removed. Displacement is always proportional to load. Rubber, an eraser, and fabric, of course, do not fully recover, because they are plastic. The magnitude of displacement depends on the size and shape of the structure and on the material itself.

When a drop of water hangs from an object, the weight of the water is balanced by surface-tension forces. The tension at a liquid's surface is as real as the tension in a string, but it differs in three ways. First, the force of surface tension does not depend on the magnitude of deformation; it stays constant no matter how much the surface area grows. Second, a liquid's surface can be expanded indefinitely, creating arbitrarily large deformations without rupture. Third, the surface-tension force across a cross-section of the liquid depends on the length of the contour at that section.

## How materials break

Buildings fail mainly from tension; compression matters far less, though part of a structure can slide out sideways under load. Brittle materials fail by shearing at roughly 45 degrees; in concrete this happens the moment the shear stress reaches its critical value. That is why glass and concrete shatter under a hammer — they cannot resist the load. Compress glass and damage is unlikely; pull it in tension and the chance of damage is high.

The energy picture explains the rest. Science recognises many kinds of energy — potential, thermal, chemical, electrical — and any action is the conversion of one kind into another. You cannot get something from nothing, and energy cannot be destroyed, so the total amount always stays the same. What happens to a bow if you loose it without an arrow — where does the energy go? Into cracking the bow. When you design anything, then, you must think about where elastic energy might convert into kinetic. Brittle materials — stone, brick, glass — should not be used where they are placed in tension, because it takes very little energy to break them. The energy required to fracture wrought iron or mild steel can be a million times greater than the energy needed to fracture the same cross-section of glass or ceramic, even though the static tensile strengths of these materials are not very different.

Tension is pulling, compression is squeezing, and shear is mutual sliding. All solids respond to shear stress the same way they respond to tension. Biological tissues and rubber can undergo far more elastic, reversible shear deformation than metal, concrete, or bone; for a liquid, shear deformation is unlimited and irreversible. Practically: if you want a box built from a lattice, the optimal arrangement is at 45 degrees to the beam's axis, otherwise there is not enough shear stiffness. A fine illustration comes from arch bridges — two parallel steel arches let you place the roadway at any level between them, and that freedom exists precisely because the compressed arches take the entire load.

## Glass

Glass has its own failure logic, and the industry has engineered around it. Tempered and reinforced glass both exist. Reinforced glass will not shatter into small shards because there is wire inside it. Tempered glass fails differently from ordinary glass: it either withstands the impact entirely or breaks into a multitude of safe little pieces.

In Russia, glass strength is codified by GOST. There is impact-resistant glass in protection classes A1, A2, and A3; impact-resistant and safety glass for construction (SM1–SM3, ST1–ST3), the glass doors a person might accidentally walk into; glass resistant to penetration or chopping in classes B1, B2, and B3; bullet-resistant glass in classes 1, 2, 2a, 3, 4, 5, 5a, 6, and 6a, usually multilayer tempered glass; blast-resistant glass J1–J7 and G1–G7; fire-resistant glass of many kinds; and frost-resistant façade glass that shows no loss of clarity down to −40 °C. Alongside all this, the modern world has float glass — cheap, practical, decent in its properties, and democratic in price.

For façade cladding there is also Stemalit, a glass-derived material that is easy to mistake for ordinary glass but is not. The glass is painted on one side, the paint is fused into it, and then the glass is thoroughly tempered until it is strong enough to clad a façade. Calling it glass is a stretch, since its transparency is close to zero. Do not confuse it with Lacobel, which is not strong.

## Wood

Wood fails in its own way: all the fibres compress at a 90-degree angle, and compression, as you would guess, works poorly. Here is nature's cleverness, though — in a tree the outer layers are usually in tension and the inner ones in compression, which doubles the trunk's resistance to bending. All cracks and folds begin at the points of maximum stress, around holes and defective inclusions; nails and screws do not weaken timber much if they sit tightly in it. From these simple but widespread materials you can build stunning Scandinavian-style interiors and objects — Apple, for instance, finishes its retail stores with only glass, wood, and stainless steel.

Wood has an important parameter: moisture conductivity. As timber is heated, moisture is distributed unevenly within it — it evaporates from the upper layers but remains inside. Moisture always moves along the temperature gradient toward the colder side, so raising the temperature of the wood raises its moisture conductivity.

For interiors, the wood family is wide. Solid floorboard, decking, and other sawn timber are the starting point. Solid board is a durable, expensive material that can be sanded back — simply a whole piece of wood, and very much in demand — but for an apartment I would choose glued semi-finished products, where the timber is laminated: cork, window blocks, parquet and engineered boards. Parquet boards are quite good; budget clients get Korona Parket, premium clients Amigo, or a parquet panel. Cork is porous, so it holds a lot of air and insulates heat beautifully; it is soft underfoot and easy to damage, and for clients I have used Corkstyle, Haro, and Egger. Veneer is thin slices for finishing furniture and doors; milled materials cover handrails, skirting, and everything else that comes off a router; piece parquet is small solid blocks, where I have used Marco Ferutti and Kamodoor on projects, while for myself and my parents I fitted budget Barlinek and the Kireyevsk factory. Worth mentioning separately are HPL panels — paper impregnated with resins — expensive but visually excellent, fireproof, and indifferent to moisture and temperature swings, with Gustafs and Formica among the makers. Tongue-and-groove boarding and plywood I won't even discuss; they are rough materials sold beside every dacha. MDF is worth a word: flammable, not for wet rooms. Chipboard (DSP) is coarse pressed shavings, cheaper than MDF with the same drawbacks plus the occasional phenol, mostly for budget furniture. OSB is a good choice — multilayer glued sheets of shavings, with OSB-3 the most popular and, in my experience, well-behaved; it is less strong than plywood but much cheaper, its downside being formaldehyde emission. As for the species themselves: pine is rot-resistant and strong; cedar is beautiful and light; fir and spruce are brittle; oak is very hard; ash is decent but yields to oak; walnut is fine; linden is ideal for carving; maple is acceptable; aspen is unsuitable for work.

## When the render meets reality

In my work I periodically run into beautiful interiors and buildings that can exist only in a picture. 3ds Max plus V-Ray is good; add Mental Ray and it becomes nearly divine. But as always, people educated at home or on short courses forget the most important thing: a project must be buildable. The sections that follow walk through the kinds of shortcomings I have hit over the years.

## Principles of the building envelope

Start with apartment planning and entrance groups, and with the **dew point** — the temperature at which dew forms in or on objects, around 6–8 °C. If the dew point forms inside a wall, the material's longevity drops. To prevent this, you either add a vapour barrier (reducing the vapour flow through the wall to zero) or insulate the wall, the key being that the dew point lands outside the load-bearing structure. The mechanism: during the heating season, some vaporous moisture leaves the room through the walls, but on its way through it inevitably meets the cold outside air and condenses into liquid, and that water then directly affects the wall's thermal physics. So in summer the wall's heat-retaining properties recover, in winter they vanish, and so it goes year-round. Concretely: if it is +24 °C indoors and −30 °C outside, the temperature about halfway through the wall will be around six degrees, marking the dew-point boundary; if the external insulation is not vapour-permeable, condensation accumulates at the boundary between insulation and wall, with unpleasant consequences. But with a thicker layer of vapour-permeable material the dew point shifts into the insulation, the wall stays warm and dry, and you profit. The takeaway for interior design is not to clad internal walls with vapour-permeable materials — or simply build the walls a bit thicker and skip the insulation altogether.

**Thermal insulation.** For a fast, budget option, insulate from outside (so the dew points do not migrate through the house); if you must insulate from inside, use moisture-resistant gypsum board (GKLV) panels and walls, foam every gap and corner, and insulate the floor and ceiling first. Heat leaves a house through the enclosing structures (walls and slabs), through the windows, and with ventilated air, so those are the points to watch. Heating in general is something many contemporary interior designers never think about, after which the residents end up solving the problem at extra cost and the builders end up inventing perverse layered "pie" constructions. At minimum, remember that a room is heated by warm air and that heat transfer arises from convection, conduction, radiation, and the diffusion of water vapour. Cast-in-place reinforced concrete usually stacks into this sandwich: a reinforced-concrete wall, then insulation, then a finishing protective-decorative outer layer of brick or tile. For insulation itself the materials are mineral wool, expanded polystyrene for non-residential structures, basalt wool, and Penoplex.

**Vapour barrier.** This is really just ordinary polyethylene plus an anti-fungal treatment. Do not stuff a vapour barrier between the wall and the insulation, and the barrier must be fully sealed. Some types are air-tight yet pass water vapour, so they are not a full waterproofing layer. The barrier is installed with at least a 3 cm gap between the plasterboard and the membrane, snug against the insulation. In a dwelling, wet zones need a waterproofed ceiling — balconies, kitchens, toilets, bathrooms — and so does an apartment on the top floor of a block, just as in private houses, because the roof can leak and the slab can swell with moisture. Your upstairs neighbour might even carry out an illegal redesign and put a kitchen above your bedroom. Waterproofing can be coating-type or membrane-type; manufacturers include Knauf and Ceresit, with Rockfon for panels, and Knauf Aquapanel is also reasonably moisture-resistant.

**Air conditioners** are cheaper than a heat pump plus the work of laying an external loop: less excavation, easier repair since the unit sits outside rather than underground, no soil-freezing "lunar landscapes," fewer technical fluids such as antifreeze or brine, and nearly all models are factory-made. There is a dependence on intake air, but in practice only below −15 °C, and energy use is fairly modest at 3–4 kW in winter. The effect of a constant draught and not-quite-fresh air can, however, cancel out the advantages.

**Windows.** If the interior designer has laid a rough-stone texture on a wall, the rest of the project should lean on smaller windows; brick houses, too, are better kept to modest window sizes, though it always depends on the case. In timber buildings and modern steel frames the window opening sits between the studs or columns. Sill height follows the room's purpose: with a beautiful valley beyond the glass you can drop the sill to 50 cm from the floor; for an ordinary view it moves to 75 cm; in a workspace, from 1 m; in a kitchen, 125 cm; in dressing rooms and the like, human height, 1.5–1.75 m. And a normal storey height for school-type buildings is 3.84 m — when designing such places, always keep ventilation in mind, because people in a closed room consume oxygen and emit carbon dioxide, and a shortage of oxygen leads to headaches, provokes illness, reduces capacity for work, induces nausea, and so on. Always think about how carbon dioxide, steam, and moisture will be removed and how airflow into the space will be ensured; windows are far from the least of it here.

It also helps to understand the window profile — the set of PVC profiles needed to make windows, doors, and other transparent elements. Thickness comes in 70, 82, 90, and 104 mm, chambers run from two up to a great many, and sealing contours from one to three. Unlike the glazing units, window profiles do not fog up (with exceptions); to fight fogging, lower the indoor humidity or raise the temperature of the glazing unit itself. For Moscow, three to four chambers at 70 mm is the norm. A sill can be any material, but because plants and therefore moisture usually sit on it, the most popular choice is white plastic; you can also use solid lacquered wood, natural stone (not marble — granite is fine), concrete, tile, or metal. And to dispel a myth: when you order a plastic window you pay per square metre. PVC windows satisfy most people perfectly well — warm and quiet — and for the makers there is no such thing as a standard size; everything is calculated by the square metre.

**Wooden windows** have obvious drawbacks: expensive, prone to rot, "breathing" (they won't close, then gaps appear; they shrink in winter and swell in summer), and they need painting. But to sum up, wooden windows perform well on ventilation, air-conditioning, and heating norms when fitted with modern glazing units, and they offer far more freedom to play with the window's construction than PVC does. Wood, as a living interior material, has good acoustic properties thanks to its structure. I have sometimes used **plywood** as a wall finish — it absorbs sound well, is pleasant to the touch, and has low thermal conductivity; its main drawback is the anisotropy of its structure, where the grain dictates the material's density. Wood is loved by insects, fungi, and rot, and it burns well — but nearly all its faults are offset by impregnation.

## Floors

**Laminate**, in my experience, is not the best material. It is a cheap polymer imitation — and cheap is sometimes the point — with any pattern you like, cheaper and stronger than parquet board. Into my projects I add only industrial laminate, classes 31 through 34. For underfloor heating, prefer ceramic tile or real stone for the covering because their thermal conductivity is higher, though laminate also gets along with underfloor heating (class 34, say) while disliking sharp temperature swings; apartments often get class 31, since 34 is usually too expensive. For open verandas, larch works and is cheap. I always offer clients the underfloor-heating-plus-porcelain-stoneware combination from the start. Tile, poured polyurethane floors, and even linoleum are considered good; a poured floor goes onto a perfect screed, and bevel-edged laminate looks fine.

**Parquet board** is very poor despite being natural wood: hard to sand back under a lacquer finish and easily spoiled — better to take engineered board. **Engineered board** is the optimal apartment floor: a thick layer of wood, expensive, glued onto a poured floor. It beats solid board, carrying a useful wear layer plus plywood, and it can sometimes go straight onto a perfectly level screed, though that depends on the foreman's skill. It is fussy about installation, but it is warm, natural, and durable, and if it is oiled it can be restored with special crayons and oil; you cannot, however, run underfloor heating beneath it.

Then there is porcelain stoneware. Red *gres* is porcelain stoneware on red clay, an unglazed tile; *porcellanato gres* is on white clay. It will dress not only floors but façades, and it is in fact the most popular material going. Pressing plus firing at 1,200 °C yields a result close to granite; it can convincingly imitate stone, wood, metal, and much else, in sizes up to 4 m at 4 mm thick. For myself I fit Archskin; for clients, FMG and LaFabbrica. Surface prep for porcelain stoneware is screed or plywood — I personally love plywood and often use it in interiors as a substrate under wood, and sometimes resort to finish-grade plywood. There are different grades: the most popular, FK, is eco-friendly and safe but afraid of moisture, being simply wood; FSF plywood is unafraid of moisture but emits formaldehyde, so it cannot be used in dwellings (though the law doesn't forbid it). For supply, I buy from the Murmansk plywood factory and from Sveza. You will also need grout — epoxy is better — and tile adhesive; the stoneware itself is hard to cut and lay, and the fitters dislike it. Floor (technical) stoneware starts at 10 mm, wall stoneware is thinner, and floor stoneware wants underfloor heating because the material is cold.

**Linoleum** comes in natural and PVC forms. For natural, the makers are Forbo, Tarkett, and Armstrong; PVC linoleum we all know from standard apartments — it tolerates frost and direct sun poorly and it smells, and it is too cheap a choice to recommend to anyone. A good alternative is **quartz vinyl**: it does not burn, absorb moisture, or conduct electricity, and it is a very practical material for public spaces at minimal thickness; its one drawback is that its imitation of natural materials yields to porcelain stoneware, with no bevels. Budget is still Tarkett; for clients, Vinilam. **Terrazzo** is by now an established material, cement with marble chips — true terrazzo is a poured floor, or porcelain stoneware with a terrazzo print as the budget version. Another alternative to linoleum is **marmoleum**, suitable for play areas and children's rooms: natural, easy to lay, single-colour. Or carpet, which wears fast and is easily replaced.

The floor "pie" carries the strictest requirements, particularly sound, moisture, and heat insulation, and the wear layer must have minimal abrasion — at the Ploshchad Revolyutsii metro station in Moscow, the bronze dog's nose is already worn smooth from constant stroking. Eco-friendliness of the whole assembly matters too, especially the absence of formaldehyde and of expanded polystyrene, which releases toxic poison when it burns. Any floor has a base in the form of a load-bearing slab, usually primed; then the rough floor, which runs underlay → waterproofing → sound/heat insulation → screed (the levelling layer); then the finish: preparation for the finishing layer (underlay, adhesive) → the finishing layer → an optional top coat. The base is usually a monolithic or precast reinforced-concrete slab, though dachas and offices vary — Stalin-era buildings have timber floors, and single-storey village banyas may have no slab at all; reinforced concrete is primed, while timber beams are treated against mould and fungi.

The **rough floor** begins with an underlay that distributes load, especially over soil, made of concrete or simply compacted gravel or expanded clay. Then waterproofing, which may be roll, coating, or fill type. Roll waterproofing is a sealed overlapping membrane joined with a heat gun, torch, or tape — the fastest option over large areas, and I usually bought Technonicol. Coating waterproofing is essentially a mastic: high quality, easy to wrap around complex shapes, but time-consuming. It is common to combine roll plus coating for a comfortable result, with liquid waterproofing as the fast option using spray equipment, good for sealing small cracks; makers include ICOPAL, Knauf, Ceresit, and Redington — though rough materials depend far less on the manufacturer than finishing ones do.

**Soundproofing** must defend against airborne and impact noise, with a third kind, structural noise, that carries the sound of drilling a load-bearing wall to a dozen floors of the building. The core principle is to alternate hard materials (foam block, brick) with soft ones (mineral wool, for both heat and sound). The hard materials are there by default — reinforced concrete — so it is the soft ones you add. Soft soundproofing comes in board and roll forms: Rockwool makes a great deal of mineral board, as does Technonicol, while for rolls I bought Shumanet and Stopzvuk — thin materials that damp impact noise and require a floating floor beneath them. Crucially, you must isolate the entire room around its perimeter rather than a single wall, and if you do isolate by planes, use only floating schemes.

More important still is the **screed**, a generous levelling of the base for the finishing layer and a place to run the floor's engineering systems. A solid screed is simply the floor's thickness, sold as a dry mix in a bag to be combined with water — cement plus sand plus something more — often reinforced with steel mesh and topped with a liquid self-levelling layer for a perfect finish. Knauf recommends starting with the solid screed, then tape, backing paper, and self-levelling screed, the whole thing taking up to a month to dry; the self-levelling screed produces a flat surface by gravity alone, and if you pour it straight onto a reinforced-concrete slab you will be paying for the downstairs neighbours' repairs, since waterproofing was skipped. It can even serve as a finish with a little paint over it. Makers here are Ceresit and Knauf Tribon for good thickness. There is also semi-dry screed — simply less water in the mix — which dries faster than solid screed but is harder to execute. I usually recommend a dry, prefabricated screed, typically expanded clay: easy to lay, a dry process with nothing to cure, and it both retains heat and blocks sound. It is worth browsing the manufacturers' technical-solution albums — rockfon.ru, albes.ru, gyproc.ru, knaufarmstrong.com, knauf.ru, tn.ru, rockwool.com, acoustic.ru.

## Ceramics and tile

Clay materials — ceramics — withstand high temperatures well. Red brick is clay; its pluses are strength, eco-friendliness, any design you like, and moisture resistance, though the material is cold. The Kremlin is made of it. But brick is a fairly expensive material. The Italians adore ceramics, and there are five base types of ceramic tile worth distinguishing. Matte is the toughest and roughest, since it is not processed after the kiln. Polished is glossy, beautiful, and vivid but brittle. Lappato sits between the two. Satin has a soft sheen from mineral salts. Structured imitates wood, concrete, and other pressing results and is mostly applied to porcelain stoneware. Ceramic tile can be recommended to clients in warm countries even for floors, and for kitchens, hallways, bathrooms, and toilets it suits nearly every client, paired with underfloor heating. Ornaments can be laser-cut from it.

## Polymers

You can play with materials. Potters use clay and glaze, carpenters work timber, glassmakers craft beautiful objects from the unique qualities of their material. On the other side stand the polymers, which cover an enormous demand for cheap, practical materials and imitate almost any surface. Polymers are not ideal, though they are good: they can be transparent and are generally reliable, but they are highly flammable, they age and lose their properties (without decomposing), they are broadly toxic at high temperatures, and their imitation of other materials is not as good as it ought to be. Laminate is the example — not wood but polymer: easy to install like a construction set with no wet work, cheap, but with high hydrophobicity in the wrong sense, since spill water on it and it swells. Budget is Tarkett, mid-range Quick Step, premium Kronotex. Decorative films are polymers too, made of PVC, polyester, and polypropylene — a very budget option; the facing films that wrap furniture are applied only at the factory on the cheapest furniture and look more or less acceptable. You can choose such a film yourself and ask for it to be applied to the furniture you order for a client: chipboard furniture plus a facing film is a decent result.

A note on gluing polymers, especially for high-temperature use where you want a permanent bond. Chemically, a permanent bond is simply the cross-linking of the adhesive's molecules with each other and with the surface, so the objects cannot be separated without damage. The familiar example is epoxy resin — the closer it is in composition to the surface material, the better the bond. With polymers you can contrive something for high temperatures; the real problem is the brittleness of the resin. Adhesion can shift, and polymer adhesives can resolve the question.

## Hard and soft, and a word on insulation

Materials divide into hard and soft. Hard materials polish well, resist wear, and last; steel is hard, glass is hard, diamond is harder still. Hard materials are not easily scratched, but they can scratch other things. Opposite them sit the soft materials — elastomers, polymer foams. The importance of choosing the right material is measured not only by appearance and tactility but by very applied concerns: cast-iron radiators reduce "noise exchange" between apartments, low-maintenance structures like the bridges of the Russian Far East demand steel with high corrosion resistance such as 14KhGNDTs, and even a fire hook is made of steel.

**Arbolite** (wood concrete) is a coarse-pored material with mediocre thermal resistance (40 cm gives 1.82 m²·°C/W), and with heat being expensive and getting more so, that figure is worth attention. Arbolite feels cold to the touch, drawing heat from the finger quickly — a function not only of technical thermal conductivity but of specific thermal conductivity, which also depends on the material's properties. Otherwise it is a decent material; reasonably strong houses with 30–40 cm walls can be built from it. But for an energy-efficient house with heat losses down at even 20 W/m²·°C, you are into brick or concrete plus XPS insulation plus something else warm — though that is debatable, and alternatives such as Durisol and Brizolit exist. The most important conclusion from the start of this note: if you build from arbolite, plaster everything thoroughly so all the gaps disappear, which improves the insulation.

Thermal insulation is a hard topic in its own right. Brick by itself is not great against heat loss; a concrete panel with insulation is better, as are expanded-clay-concrete blocks, working on the same principle as the expanded clay laid in the bottom of flowerpots to absorb excess moisture and give it back as needed. A cheap alternative is crushed brick.

## Concrete

The concrete look has become fashionable, mainly for monolithic seamless floors. Concrete is cold but strong, works only with colour pigments mixed in and a coating on top, and can take an orange-peel texture. Nothing is cheaper — a poured concrete floor in its cheapest form costs less than PVC linoleum. The maker is Knauf, though you can find cheaper at the same quality. There is an ultra-strong version, LiTraCon, expensive and tough. Concrete is subject to corrosion because it is a porous material. Concrete sleepers, unlike timber ones, are not prone to sleeper or house fungi, though timber springs better and is easier to replace. To impress a client, run a slump test on site, then carefully ask the workers to add a little more water if the sand grains are round — remembering that more water yields less reliable concrete, partly offset by grain shape, which should be sharp.

Most industrial buildings are made of reinforced concrete, either assembled from reinforced-concrete blocks or formed from monolithic reinforced concrete with formwork. The formwork is usually not single-use; during the construction of the Ostankino tower it was raised level by level.

## Walls: panels, drywall, plaster, and finishes

**PVC panels** I mention only to say: stop using them. A hollow material with a printed texture, unafraid of water and fine for a dacha — but from a design standpoint it looks dreadful, with gaps visible at the joints. For a site cabin it will do.

**Drywall** is good for levelling crooked walls — and that is all — but for interior designers that is not very relevant knowledge, since plaster can compensate a 2–3 cm deviation just as well, the initial plaster layer working out to about 2 cm. People simply dislike a wall made of cardboard, and if builders insist hard on drywall, that is grounds to question their qualifications, though levelling a ceiling with it is sometimes acceptable. That said, if you do build partitions from drywall they will last a good while, and it is the most popular partition type. Drywall partitions are not very strong compared with masonry materials — better not to hang a television on drywall — so you prepare embedded parts for the bracket in advance; three layers of drywall can hold a picture and no more. And don't forget to run reinforcing tape over the joints.

**Plaster**, to my mind, beats drywall and works out far cheaper. It does have to dry, of course, but waiting a week to save money is no problem. Plaster is not only a levelling material but a decorative one. I am no fan of mineral-binder plasters, which need replacing after 15–20 years, and acrylic-based plaster is worse still — but silicone plasters are very good despite the price.

Choosing wall finishes and paint means knowing a few basic terms a client will ask about. Hiding power is how many coats are needed to cover a surface fully. Drying time is self-explanatory. Adhesion is how strongly the paint sticks; adhesive primers are usually applied for better grip. Consistency is viscosity — if the paint is too thin it runs down the walls. Thixotropy is when paint left undisturbed thickens. Eco-friendliness is harmlessness: interiors use only eco-friendly paints, while exteriors use the non-eco kind, which is more resistant to the elements. For makers I would take Caparol as the budget option, Dulux or Tikkurila for the mid segment, and Benjamin Moore for premium projects. For wallpaper, the budget names are Avangard, Palitra, and the Moscow Wallpaper Factory; mid-price, Marburg, Erismann, and Rasch; premium, Arte, KHROMA, Decori Decori, Loymina, and Emiliana Parati. As a rule look at fleece-backed wallpaper, or fibreglass wallpaper, which protects the walls a little but is slightly painful to hang and pricier.

There is no single "best and cheapest" wall finish — cheapest at what price, best for which room? The universal answer is ordinary water-based paint: it can be smooth, matte, or textured, any shade, applied in layers, with moisture-resistant versions for the bathroom. The material is cheap, but that is offset by the cost of surface prep: three coats of putty plus fibreglass mesh plus a finishing coat, not forgetting primer and rollers or brushes. The second universal answer is wallpaper as an accent material — fleece-backed is universal, vinyl is more durable and easy to hang, and for lovers of relief there is **lincrusta**. When you tire of choosing endless wallpaper, lincrusta is worth a look: a deeply embossed relief pattern on a dense paper base, which plus good lighting equals a client's love — though it is not cheap and the choice of design is limited to the Victorian style. My own favourites — porcelain stoneware, tile, and mosaic — work for wet rooms with epoxy grout for the kitchen; if you want mosaic, buy it ready-mounted on mesh, and above all never use glossy tile on a floor. Decorative plaster imitates anything at all but is demanding of application quality, needing mid-grade wall puttying and coming in at a democratic price. Joints can be hidden and walls dressed with moulding and stucco — cheap polyurethane or expensive gypsum, the gypsum made to order, heavy, and needing screws; a cheap route is to paint the walls and add mouldings over them, with polyurethane mouldings cheaper, smaller, and glued on, while ceiling roses fix to plaster rather than putty, off which they would fall. One more relatively cheap option is wall panels — increasingly rare, plastic, though gypsum 3D panels exist, as do MDF panels with inserts; built-in furniture, though, should be of a very hard material, and MDF is just pressed shavings with glue.

For a budget renovation specifically, sensible picks are MDF panels on the ceiling of small rooms (better still a slatted duralumin ceiling), polypropylene pipes, UNIS tile adhesive, Keracolor grout, WAGO 2X terminals, Legrand sockets, Knauf gypsum plaster, Weber Vetonit putty, and non-sectional Rifar SUPReMO radiators. When a bare-material finish is wanted — the natural texture of concrete or wood — it must still be treated: for concrete, Dufa, Ceresit, Sika, Neomid; for wood, Axton, Dufa, Belinka.

## Rubber

Rubber is very elastic, springy, and strong, used for everything from electrical insulation to industrial production such as waterproofing membranes. It is made from natural rubber, obtained from the milky sap of rubber-bearing plants, and also from gutta-percha; in the twenty-first century mostly synthetic rubber is used. There is also liquid rubber, a harmless and barely-flammable material. Rubber takes paint and comes in interesting colours to begin with — just don't smear it with petroleum jelly, which spoils it. If you ever get the chance to visit a natural-latex production site and verify the material's quality, take it.

## Natural stone

We have talked a great deal about simulating stone — but what if you splash out on the real, natural thing? For cladding it is perfectly fine and sells very well in the business segment, where there is money to hire qualified fitters; you can also make a basin from stone. It is durable, eco-friendly, heavy, unique in texture, and restorable, but cold, prone to breaking, and a weak sound insulator. Granite, quartzite, and basalt have high acid resistance. Backlit onyx for a reception desk is just the thing, but onyx is not wear-resistant, unlike granite and slate. Slab sizes are a separate headache when planning delivery.

That said, take marble and granite — seemingly good, strong materials — and then look at artificial quartz stone, which is 90 % natural quartz plus polyester resin and colour pigments. It comes out stronger than marble or granite and is excellent for a countertop, and for exteriors too. Or artificial acrylic stone, Corian, which has better characteristics than natural stone despite being a polymer: it can be scratched, but it is repairable.

Among the most popular natural stones is **marble**, a material that loves plenty of space and open room around it. It soaks up anything you spill if not cleaned at once, so litho-ceramic is often used instead — 3 mm of marble over a ceramic core. Marble is a material for monuments; many Moscow metro stations are finished in the "Koelga" grade and in the grey-blue "Ufaley" marble, with granite slabs for the floors. **Quartzite** is similar to marble in characteristics, sharing its texture but costing more, with low acid resistance like limestone and travertine. That is precisely why city spaces are clad in granite rather than marble: try to scrub a swear word off granite with acid and the granite survives where marble would not. **Granite** makes up most of the metro because of its fine-grained texture and excellent wear resistance. Useful references here are the builders' calculators at vladirom.narod.ru and the climatology tables at rosteplo.ru.

## Documentation and BIM

An enormous number of problems befall young specialists who lean too heavily on authorial supervision. Yet it is enough to ask a person what building information modelling is. If they answer something like "from a good, large model of the building you derive sections, drawings, and façades," they probably understand the profession. Floor explications, window and door families, axonometric schemes, the finishing passport, and the rest of the working documentation should likewise be no empty phrase to them.

## Form follows function

With all of the above in mind, remember that a product's form should be dictated by its function, because in most cases design is canonised and arose through the historical development of the object. Design is often modified, but not fundamentally — a chair must remain a chair. If it is a public chair, the base is almost always metal. The wheels of lunar rovers are likewise metal.

## Metals

We have talked a lot about simulating and using metals; now the metal itself. **Metals** are ferrous or non-ferrous. The ferrous ones are cast iron and steel, being carbon-and-iron alloys; the non-ferrous ones are copper, bronze, gold, brass, and so on. Non-ferrous metals are usually dearer than steel and cast iron but offer good thermal and electrical conductivity and interesting aesthetic properties.

**Aluminium** is a very popular metal for decorative objects: it does not fear corrosion and is used nearly everywhere. Light in weight, it suits house façades and slatted ceilings. In its pure form it is quite brittle, so houses use alloys such as Avial and Duralumin; for colour it is either sprayed or anodised. It is used even in space, though titanium is preferable, and in a corrosive environment pure aluminium spoils very quickly — polyurethane spraying rescues many situations, as does a fill of rigid integral foam. Even for household cookware aluminium is best avoided in favour of copper, titanium, glass, or ceramic, the key being that it not be magnetic; aluminium is light and convenient, but it behaves none too cleanly when heated.

Any iron furniture — safes, workbenches, racks, tool cabinets, general-ward hospital beds, postboxes, street bins — rarely needs much decoration. Ordinary powder coatings over steel in blue (RAL 5005), light grey (RAL 7035), grey (RAL 7046), dark grey (RAL 7021), and white (RAL 9016/9010) cover the basics; for more creative tasks the site ralcolorchart.com helps. Or galvanised steel on its own, uncoated. A slightly more advanced level is a shade of green or blue with a hammered-enamel effect. Safes can show off an external colour No. 11-A, enamel, or structured graphite, while medical cabinets must be hygienically safe with a corrosion-resistant powder coat.

**Bronze** is made of copper and tin: it does not rust, resists any influence, and is used in machine-building — the third-place cup at any competition is bronze. **Brass** is an alloy of copper and zinc, not much troubled by corrosion and popular in plumbing fittings and hardware: lamps, mixers, engineering equipment, handles, plates. It resembles gold but is cheaper; a current shunt, for instance, is made of brass plus a tin-zinc coating. **Invar** is a nickel-iron alloy with a low coefficient of thermal expansion, which is why it is often used for space; only Steel 20 can compete with it. In its classic form Invar can deform, so it suits only small, flat parts.

The quality of metal tools follows from the steel. A pair of cutters made from poor steel cannot bite through a harder material — obviously you cannot make a part from steel harder than the machine that made it. Tools use tool steel, which is baked, and it comes in grades for static and for impact loads: spanners are made for static loads, axes for impact, and for myself I keep adjustable pipe wrenches of high-strength cast iron. Even a simple kitchen knife can be made from 58 HRC or 59–62 HRC and be genuinely excellent, if dear; if you would rather not overpay, the budget 440C (95Kh18) makes sense.

Steel is usually hardened to raise its strength and hardness, though this makes the material more brittle. It is heated to 740–850 °C and cooled rapidly to 400–450 °C, which produces the temper colours:

Fighting rust is a recurring chore. I would rather not advertise a specific brand, but the HI-Gear aerosol works well and needs no neutralising with soda or rinsing; for truly bad cases, N-methylpyrrolidone; Rust-stop for internal parts, after which you cannot paint. For rustic experiments there is the classic recipe — 10 ml of ethylene glycol, 15 g of sodium nitrite, and urotropine per litre of a 10 % solution of lanolin in petrol or in used sunflower oil after prolonged heating, the kind you can get at any chebureki stand — and for aluminium surfaces you can wet foil with water or vinegar and rub the rust away. Industry uses dry ice, lasers, and high-pressure hand washing.

Fasteners deserve a word, since the right choice depends entirely on the required service qualities: hardware for an apartment, a shopping centre, fibre optics, a car, and a spacecraft are plainly different. PEINER bolts are on average better than GEM-YEAR, but if delayed brittle fracture is no problem in an apartment, GEM-YEAR will do. For pipes there is friction stir welding; for fasteners, adhesive joints are gaining popularity, as are chemical anchors and weld studs — though none of this belongs in electronics repair. For gypsum board, take Taiwanese screws, since the domestic ones sin on geometry. Any metal-to-metal joint of differing densities should be reinforced with screws plus blue threadlocker for DIY; lock washers and rings are also better Taiwanese if you cannot judge quality in the shop, and if they are galvanised, think about aesthetics in advance, because no coating will take on them — only chrome plating, which goes green over time. In wet environments use fasteners with protective coatings or stainless steel, and keep galvanic corrosion in mind; a good brand is Fischer. Optical anchor clamps will hold fibre on poles, or steel clamps with turnbuckles. And if you intend to launch something into space, that is already the domain of VIAM.

## Functionality and plumbing

Trade precedes production, and it is easier for production to make something familiar. A mug is round not only because it is convenient but because it is simpler to make round on a potter's wheel or in a master mould, and heating and softening a PET preform will in any case dictate the form. It is fitting to mention the much-popularised flat design, which is not merely another trend but something close to the final stage of design authenticity — a renunciation of everything superfluous. By the same logic you can use heat from data centres to warm homes, or cold ambient air to cool data centres; there are nuances with seasonal humidity, solved by filters, but conceptually it works exactly like that.

An interior designer, for instance, must understand which pipes are used for which cases and the schematic layouts of the runs; an industrial designer must understand this to design proper plumbing fixtures. Both roles must know the schematics, especially for the sewer. Everything that runs from the riser into the apartment is our responsibility as designers, where we can set a certain aesthetic; the rest is the property of the utilities and the building-management company, as are the shut-off valves and the pipes up to the meters. Our role is to anticipate and design the inspection hatches, since access to the meters is needed.

A water-supply system is a key life-support system, and without correct design and proper installation it will not work. The criteria to watch: keep the regulation slope and the right pipe diameter for the free movement of effluent; the riser must be larger in diameter than the branch pipes; connect fixtures by the shortest path with the fewest joints — a rule that is a death sentence for half of all "creative" design solutions; run pipes without turns, or at least not at acute angles; place the toilet and the riser not merely near each other but as close as possible; and keep the drain point above the point where the pipe meets the sewer. Two popular systems exist. The **tee scheme** runs two pipes from the riser, hot and cold, and uses tees to branch off to every point; nearly all Soviet houses are built this way, its virtue being simplicity, its problem being that water pressure depends on the pipe run and the number of taps open at once, so pressure does not distribute, it drops. The **radial (manifold) system** has a single inlet and several outlets, one per consumption point; its advantages are that you can shut off a single water outlet in an emergency and that pressure is always equal, but it is harder to design and needs more pipe, which raises the cost.

## Pipe materials

The material of the pipes matters greatly. The first and most interesting to us is **copper** — elite piping: expensive, beautiful, and not necessarily hidden in the walls, so it can be part of the interior, and there are copper roofing tiles too. Copper pipes are safe for health, and working in copper is usually the dearest and hardest job, one not every fitter will take on; copper is hard to cut, can crumple when heated or cooled, and greens and oxidises. It is also the basis for many alloys, for electrics, for household objects, and for pipes carrying liquids and gases. Because of the price, clients often prefer polypropylene over copper; for a boiler room copper is an excellent choice, but for an entire heating system it is rather irrational on a mid-range budget, and with Moscow water even copper pipes rust, though their service life reaches 70 years. Copper-nickel alloys, incidentally, are used in minting coins, a material called Monel.

**Cast-iron** pipes are popular for sewerage in older residential buildings and turn up in industrial premises and private houses; they suit external sewerage because they withstand heavy load, especially soil pressure. Cast iron as a material is dense and reliable, used for household and decorative objects as much as for engineering equipment — the vivid example being the cast-iron radiators of standard Soviet apartments. It is cheaper than steel and lets you cast more complex shapes such as manhole covers or storm grates; many car parts are cast iron, and high-strength cast iron is used even for machine tools and car engines. In exteriors you find cast-iron lighting columns and spiral staircases. High-strength cast iron (VCh-50) you meet constantly around the city, and the whole range from 10 to 100 appears in shut-off valves, balusters, sculptures, lighting supports, brake discs, valve bodies, and even floor tile — not for an apartment, naturally.

For external **panels** on houses, fibre-cement ones are excellent and easy to cut, and concrete tile imitating brick is also a good, cheap material, clearly better than cheap gypsum tile. On roofing, slate (the asbestos-cement kind) is worth a fair word despite Europe avoiding it: Europe's slate was made from actinolite and is toxic, whereas the USSR used serpentite. The material keeps evolving with new shapes and colours; it is very cheap, easy to lay, and good for 30–40 years without significant loads — far more with good laying, moss and mould removed, and loads minimised. It is in no way a designer material, but on a small budget with no claim to status it is clearly better than metal tile, and clay tile costs only a little more.

**Steel** pipes were once popular but are now barely used. They are the cheapest, withstand up to 100 °C, corrode easily even with a zinc coating, and inevitably fur up inside; chromium alloying is possible but a question of money. Steel is an iron-carbon alloy — every materials-science course opens with the iron-carbon diagram — and it is used almost everywhere, the metal being quite aesthetic in itself. Ordinary steel rusts, so taps and basins use stainless steel (AISI 304, with a large amount of chromium and nickel); also used are brass (copper plus zinc), bronze, copper, and silumin (aluminium plus silicon). A pipe subtype is structural steel with niobium: ship hulls, huge-diameter pipes, jet engines, and nuclear reactors are structural steel. Galvanic-series alloys are also used for greater corrosion resistance — even mild steel is quite corrosion-resistant. One would like to make everything from stainless steel, but that is too expensive, so it is cheaper to protect ordinary steel. In the simplest case that is paint over the metal, a good but not universal solution — it cannot be applied to the rebar inside concrete slabs, for instance. Another option is a zinc coating, used on cables, ladders, handrails, rebar, and screws for outdoor use, though in a cold or very humid climate its life is still too short. The second popular solution is cathodic protection. Corrosion is an electrochemical process, the electron flying from anode (+) to cathode (−); the anode can be a piece of magnesium (which won't last long, rotting away itself), aluminium, or zinc, which in my case gave the best results. On large objects an impressed-current cathodic system is often installed — a negative potential. Since so many objects are made of aluminium, small boats for example, the value of a zinc anode is hard to overstate, water heaters included, where the magnesium anode should be changed roughly every two years.

Pipes can also be **polyethylene**, usually low-pressure pipe with good temperature resistance (up to 400 °C), precisely the kind used to carry water from a well into a private house; on the Russian-language market there is a direct association — cross-linked polyethylene means Rehau, relatively dear and good. Polymer pipes are very light, resist aggressive external conditions, and last some fifty years. **Polybutene-1** is a separate material used only for transporting food liquids such as wine and milk: such pipes have the highest hygiene properties and, naturally, a high price. **Polypropylene** is the best option, which is why it is last in the list — a fibreglass-reinforced, high-strength pipe good for both heating and water supply, holding up to 95 °C with easy installation; its drawback is that it tolerates no physical damage, so if a careless fitter steps on a pipe during installation, cracks form that cost dearly later. Outdoor dacha furniture of weather-resistant polypropylene is a very good idea; travel suitcases are made of it too, as are car bumpers, medical equipment, and cold-weather gear. A similar material is metal-plastic composite pipe. **Fibreglass-reinforced plastic** is the bumper of a sports car, and fibreglass pipes carry oil and gas.

## Plumbing fixtures and wiring

As designers we place the water outlets at the expected connection points for any plumbing fixture; how the pipes are run is the builders' task, but the plumbing fixtures themselves are entirely our responsibility. A bath can be cast iron — and once could only be cast iron — but now there is a choice of steel, artificial stone, and natural stone. Cast-iron baths are popular, retaining heat and very strong, but heavy and limited in form. A stainless bath boasts low price and weight, but the water cools very fast and the forms are again few. An acrylic bath holds heat long, and acrylic is itself a warm material, though such a bath turns matte over time and cannot be restored; enamel is far better than acrylic but dearer. Baths of cast faience have a special aesthetic — a wealth of forms, very expensive because of production complexity, and brittle, usually free-standing. Beside the bath you need a towel rail, whose main value is removing moisture and therefore far less mould and fungus, while also keeping a comfortable temperature. In a tap, hot water is always connected on the left and cold on the right, because children are mostly right-handed, which helps avoid unnecessary burns.

Besides the water outlets, do not forget the sockets and switches. At the design stage you set the wiring scheme, open or concealed. The open type runs wires directly on the walls — a fast method, not pretty unless it is the designer's intent; the concealed type, used in nearly all houses, is the safe method. In Russia the voltage is 230 V. Sockets are arranged room by room, each room its own group, with lighting a separate group, as are wet zones and bathrooms. When placing sockets and switches, allow 30 cm from floor or ceiling and at least 15 cm from openings and windows, with the switch at 90 cm from the floor; sockets must not sit close to gas channels. All wires in the wall go into blue polypropylene corrugated conduit, which resists moisture and UV and is self-extinguishing. The wire itself should be copper, with its high conductivity, more flexible and easier to solder, weld, and tin, though dearer; aluminium is cheaper, but such wiring is now hard to find.

## Ceilings

A ceiling must not emit formaldehyde, since the surface is large. The cheapest option is a glued polystyrene ceiling, very popular in Eastern Europe; expensive versions exist that are eco-friendly and non-combustible. Any light material can go on a ceiling — paint, plaster — and for offices, suspended panels including mirror and metal. A ceiling can be drywall, but a flood will utterly kill it. Painting and whitewashing can be interesting too, as can plasters, bamboo, wallpaper, ceiling tile, cork, untreated wood, cube-shaped slats, plastered concrete ceilings with lacquer, and coffered vaults. But there are structural limits: you cannot apply gypsum plaster to a timber-beam ceiling, nor plaster a metal ceiling, whereas a suspended ceiling works for everything. That is why the most popular option is a continuous suspended ceiling, which most reinforced-concrete slabs will use; brackets fixed to the ceiling let you tuck away engineering systems and complex luminaires, and for commercial premises modular suspended ceilings are used. Stretch ceilings are the popular economy solution, a heated PVC film tensioned between the walls — you cannot even close the joints properly with stucco, though a shadow gap is considered handsome these days, and installation is fast; quality varies little by maker, but Barrisol and Clipso are the better choices. I would recommend a plastered ceiling: the work is filthy, not very fast, and it pauses everything else in the room, but for a typical resale flat with low ceilings it is just right, and in my practice Knauf, Ceresit, and Volma have served well. Sometimes the bare material is left — the natural texture of concrete or wood — but it must be treated all the same: for concrete, Dufa, Ceresit, Sika, Neomid; for wood, Axton, Dufa, Belinka.

## Designing real hardware

Industrial design covers not only a device's appearance but the convenience of taking its boards apart, and beauty is needed even for equipment shut away in cabinets. There must be no skew or gaps, the weld must be perfect, there must be enough solder points (usually specified in the design documentation), everything must screw and unscrew easily, and the battery holder must be convenient. Connectors must be fixed; a connector must not press into the housing, which is why brackets are used, welded to the body or fastened with rivets. Obviously you always reduce the cutout gaps as far as possible — provided this does not impede pressing the terminal-block buttons with a screwdriver — and you work with the bend tolerances on the base; a favourite manufacturer's blunder is to change the internal bend radii. A common case is when it is awkward to hold a nut while driving a screw or standoff, solved by adding press-fit nuts and bushings to the boards, or by considering standoff-to-standoff through-mounting with the holes on one axis. If modules snap in with force at an optical connector, that too is not okay, and the fix is plastic clamps or cutouts to secure the wires. That is what an industrial designer does when working on network equipment.

Even in such work there is room for aesthetics, and marking the housing has its limits: the colour of laser marking usually differs substantially from what silkscreen gives, and not for the better. Silkscreen is easy to botch as well — I aim for 0.30–0.40 mm letter thickness and demand uniform line weight — and stickers won't solve it, since they look cheap; misalignment of labels relative to the cutouts also badly spoils the impression of a product. LED lenses often shift off the centre of their holes, and uneven LED protrusion ruins the look too; the first is helped by shrinking the LED holes, but the general fix is LED holders, or better, SMD LEDs and light guides — with the constraint that you must use an LED with the lowest possible reverse current to provide the required battery life, and the dimensions of alternative LEDs often force changes to the housing and appearance that the brief may not allow, to say nothing of the labour of developing the LED driver. Meanwhile the engineers will come running to talk about vibration resistance, the housing's thermal conductivity, the power board's efficiency, operation at extremely low temperatures (−40 to −60 °C), capacitor service life, cost, and availability, and all the other constraints.

## Grids, form, and composition

Tactile feel, sound organisation, and an understanding of materials are not the whole of success. There are also **modular grids**, against which you design form, proportion, and placement. Grids for the artist are as ancient as Egypt — everyone knows from school history how strictly the grid for drawings was standardised in Ancient Egypt, where human figures could be drawn only in a particular way, strictly to canon. Four thousand years passed, and now many web designers believe a grid can only be twelve columns and a site must fit into 960 px — thanks, IE6. Artists try to argue, but the counterarguments usually break against the dogma that "design is not art but the solving of a business problem." True enough, yet that solution has a definite body of theory, a weighty pool of which lies in classical fine art.

Lighting, colour balance, the setting, the angle, and much else affect composition, yet composition cannot be decomposed into parts. The designer's choice of a particular perspective, of the image's borders, and of decorative elements depends on the goal; building the right ratios and maintaining the structure of elements within the frame eases the task of conveying the intended message and is absolutely necessary for setting the right atmosphere. The question is how the whole image relates to its components. For an experienced designer it is no secret that dependence on rules constrains the possibilities — the more so as the rules are often contrived. A good designer knows there are no "universal laws of perception," and one must always account for the activity, social status, and likely experience of the person the product is for, forms included. The conclusion: proportion in itself cannot be beautiful, but it contributes to the beauty of an industrial product.

A related point: the rounding radii used in design software are, in fact, not quite right. This is seen most clearly in Apple's products, which use a form called the squircle, both for UI and for physical devices. The essence of such rounding is continuous curvature; the form is easy to recognise, and any 3D modeller or industrial designer knows how to avoid the abrupt transition from a straight line into a curve, so that light falls on the surface softly, leaving no hard boundary. That is the theory; in practice you can round the corners of buttons better than the software does. The software leaves kinks at the boundary between the straight line and the start of the bend, and to avoid them it is enough to pull the Bézier handles somewhat further than the machine would. The whole system has long been documented and structured — you can use Apple's original icon templates as a reference.

## Automotive design

It would be a mistake not to cover automotive design as the pinnacle of industrial design. Take the "prestige distance," the large gap between the front axle and the windscreen. The crucial thing is where the line that begins at the base of the windscreen pillar lands: it should aim at the contact patch, or at least at the front wheel hub. A longer, lower, wider body always looks more prestigious and powerful. Many garage tinkerers lower their cars and stretch them without regard for the wheelbase, which only produces a foolish appearance — the distance between the wheels should be 3.5 to 4.5 times their own diameter, achievable only with large wheels. The side glass is a third of the body height; nudge the glass line down a touch and you get retro, raise it and the car turns into an armoured van, and you can add retro by setting the headlights higher too.

The interior holds plenty of interest as well: the further back the lower edge of the windscreen sits, the larger the car feels from inside. If the cabin is light, the front panel and the instrument-cluster cowl are still better kept in dark tones, otherwise there will be glare on the windscreen — solved with anti-glare film and a higher price for the car. Projects like these run on a sizeable team. For small products a designer and an engineer suffice, but a car needs at minimum a chief designer-engineer, a lead design engineer, design engineers, and, for design, a chief designer, a lead designer, a Color and Trim designer (the colour-and-materials specialist), and a UX designer. Besides the designer there is always a need for a design engineer and, ideally, the means to produce plastic and silicone parts for successful prototyping. Give a designer the task "draw a buildable touch-sensitive easel" and you need construction knowledge the engineer must hold — and the designer must understand the question too, so as not to fall victim to a host of technical constraints and to the engineer's laziness. Ideally one designer works with one or two design engineers (the calculators) on a permanent basis.

So where does one draw knowledge of the right, complex grids proven over years? Studying classical painting and understanding how it is built can give the modern designer the key to its beauty, and you can spot countless regularities in the surrounding world and bring them into your work. Return to Apple, whose goods sell out: take their favourite grid, the **Vesica Piscis** — the figure formed by the intersection of two circles of equal radius, overlapped so that the circumference of each passes through the centre of the other. The Latin name means "fish bladder," and in plain terms it is the figure from which you can construct the square root of three with a compass. Apple's designers wield this composition trick masterfully in ordinary commercial things, which lets them stand head and shoulders above competitors even without a technical advantage.

## Prototyping and 3D printing

Industrial design in Photoshop is, perhaps surprisingly, possible — by way of 3D printing, which lets you quickly prototype an object's parts. This is RP, or rapid-prototyping, technology. You create a shape, set the options, and press Create; then you give the resulting 3D model its settings, choose units of measurement, and press Move to ground to land it correctly in space. In the layers you select the scene, in Properties you choose the print material — plastic, wood, metal, sand, sugar, even chocolate — press "add 3D print setting," dial in the settings, and press "Start print." Don't worry, the model won't immediately march off to your office printer and frighten the sysadmins with strange file formats; a new window opens with a preview and colour indicators, where green shows the original mesh, blue highlights thickened elements, and red marks closed holes in the model. It also shows the print price on Shapeways.com, an option to enable ray tracing for a better sense of the material, and Print and Export buttons. You export to an STL file to hand to a 3D-printing studio. For serious industrial design, though, the tool of choice — at least in Northern Europe — is Creo.

For more grown-up work the constraints tighten. One part equals one mesh, and the mesh must be closed, with no holes (the production operator will check this too). You needn't fuss much over topology — triangulation and Decimation Master will help — and the polygon count plays no role. STL is the universal print format; download a couple of models from Thingiverse to see how it's done. Wall thickness is sometimes set directly in the 3D editor and sometimes in the printer software at print time, so it pays to know how the casting will be made, because that drives the final requirements. The main thing is not to model details smaller than the printer's resolution, and stereolithography is the most precise. For grown (additive) prints, make everything a single mesh whose surface tears nowhere; for milling it is sometimes enough to merge everything and refine the coordinates (3, 4, 5); for casting you will have to work in IGES, obtained from OBJ with Geomagic Studio; stereolithography, where resin hardens, implies saving material, so the model should be hollow. You will also need Magics to check the model for errors before printing — in short, find out what slicer the production house uses, or experiment yourself with Ultimaker tools. As for the printer, choose a direct XY-head, Z-bed machine with an enclosed housing: it moves along the X and Y axes and rises on Z, whereas a bed that travels on the Y axis loses quality on tall parts because of the shaking. A bed size from 20 cm, extruder count at your discretion, nozzle diameter 0.4 mm; the kit usually includes spools of PLA for your first experiments — a good, strong material, though ABS (slightly flexible), PETG, and SBS are better still.

To check a model in the editor, I would recommend Materialise Magics, which catches not only the main blunders but many nuances; you can also use the 3D Button service, which has a built-in check, though the internet being the internet, a model can be stolen. If you would rather not leave Rhino at all, the sequence is to select the surfaces (`selsrf` or `selPolysrf`), mesh them, and delete the surfaces; select the mesh and run `unifyMeshNormals` to correct the normal direction of every triangle; `weld` at an angle of 180; `dupborder` to check for open areas; select and delete the resulting curves; run `_FillMeshHole`; run `what` to inspect the object; then select all and `_export` (export selected).

Casting from a printed model works similarly. Finding 3D printing in copper alloys in Russia is no great problem — I recently ordered aluminium casting for housing parts. For lost-wax-style casting of bronze or brass, where the model burns out without ash, the options are: FDM/FFF with a polymer filament, where you can use PLA and ABS but must mind the coefficient of thermal expansion and the variable quality of plastic, whose ash content can run to tens of a per cent; SLS, selective laser sintering, in polystyrene or polyamide; SLA, stereolithography, using a master model with an automatically calculated honeycomb fill in a special resin, far from available everywhere; and Binder Jetting in polystyrene — fast and cheap but lower quality. If quality is the priority, though, a chill mould. In the lost-foam process, the part and the entire gating system are formed from expanded polystyrene and painted with a special paint, the whole thing is vacuum-set in sand, then the polystyrene burns away on contact with the molten metal and the metal takes its place, the combustion products being drawn off straight through the sand.

## On site: the order of works

Interior finishing of an apartment follows a process. If it is an old fund, first the demolition — tearing the apartment apart and hauling out a tonne of rubble, which is slow. Then preparatory work: creating conditions for the builders, covering everything with film and hardboard, and laying rough wiring. Then partitions of foam blocks on the slabs — brick makes no sense any more, while a foam block is fire-resistant, does not absorb moisture, is light, strong (D600), easy to drill and saw, always tied with mesh or rebar, and adds heat and sound insulation thanks to the air inside. Foam blocks vary in density from a minimum of D300 to a maximum of D1200, the larger figure meaning heavier and stronger; in almost all my projects I used D500–600 at 70 to 100 mm thick. A good alternative is aerated concrete, dearer and flatter; a sad alternative is foam-gypsum block, cheaper, worse, with minimal soundproofing; other materials include the larger tongue-and-groove block, the silicate block for strength, and the gypsum block as an accessible choice. In places drywall may be used — a plain sheet with gypsum filling in the centre, ready to be puttied at once — though it is not strong and nothing can be hung on it. Throughout, check that the corners hold at 90°.

After that, all the walls are plastered to level them, gypsum plaster for dry rooms and cement for wet. Then the heating is installed — all the risers re-welded, the systems run, the radiators roughly connected. Then the electrical work, most likely along the floor with low-current along the ceiling, all wires in conduit, cut with a margin, the sockets and back-boxes decided by now. Then the screed is poured, hiding all the wires — a semi-dry screed delivered under pressure by machine — with waterproofing in wet zones and soundproofing everywhere. Then the plumbing is run. Then the drywall: partitions, wall soundproofing, suspended ceilings, with the builders cutting holes for every device and socket. On soundproofing specifically, lay it in from the very start; it can be framed or frameless, with panels fixed by screws or anchors, the slab perimeter protected by damping tape and topped with a plain sheet of drywall — but isolate the whole room around its perimeter, not one wall, and if you do isolate by planes, use only floating schemes. Ready-made solutions can be bought from Acoustic Group, with Knauf for mineral wool and panels and Isoplat for expanded cork. Then tile. Then the windows, sills, porcelain stoneware, and ventilation. Then puttying and finishing of the walls — putty in several coats, then fibreglass mesh, then a finishing coat, finishing sanding, primer, and paint, with fewer stages under wallpaper. Then the floors are laid. Then built-in furniture and doors are installed. Then the finishing work after the walls get scratched, and the skirting goes on.

As for the price of all this, the cost of renovation in Moscow runs roughly 20–40k ₽/m² for economy, 40–60k for comfort, 60–140k for business, 140–250k for premium, and from 250k upward for luxury.

## The design process

The stages of designing an industrial product make a long process, because the idea needs to evolve — which is the trouble with designing in a single night: there is no development of the idea. It begins with the brief, covering competitors, consumers, and use situations; then a style board; then a layout scheme; then the sketch search, which is the same thing as concept art; then a 3D model and a demo presentation; then a scale mockup, which is necessary because a 3D model will not convey how the lines, forms, and scale actually work.

The whole development runs on the Stage-Gate model: you can move to the next stage only after the current one is complete, the equivalent of waterfall. The transitions are governed by quality-assessment checklists — a very simplified example asks whether the form is stable; whether there are too many elements on the surface and whether they protrude too far; whether the form is tectonic; whether the rhythmic order of the form agrees with the device's structural features; whether the form of the elements informs the user of their purpose and whether the functional zones are sufficiently revealed; and whether there are gaps, recesses, and joints where dust and dirt could collect. Inside each Gate are its own steps and internal rules, hundreds of regulations and manuals, and there is always one person responsible for the decision to move from one stage to the next. Some participants bear criminal liability for their decisions, which is why the documents carry three signatures — "executed," "checked," "approved" — and even without criminal liability, any error in the drawings that reaches production can cost many millions of dollars.

## Protecting and procuring the work

For protecting design work in Russia, computer user interfaces and individual UI elements can be patented in the form of industrial designs (*promobraztsy*). The important properties of a protectable object claimed as an industrial design are novelty and originality; if an interface element is widespread, it has most likely already been patented, either on its own or as part of a more complex object. For precise information you generally have to search the patent database at FIPS, accounting for the current classification of industrial designs, and you begin studying the question with the Russian Civil Code (at least chapters 69 and 72) and the rules for industrial designs. In my experience it is not all that hard: you can patent the appearance of an interface, divided into two categories — purely beautiful pictures as mock-ups, and samples of the laid-out interface, the latter requiring you to describe the features, field states, controls, and so on. For something more complex, or if you would rather not show up as the author, there is the concept of an anonymous patent — though to make changes you will eventually have to become the author. For large companies, holding patents is a very sound story, since billion-dollar clients like to ask about them, and here deposition helps, as it does in disputes over the date rights arose.

Procurement is the other half of the business reality. A typical procurement document states the purpose and date of the purchase, the name of the goods or service, the starting price and the principle of its formation, the volume and the order of settlement, the method of conducting the purchase, and the place of delivery — plus the requirements of Federal Law 44-FZ, such as instructions for closed auctions, open tenders, electronic auctions, and requests for proposals; in the first part of a bid, for instance, the company name must not appear. Some companies are obliged to publish their annual procurement plan on a dedicated state site, which lets the parties discuss the purchase's expediency in advance and lets suppliers prepare for the tender, and an information card is mandatory and takes priority. To determine the budget there is expert appraisal, the collection of commercial offers from outside companies, and searching the internet, including ordinary online shops — but under Part 1, Article 22 of 44-FZ the initial (maximum) contract price (NMCK) must be set by one of the methods: tariff, normative, comparable-market-price, design-estimate, or cost. Where the basis is competitive tender, 223-FZ obliges you to choose a winner under conditions you set yourself; under 223-FZ you work with companies more than 50 % state-owned and their subsidiaries, and with natural monopolies (the railways, oil and gas, regulated activities) — a state purchase in simplified form, where tenders are sometimes cancelled, making it hard to tell whether a contractor was chosen at all. 44-FZ is stricter still, covering federal and municipal budget institutions: for a large project the contractor is most likely already known, while small projects must be read through thoroughly, and before the tender you can still amend the contract and the statement of tasks. 44-FZ regulates every stage of the deal, and departing from the rules means fines and a place on the register of unscrupulous suppliers. One nuance is the national regime: the EAEU countries (Belarus, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Armenia) and Russia take priority, which can mean a full ban on foreign goods where domestic analogues exist, or exceptions for medicine and food; an American brand can fall under the ban regardless of where it is assembled, but this is resolved by the complexity of the service — buying an air-conditioner part from America triggers the regime, while selling the service of installing the air conditioner does not. Requirements for a general contractor include pre-qualification (licences and clearances for the sites, proven experience with the software and similar projects, a turnover limit for the prior period, being a local company, and owning equipment), the price, and the number and quality of staff, including knowledge of English.
