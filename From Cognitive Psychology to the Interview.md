# From Cognitive Psychology to the Interview

*Max Tsvetkov · originally published 11 October 2019. Translated and adapted from the Russian, with the author's follow-up answers from the original comment thread woven into the body as part of the argument.*

---

Practice makes one thing clear: almost nobody knows how to run an interview. Product people sprint around the market announcing that "demographics aren't personas, you have to build a CJM" — and then stop, as if naming the problem were the same as solving it. The games world isn't much healthier. Plenty of teams still lean on Richard Bartle's classic taxonomy: Achievers, Killers, Explorers, and Socializers. Bartle, a game-design professor, built the typology with online games in mind — specifically, on the strength of the first game he made back in the '90s. All four roles sit on a chart with an X and a Y axis. The Y axis runs from acting to interacting; the X axis runs from a desire to engage other players to a desire to engage the world.

![Bartle's player taxonomy on two axes](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/Group-54.png)

Killers, for instance, gravitate toward action and toward other players — because someone has to be on the receiving end. Achievers also act a great deal, but within the world; they're careerists at heart. Each type then splits again along a second dimension: whether the decision is made consciously or unconsciously. A killer who kills deliberately is, in Bartle's terms, a *politician*. One who does it without conscious intent is a *griefer* — someone who simply wrecks things and tries to violate the integrity of the world.

![Bartle's four types split into conscious and unconscious subtypes](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/Group-56.png)

Killers and careerists are usually the majority, and killers are the kings of ARPPU because they pay. Successful games are built for the careerists and explorers; do that well and the killers and socializers will automatically find something to do. Killers and socializers sit at opposite ends of a seesaw: pack a game with too many killers and the socializers thin out. Killers also churn fast, while socializers and explorers tend not to pay at all. Killers try to kill everyone except the explorers — they fear running into a devastating counter from someone armed with rare artifacts, and they fear public humiliation. Careerists interact heavily with killers and socializers even though they aren't especially liked; socializers matter to them as a source of information and as an audience for their exploits. Explorers will chat with socializers, but otherwise other players hold little interest for them — and they stay for the long haul.

![How Bartle's player types interact with one another](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/Group-58.png)

There are, of course, other ways to sort players — and people generally — by psychological type. Newzoo has one; Octalysis is another. But the framework with real momentum right now is BrainHex, which is built on the idea that different patterns of neurotransmitter activation drive different kinds of player satisfaction. Neurotransmitters are the couriers of the nervous system: they ferry electrical impulses between neurons — the neuron-to-neuron messaging you first met in school biology. Each transmitter either encourages or suppresses the production of particular chemicals in the cells. The headline four are noradrenaline (alertness, the thrill of the extreme), dopamine (the anticipation of reward), endorphins (joy), and oxytocin (the simple pleasure of a good chat).

BrainHex, then, gives us several behavioural archetypes:

1. **The Conqueror** — wants to win, to grind down difficult bosses, to walk to the edge and come back victorious.
2. **The Collector** — hunts for great objects: beautiful, vivid, rare. Happy to endure repetitive gameplay for the payoff. The core audience of farming games.
3. **The Survivor** — wants to run, to escape, to flee the monster; lives for the jump-scare that leaps out of a corner screaming.
4. **The Daredevil** — an adrenaline hunter, at home in racing games, platformers, loud firefights.
5. **The Seeker** — needs to find the treasure specifically because it's rare and off-limits to other players.
6. **The Mastermind** — needs puzzles and non-trivial quests.
7. **The Socializer** — the counterpart to Bartle's role of the same name. Wants to talk, to gossip, to live in the chat, and genuinely needs social contact; discusses the news; is, in effect, hunting oxytocin.

But motivation depends on age, personality type, and gender. All of these shape the needs that, in turn, drive someone toward one service rather than another. And never forget: the better you understand the market, the sturdier the foundation you're pouring for the product.

The usual line is that motivation is either intrinsic or extrinsic. Look a little deeper, though, and you'll find [a more interesting way to carve it up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category:Motivational_theories). One useful cut divides motivations into:

- hidden motivations and biological drivers
- individual needs
- cognitive needs
- motivations shaped by the environment

Hidden motivations and biological drivers form the base of Maslow's pyramid: eat, reproduce, avoid pain — and the social layer on top of that, such as dominance within a group, social recognition and reward, and social interaction. This is also the territory of Alderfer's ERG theory, Adams's equity theory, Vroom's expectancy theory, Locke's goal-setting theory, and Herzberg's two-factor theory of motivation.

![Herzberg's two-factor model of motivation](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Group-1.png)

Individual needs sit closer to personality — the OCEAN model, for example: Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism. Herzberg gives us a lens here too: if someone's work has fallen off and they've stopped showing initiative, the problem usually lies with the hygiene factors.

Motivations shaped by the environment are the carrot and the stick. Kill an enemy in the game, collect a reward. Miss a deadline at work, take the penalty. Rewards themselves come in two flavours — continuous and intermittent. Continuous: pass the HSK exam and the certificate stays with you for years. Intermittent: daily login rewards in games, a discount that expires in 24 hours, a payout from a slot machine.

So there are many ways to segment users — and without those skills you'll never assemble a decent persona, however powerful your empathy. Let's get into it.

## Classification

You can split users into cohorts a hundred ways: new versus returning, paying versus non-paying, veterans versus newcomers, customers versus visitors. Start digging into psychographics and the options become effectively infinite. At the broadest level, though, people fall into two big camps: those inclined toward spontaneous action and those inclined toward planning. (There are cruder cuts too, of course — the tongue-in-cheek kind: women versus men, Western culture versus Eastern.)

The first camp prefers fast interaction — an instant reply in chat, quick gameplay, low cognitive load, short horizons to a goal. They want services full of random events, where nothing needs planning and everything can be reacted to. Think Facebook, or Mario Kart Wii. The second camp likes to think, to plan, to make hard decisions while weighing the consequences. Think task planners, StarCraft, Europa Universalis, Stellaris. It's in roughly that spirit that we'll walk through the main ways of grouping users.

### Extraverts and introverts

A great deal has been said about extraverts and introverts, and almost all of it is wrong. Here's the crux: introverts are worn down by a relentless stream of the new, which they simply can't process on a timeline that feels comfortable. Extraverts don't do that kind of processing; they're busy broadcasting their worldview outward instead. In decision-making terms, the difference shows up as thinking versus feeling. Introverts build a logical sequence of facts and analyse the information; extraverts lean on empathy, on people, and can't reach a decision without convening a meeting.

Time to meet the Swiss psychiatrist Carl Gustav Jung — a friend of Freud's — who divided the human psyche into three parts. First, the *Ego*: what a person is aware of and understands, the level of conscious reason. Second, the *personal unconscious*: everything a person has run through their perceptual filters but doesn't recall as fact, though it can surface now and then from below. Third, the *collective unconscious*: our shared library of behavioural templates — the patterns by which we react to circumstances, situations, and people.

That middle layer, the personal unconscious, is the one psychologists find most interesting, because this is where a person's fears and pains are hidden, and where you can lead them back to what they've repressed. Something bad happened in the past and was pushed beyond the border of the conscious. Children who were bullied at school, for instance, will often decline to defend their position when they meet corporate aggression as adults. Over time this can even calcify into chronic illness, because the body answers physiologically — that's how deeply these patterns are wired into us. Jung read the unconscious as instinctive behaviour.

You can already build product positioning on this alone: an absence of agitation on one end, constant motion on the other. Games make the contrast crystal clear. In Civilization you advance step by step through defined stages, with pauses; the process is relaxed and predictable, free of sudden visual spikes. At the opposite pole sit Call of Duty and Super Smash Bros. Melee — a fast tempo full of abrupt situations that demand snap decisions under a surge of adrenaline. By now you're probably thinking that, in your particular case, you enjoy both shooters and strategy games — so is the whole distinction nonsense? It's simpler than that: you're an ambivert, and that's not an insult. It's the middle state between extravert and introvert, with the pendulum swinging from side to side.

Different schools that study people see the cause-and-effect of behaviour differently. The physiologists' view is refreshingly blunt: a person has energy, and off they go to spend it. Give a child a banana and they run off to fight; give a child porridge and they sit down to study. The biogeneticists have their own take: we carry ancient ancestors whose behavioural patterns we inherited, and the echoes of those patterns live in us still — we want to run and hunt in games because it's written into our genes. The social view says children imitate adults, so the behaviour of the people around us in childhood gets copied forward and shapes our adult lives. The biopsychologists say play is a way to discharge tension and return to a balanced state. Then there are the psychotherapeutic, cultural, and synthetic schools, and many more, each with its own way of classifying and explaining human behaviour. And every game is a small simulation of life, a place to resolve questions outside our ordinary existence with all its constraints. Life not adding up? It'll add up in Match 3.

Extraverts tend to chase new sensations; they're more social and more fun — and this is where we have to talk about the importance of community. Extraverts obviously find it easier to use services and play games built around community: they want to band into teams, to pour time into social interaction and conversation. Facebook, VK, Rainbow Six Siege, Battlefield, Destiny. Introverts are better served by something like Pinterest, mos.ru (Moscow's city-services portal), Hatoful Boyfriend, or Farm Heroes Saga — where the interaction is built around a single user, with full control of the situation and independence from everyone else.

That much is intuitive. Here's the counter-intuitive part: for men under 35, community matters more than it does for women. Which seems backwards, given that women are usually cast as the more communicative and social sex. And it cracks a few foundations of pop psychology: men care about community? Then how are they supposed to compete with one another — isn't rivalry the bedrock of male nature? The provider, the warrior, testosterone, all of it. Look again from another angle: some men want a free sandbox, others want precise goals to hit. It follows that the male behavioural model doesn't have to be competitive at all. In Lego Dimensions or Gone Home there's no need to fight for rank, and other players can't directly touch your progress. Compare Cities: Skylines with Aura Kingdom — the latter marches you cleanly from task to task. But when players do love conflict, you get DotA 2, League of Legends, Call of Duty: arenas, matches, duels, leaderboards, and there the testosterone kicks in, competition on the *faster, higher, stronger* principle.

By the same logic, services can introduce user rankings — or strip out every comparative feature — depending on the dominant psychotype among their visitors. Some people love a challenge and love levelling up a skill: DotA, Street Fighter V, chess. Others want quick pleasure with no punishment for mistakes: Chronicles of Chaos (Hero Wars), Oxenfree, Animal Crossing, Stardew Valley. Men under 40 value challenge more, true — but past 40 it becomes more important to women. Take the world's two largest markets, the United States and China: cultures of individualism and collectivism. You'd assume the first would strain far harder toward competition. In practice it doesn't. As you can see, you can't build personas on stereotypes. These two examples make the point plainly: not everything can be built on empathy and cliché.

Players care about the sense of a character growing, and that growth is bound up with story — the road the character travels. Yet some players want a character who's already superb from the outset and simply drops into adventures, as in 80 Days or The Longest Journey; the growth games you already know — Chronicles of Chaos, World of Warcraft, Diablo III, Summoners War. There's a wrinkle here: for some players it's enough to level a character up over the course of a single match, after which the progress resets to zero. And you can build progression either without any possibility of destruction (Riven, Myst, Harvest Moon) or with total destruction and no creation at all (Halo, Call of Duty, Battlefield).

You can also split players into those who love exploring the world and its latent possibilities (The Elder Scrolls, Fallout, The Legend of Zelda) and those who prefer the fewest extra options possible (Mahjong, FIFA, chess).

Understanding these principles is what lets you understand how to build the service itself. Say you've got a ticket to build an avatar editor for a mobile app — but what *kind* of editor? Some users want an avatar editor with a fixed set of beautiful, pre-made elements; others want a fully free graphics editor with a proper colour picker. The first group just wants to pick something lovely from a list of lovely things — the Spelunky, Super Meat Boy, Super Mario Galaxy 2 crowd. The second wants self-expression, the chance to leave their mark, to pile accessories onto their character, as in Guild Wars 2, The Sims, The Elder Scrolls. On top of all this you can layer perceptual styles: visual, auditory, digital, kinesthetic. An auditory type takes in sound; a visual type wants images and can't even hold a conversation with someone who's turned away. And then temperament — sanguine, choleric, melancholic, phlegmatic. And gender on top of *that*: across every study and in my own experience, self-expression through an avatar matters more to women than to men, regardless of age. About the only exception is that a fifteen-year-old boy may want to customise his character's appearance more intensely than a sixty-year-old woman.

We can't skip past *anima* and *animus*. The anima archetype is the unconscious feminine side of a man's personality. There's a stereotype that a man must be masculine, strong, brave, must not cry, and so on. If a man is willing to acknowledge only that portion of himself, the feminine side never finds expression — and that leads to specific distortions in how he behaves in society. The animus is the mirror image for women: a woman with a strong character can't express it because she's been fitted with the belief that she must be feminine, soft, and gentle.

In any case, don't blend all these classification systems into one and lean on that alone. You can be honest, pleasant, responsive, polite, and brave — or dishonest, rude, tactless, envious, embittered, and cowardly — under any temperament, even though temperament will colour *how* those traits show up. And character shifts across a lifetime — obvious enough from the fifteen-year-old-boy-and-sixty-year-old-woman example. So we don't treat character as a fixed constant, and not only for reasons of political correctness but for perfectly objective ones. Character is also shaped by worldview, convictions, habits, one's social circle, one's family, the sheer number of people someone has interacted with across a life, and a person's appetite for self-improvement. All of these are brutally hard to quantify. Which is precisely why Jung's archetypes are now considered a dated approach.

A quick, practical caution about the psychometric tools themselves. If you want to borrow a published questionnaire, you're obliged to credit its author — and to know which instrument you're actually using. Is it Cattell's 16PF? The Big Five? Bear in mind that a test like the MMPI has a Russian-language adaptation by L. N. Sobchik, known as Mini-Mult (SMIL / SMOL), plus a great many other interpretations floating around without proper psychometrics — while the original content is paid and owned (Pearson). Treat these instruments as licensed, attributed tools, not as raw material to be quietly rebranded as your own.

### Physiology

We've touched on age more than once, and no, this isn't ageism. People change radically over the years — sex hormones do their work, testosterone especially. At the testosterone peak, salespeople close a higher number of very profitable deals, because they're primed to take risks (this holds for women as much as for men). Between 20 and 30 the risk of becoming a killer is at its highest — the trivial killings committed in a burst of emotion. Then comes the parental period: people age, acquire children and a steady partner, and the body's chemistry shifts accordingly.

Start with the simple stuff. Anyone who's spent time in a gym has heard the split into ectomorphs (thin), mesomorphs (naturally athletic and muscular), and endomorphs (carrying extra weight). The German psychiatrist Ernst Kretschmer argued that the body determines character. These are the most visible and easily verified markers, and they bear on the type of nervous system, which in turn shapes character. It shows up even at the level of ordinary eating: an ectomorph can eat whatever they like without a permanent diet. That one fact alone strongly colours how a person perceives the world — an ectomorph will never quite understand the endomorph who gains half a kilo from merely *looking* at a cake. There are clinical threads too: hysteria and depression run more typical for mesomorphs and endomorphs, while symptoms of anxiety and fear lean toward ectomorphs. Physique, however, has no bearing whatsoever on intellectual ability — and this is roughly where HR can stop reading.

Sport and diet can turn you from an ectomorph into a mesomorph for a while. But the core constitutional features — the ones set by heredity and formed while still in the womb — stay very nearly constant across a lifetime. And just as a person with undeveloped muscles gives a wide berth to the dumbbells, barbells, and the ordeal of hauling a fridge up ten flights of stairs, so our user will steer clear of tasks that demand long, sustained, concentrated effort. In neurophysiology, the strength or weakness of the nervous system is judged by its capacity — or incapacity — to sustain a state of excitation over time.

So you've just isolated one of the conditions for pinning down a personality type: whether the nervous system can withstand continuous load. For us that means some users simply can't boast of steady stamina; they need frequent breaks. Such people seek out the easy path to solving a problem, especially in interfaces that offer a choice, and they'll happily pay extra for their comfort. Handing someone like that monotonous work that requires painstaking care and sustained attention is, predictably, a lost cause. These are also the people who turn up late more often and take a couple of sick days for no visible reason — and yes, that's a recruiting criterion for respondents. Someone who's forever taking two-day sick leaves? That's our person.

Which is why we don't discard stereotypes when sourcing respondents. Suppose we need the schizoid type — from the classification of *accentuations*, not the clinical diagnosis. Thin people with elongated limbs and torso, a long neck, a small head, often a drawn, sharply defined nose set against a small lower jaw. Their skin tends to be pale, their hair thick and coarse. To be clear one more time: the schizoid accentuation has nothing to do with clinical illness. These are simply people who don't want to open up, who chew a problem over carefully. They don't communicate well, though; their anchor is the intellect; they look awkward and keep to themselves; there are no wild swings along their life path; and they don't tolerate criticism of their thinking or their values. On the strength of these very stereotypes we can hand-pick respondents when the target audience is that narrow.

Other popular accentuations are worth a line each. The **hysteroid** type lives their whole life in the spotlight and craves your affection; excellent for work that involves charming and captivating other people — but the energy is short-lived and there's no depth to it. The **cycloid** type is not built for monotonous work: obsessed with ideas, forever wanting to launch a startup, gifted at getting people fired up, superb in idea generation — but never goes deep on the topic. The **epileptoid** type is the disciplined, faintly unnerving one. Once they take on a project they won't let go until it's done. They demand obedience yet come across as gentle, softening their words (a little cookie, a little salad) — but the sweetness is an act. They live by *sit down and get it done*. They love money, competitive sport, controlling their food, and order in all things.

This may all read like nonsense, but try holding the following against real life:

- People in research work tend to be taller and thinner than labourers on a production line.
- Long-haul drivers show a more pronounced mesomorphic build.
- Harvard students in the natural and social sciences look more athletic than the art historians, philologists, and philosophers.
- Aspiring engineers, physicians, and dentists show more mesomorphy and less ectomorphy than physics and chemistry students.
- Young male offenders tend toward an above-average mesomorphic build.

These can serve as criteria when you build personas and recruit respondents — almost certainly as secondary criteria rather than as part of a quota. It's far from settled that this is the right approach, but the documented link between character and physiology is worth knowing, if only within the basics of cognitive psychology.

If you're reading this and trying to map the theory onto your own colleagues, you're better off reaching for the DISC methodology, devised by William Moulton Marston. He sorted people into four categories along two dimensions: whether they read the world as friendly (people-oriented) or hostile (goal-oriented), and whether they're extraverted or introverted. Combine those four parameters and you get the following grid:

![The DISC grid across two dimensions](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Artboard.png)

1. Achieving their goals through aggressive influence.
2. Influencing through an engaging personality.
3. Willing to adapt, to care for others, wanting stability.
4. Surviving by adapting.

The **first** are natural-born leaders — they know how to command, the archetypal entrepreneurs. Every change is an opportunity. But they're not built for routine, not for teamwork; they don't submit and they don't bend. They want only victory and they break at defeat. With them you communicate fast, sharp, and to the point: hold eye contact, argue, meet pressure with pressure.

The **second** are opinion leaders — sociable, cheerful, natural negotiators. But they're not about detail, analysis, routine, or punctuality; they play to the crowd. With people like this you smile with your whole face — the muscles around the mouth *and* the eyes — take an interest in their opinion, and feed the ego. No public criticism, ever.

The **third** are about empathy, care, team, conservatism. They're prone to guilt and low on ambition. They dislike change. In a meeting they may agree with the group decision, then quietly try to sabotage it afterwards.

The **fourth** are outstanding analysts. They'll analyse, plan, and systematise everything. Strategic thinkers, strong at process management. Their weak spot is improvisation; they show little emotion, dislike hard deadlines, aren't especially team players, and want to be right about everything. They read compliments as an attempt to ingratiate.

As always, real people are combinations of these characteristics — usually with two dimensions dominant. A somewhat more elaborate system is MBTI.

## Getting ready for the interview

We've made a start on how to classify the potential respondents for a test of a hypothesis or an interface. When is the best time to run one? Always. At every stage — Product Discovery and Product Delivery alike. There's no such thing as too much research; forming fresh ideas and directions is always useful. Not every product manager or business owner grasps this, which is exactly why free research goes unappreciated — and yet sourcing respondents is always hard and expensive. You have to study the company and its competitors in detail, identify the target audience, and find a reason for those people to show up. There's a whole stratum of people who sit interviews professionally, earning money from it, so you end up phoning candidates in advance and running micro-interviews just to check whether they actually understand the domain. Alternatively, if you're running Firebase or MixPanel, you can push a research invitation straight into your clients' mobile app, or lean on marketing's audience-targeting work, where a plain email blast can do the job. A few baseline rules for send timing:

- **Entertainment, self-education.** Friday evening (17:00–19:00) and weekends.
- **Consulting, marketing, advertising.** Tuesday, Wednesday, or Thursday — 12:00–13:00 or 16:00–21:00.
- **Surveys.** Monday evening.
- **E-commerce.** Friday tends to work best.
- **B2B.** Tuesday, Wednesday, or Thursday.

None of this lands, of course, in an organisation that doesn't yet believe in research — so it's worth being honest about where your company sits and how to move it forward. Picture three stages. In the first, there's no research function at all; here the designer has to run the simple studies themselves — hallway tests, questionnaires, first-click studies (usabilityhub.com) — and carry the results to the managers. In the second, a UX researcher has appeared; now the work is to bank successful case studies and translate them into the managers' own KPIs, until, over time, converts to the *research-first* approach start to emerge. This is also the moment for business breakfasts, a short video digest of findings, an insights newsletter, a training session on research for the product managers — or, if the budget allows, a genuinely strong outside coach. In the third stage, research is simply part of how the company builds: it's written into the roadmap as a mandatory step, fixed in the process documents. A designer or researcher can hold a product back from release if no research was done — or cancel a study outright if the product manager couldn't be bothered to come and watch it happen.

Even when you hire an agency to recruit, you still have to review respondent profiles alongside the client. Finding the right people is hard. Here's a real set of conditions: we need car owners who personally bought their insurance online, through the mobile version of the site. Hard? Which is why there's money involved.

Building a budget matters. Recruiting prices vary wildly and drift year to year. For a quantitative study by questionnaire aimed at a Western audience, the numbers run something like this: 3,000 people at €24 a head — the cost should scale up or down with the sample. For a qualitative study without an honorarium you might pay ₽4,200 for a qualified person, and that's recruiting alone; for a trickier profile, from ₽5,000. All-in, it lands somewhere between ₽7,000 and ₽12,000 — ₽42,000 for a single respondent including the report and analysis. Premium respondents who are chronically short on time start at ₽20,000. People for everyday-goods testing run about ₽1,500. If the client is genuinely difficult — usually C-level people (CEO, CIO, CTO, CKO) — the cost climbs further still. It also depends on the recruiting method: active recruiting versus passive (a shout-out to your existing base). A trip to the Americas or Asia is about $3,500; Europe, about $2,500.

There's also the question of whether to pay respondents at all, and there's no single right answer — only a set of arguments on each side.

*Against paying:* the motivation of paid users is distorted, and they may be afraid to voice criticism. It's cheaper to find intrinsic motivation in the respondent — a sysadmin will be delighted to visit an attractive researcher and hold forth for hours about server configuration. Decision-makers are ferociously expensive to recruit anyway. If someone's willing to talk for free, that's strong evidence you've already put your finger on a real pain. And paying constantly for fast feedback stops being fast: stepping out to the nearest café and asking around is quicker and simpler.

*For paying:* the motivation of people who *volunteer* their time for free is *also* distorted — they're either fiercely loyal or in so much pain that they've come to complain. I've seen many times that a token payment for slogging through a dull survey lifts completion rates; a $5 Amazon voucher carries respondents cleanly to the end of a ten-minute survey, and for the truly tiny ones (a ten-cent job) Google Opinion Rewards will do the same for $0.10. Payment is a micro-commitment — it's harder to bail on an interview you've already been paid to attend. Rare, difficult respondents rarely turn up for free; the search drags on, becomes unpredictable, and narrows the pool of people you can even talk to. Trying to do everything for free is a reputational risk that can turn into a bad marketing story and kill your brandformance. And there's an ethical dimension: saying *thank you* for someone's time and effort. Getting to your office and giving up an hour or two is, unmistakably, work. A reasonable middle path is to recruit without promising money, then, after the conversation, hand over swag, gifts, cash, coupons, or free licences for your products — antivirus vendors can hand out activation codes, hardware makers can give away kettles or toasters. Or don't pay the respondent at all, but donate to charity on their behalf. Or pay only some of them — raffle off $50 across the survey.

And then there's the plague of no-shows. If one or two out of eight fail to appear — or, on a bad day, six of them — a few things are usually going wrong. First, it's worth sending the interview invitation personally, with an option to add the meeting to a calendar straight from the email; that heads off scheduling clashes and time-zone confusion, especially when respondents are on PST (UTC−08:00). Second, *where* are you sourcing them? If you're luring them in with banners on your own site and questionnaires, the drop-out is entirely predictable. Third, there has to be real motivation. I don't know which country you're recruiting in, but for the US, $40 an hour is very little; you want $70 minimum. That bump is still cheaper than the losses from a no-show — and even then, a 10–20% no-show rate is normal for the US. Fourth, find *floaters* — people who'll block out an entire day for you and get paid more for it; across a day they're far more likely to be reachable. Or simply recruit more people than you strictly need.

So — how many people do you actually need? For defining the target audience, 5 to 15 respondents. For forming a view of the market and the broad direction of trends, 30 to 50. For teasing out potential buyers and building personas, 15 to 30. Gaskin, Griffin, and Hauser recommend 10 to 30 interviews. Daniel Bertaux, back in 1981, concluded that 15 is the minimum. Greg Guest settled on 12. Nielsen is confident that five interviews will do.

That last one deserves a closer look. There's an ancient myth that five respondents are enough to surface 80% of the information — a debt we owe to Bob Virzi in 1990. It holds up for expert heuristic evaluations with tightly, unambiguously defined tasks, and those interviews can run for hours when you can't find many experts on the subject. Jakob Nielsen and Thomas Landauer then looked at five studies (five!) and decided that eight respondents were enough to capture everything you needed. In an ideal world you'd interview a great many people, bounded only by your resources. But we don't live in an ideal world: at 1.5 hours per interview — plus preparation, plus a break, plus 2–3 hours of analysis and transcription, plus another hour of clustering — you're looking at three full working days. This is where the RITE method rides in: talk to just one or two respondents a week and make instant changes to the prototypes before the next session. It pulls respondents right into the development loop. RITE + usability testing + the occasional A/B test = a working machine for validating interface hypotheses.

Just keep this in mind: if four or five respondents confirm that a pain exists, that is *not* 80% of the audience. It's somewhere between 36% and 98%. If you're still forced to present five-interview results to the business, calculate the confidence interval. It won't be "three out of five"; it'll be "23%–88%." You can check it yourself with [this calculator](https://measuringu.com/calculators/wald/). Note that you need binary outcomes (problem present, problem absent). By default we set statistical significance at 95%, so our "23%–88%" will hold in 95% of cases — not bad at all for a startup. The attentive reader will notice that all these interval estimates overlap heavily, except for 1-of-5 and 5-of-5, which makes them somewhat meaningless. So you really want 25 to 35 observations.

There's a sounder approach: quantify the *cause* of the problem for those very three or four respondents. Say three information-security specialists hit trouble with Astra Linux while two are perfectly content. When you interview, the details are the point — and it turns out the three with trouble are bound to follow regulatory rules. So you look at the specifics of the companies those specialists work for, then find out how many comparable companies exist on the market.

Look at it this way: the research method is the 1×1 interview, and the analysis method is affinity mapping / clustering. We're interviewing students from various countries about how comfortable it is to study in Russia, and we want to build a persona — generative work. Are five interviews enough for 85% of the knowledge? There are more than five countries. In cases like that, the interview count should be 10 to 20.

Interviews are my favourite way to test hypotheses, and interviews come in kinds. UX interviews divide into Broad versus Structured. Consider a provocative long-form interviewer of the Yury Dud school: the goal is to trigger emotion, to needle the guest into stating an opinion. A job interview, by contrast, sits closer to a test. A UX interview aims to surface a user's thoughts and feelings and to learn the stories of how they use the product. So we give the interviewee complete freedom to express their thoughts in their own everyday vocabulary, with whatever emotion they bring; we do the interpreting afterwards. And it needn't be confined to validating an interface — it's a fine tool at the concept stage too, when the question is simply *which way to think*.

Watch out for future-tense questions, direct questions (do you like the idea or not?), and closed questions. None of these belong in your original interview plan. One caveat: closed questions are permitted — you need them to narrow a respondent's line of reasoning. That provocative-interviewer archetype again: he's stuffed his conversations with future-tense, direct, and closed questions that goad the guest into pronouncing judgments or into defending themselves — but *his* goal is to knock the guest out of their comfort zone and provoke a headline-worthy answer. So switch off your inner provocateur.

![Where each method fits: defining the concept versus validating the solution](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/Frame-3.png)

*Defining the concept: ethnography and focus groups.
Validating a finished solution: usability testing, prototypes.*

A word on focus groups, since they sit on that first side of the line. A focus group runs 4 to 8 people; it's qualitative. The sample should be homogeneous — one social stratum — but *without* a single shared view on the question. More than eight and it becomes hard to control. You'll meet negative opinions about focus groups, but in the end they're a tool, and the job is to know how to wield one. The most common complaint is that people influence one another, or that an opinion leader forms instantly. That's a matter of skilfully moderating the group's dynamics. The moderator has to hold the position of a parent and must never, under any circumstances, give feedback to the respondents — otherwise the answers get distorted.

Ask a person whether they'd like a self-refilling glass and they'll say "yes!" Rephrase it — would they *buy* a self-refilling glass — and the answer is "no." People want to seem better than they are, so they answer with a subconscious urge to show themselves in the best light. Call it the social-rigidity effect (it's the same family as social-desirability bias). The single most important thing is to choose the right people, the right segment — which is where we started this article. You need a hypothesis to launch from: *I believe that people who eat sausages love cats.* And I want to build a business on it — so I head to the grocery store to find people buying sausages and ask whether they have a cat and how they feel about it.

When uncertainty is enormous — a targeting system, say — you can talk to anyone at all: whoever's nearby, and after the conversation you'll see how to narrow the funnel. At the end of an interview it's worth asking, "Are there any questions I *should* have asked you?" It works. If you've built the connection, the respondent will tell you a great deal more. And the second closer: "Who else should I talk to about this?" You may be pointed toward smart people — and even handed their contacts.

Prioritising questions matters. The business will push you to ask, "How much money is the client willing to pay us?" or "Get them to tell us more about the features they need." The business always wants to cram every kind of question into a single interview. That's poor research design, especially if what you're running is not merely an interview but a usability test. There's a framework for this called NCredible, built from four big blocks:

![The NCredible framework of four question blocks](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Group-13.png)

- **elaborate** — how do people relate to the topic at all?
- **validate** — which hypotheses hold up and which don't?
- **discover** — what should we focus on?
- **define** — which solution do we choose?

The two left-hand squares are about deep understanding of a person's pains. The lower-left is questions like "How do people feel about shopping online?" and "Do they share promo codes — and why, or why not?" The upper-left is a different sort of question: "Many people find online shopping hard — why?" The two right-hand squares are about solving the problem. Lower-right: "Which functions do you use most often in an online store?" and "How does a consultant reach out to you about your questions — and is that convenient? why?" Upper-right: "What could we improve in our mobile app?"

The choice of aggregation framework, incidentally, is sometimes made before a single interview. It can show up right in the requirements, when the client names a fashionable framework — JTBD, the Value Proposition Canvas, a CJM. Perhaps the client's budget only stretches to a CJM; in that case, present a range of methodologies to choose from, from expensive to cheap. Where possible, interview the client first and find out which problem they actually want the framework to solve; if the client is unreachable, talk to the stakeholders and use a problem interview to identify everyone with a stake. (And for building a data-driven CJM inside a large company, Retentioneering performs well on medium data volumes — its real strength is graph visualisation, which clients invariably love.)

The truth is that interviewing is easy. People are self-absorbed; they *want* to talk about themselves. Start by learning what life is like *without* your service — and at this stage say nothing about your product. Deliver a prepared explanation of why the interview is happening and how much their opinion matters to you.

When you invite someone, avoid the words *interview* and *survey*. *Testing* can misfire too. Just explain that we're building a product for the user, and that we're testing the *product*, not the person. School trained us that mistakes are forbidden; that barrier, lodged in the respondent's subconscious, has to be lifted for the duration of the session. Capture data with audio plus notes: audio catches intonation, and quick notes are simply handy. It's not a bad idea to reach for the AEIOU framework to document what you observe.

## Conducting the interview

Interviewing is tightly bound up with emotional intelligence — the moment you ask a respondent not only what they *think* but what they *feel*. Mastery arrives only with experience. You can read books and manuals forever; your first interviews will still be dreadful.

Any interview works well when you're hunting for the *reason* behind an action. Start from the respondent's problems and talk like a friend — not "hi, I want to extract a couple of facts from you, here's some money, now tell me why you did that." That doesn't work; you have to dial in empathy. Ask for examples from life. And throughout, you hold a neutral stance — no advice, no opinions. Suppose someone gets all their knowledge about graphics editors from articles and almost never cements it in practice. You must not deliver a value judgment — "it'd be better if you spent more time on hands-on work." Let the respondent talk; your job is to understand *why* they do it, not to teach them. That's a problem interview, where we uncover the problem. On a solution interview we'll be free to propose more effective ways to learn.

Solution interviews, then, happen once there's a minimum product. Here we're trying to understand whether the product fits the customer and how to improve it. It's closer to sales: after the problem interview we should know what product our audience needs. You build the product, ring back the same people from the first interview, and invite them to try it. These people can readily become your early adopters. If the product solved the need, you've essentially made a sale — your first, and a fiercely loyal customer, because they helped create the thing.

The interview process itself: begin with reconnaissance using broad questions, learning more about the domain — this is what lets you build personas and find the growth points for the business. Do it in context, in the respondent's natural habitat, whether that's a BMW M760i or a McDonald's. This approach is called ethnography. Then the questions narrow to probe a specific field of knowledge, learning how people solve their pressing problems. You have to know how to listen and to sift out the grains of knowledge about how *this particular* respondent perceives the world. Each respondent is unique, and you mustn't warp their answers through the prism of your own experience and perception.

Your appearance matters too: the most neutral style possible, minimal makeup, tidy, nothing loud in the clothing. A business suit — an immediate no. The task is not to provoke the respondent's inner adult and to delight their inner child. When the respondent speaks, listen actively and hold their gaze — not the list of questions. Active and non-reflective listening, and echoing. Sit *beside* the respondent, not across a table, your body turned toward them. It's just the two of you in the room, without a crowd of developers and managers at your back. There's a term, *rapport* — a certain degree of trust between people. Rapport is exactly what lets you run the interview in a pleasant, trusting register and surface a great many meaningful things. And at the end, participants should never leave upset or offended.

The goal of the research: to obtain objective knowledge about behavioural factors and about how users perceive the result of our work. A user can think one thing, say another, and do a third. Behaviour is more truthful than words. It's not that users want to lie to you — it's simply how psychology works: we all always want to seem a little better than we really are. If you lack a clear end goal for the interview, here are the standard ones: user needs (making their life better), the value of features (are they willing to pay?), and emotions (the reflective level of design, the overall final impression of the product).

Constructing a question well is a skill in itself. Always separate the *object* and the *subject*. In "Why did the team decide to keep using the iPhone?", the team is the object and the iPhone is the subject. You can sharpen it: instead of "the team," use the precise "managers aged 30 to 40"; instead of "the iPhone," name the exact model. Handle nouns with care, too — a noun always plants a hint. But you can't build sentences without them, so reach for more abstract nouns; the result is less specific answers, which you then have to clarify and develop during the interview, closed questions included. It sounds involved, but it's the main way to avoid tipping the respondent off to the expected answer. Concrete nouns will serve you well in online surveys and other quantitative methods.

When you gather qualitative data, ask about the *specific user* — not their advice to the business, not their opinion, not their vision of the future — and mine for insight. These are called open questions. You can even frame answers within ranges, on soft or hard scales:

- Yes / No = ratio
- Agree / Not sure / Disagree
- A five-point scale, where 5 is the top mark and 1 the lowest quality
- A scale from 10 to 0, where 10 = often and 0 = never — an interval scale
- Never / Rarely / Sometimes / Often / Very often = an ordinal, rank scale
- Why > why > why…

*Bad:* on a scale of 1 to 5, how often do you eat nuts?
*Good:* how often do you eat nuts? — with answer options: every day, once or twice a week, a few times a month.

*Bad:* does our messenger need a group-chat feature?
*Good:* how often do you use group chats? how many people are usually in one? which functions do you use in a group chat?

*Bad:* what don't you like about our service?
*Good:* why do you prefer to use other services?

*Bad:* are you satisfied with the new interface?
*Good:* the audience's opinion of our interface has shifted — what do you put that down to?

*Bad:* how much would you pay to filter your email newsletter?
*Good:* hey, would you like to buy an email-filtering service right now? Yes (hands over real money), no (why?).

*Bad:* how was our latest release?
*Good:* tell me about your most recent experience with the product.

*Good:* is there anything you'd like to talk about that I didn't ask you?

---

During the interview you either follow a pre-written script, reading each question in exactly the order it appears in the scenario — or, less rigidly, you work from a guide (a structured list of themes and follow-up questions), which is more flexible. A guide implies no answer options but steers you into the right channels. Guide-based results are harder to analyse but more personalised, and their quality hinges directly on the interviewer's skill. These are structured and semi-structured data.

If you're a very experienced specialist, you can run an *unstructured* interview. All you hold is a list of themes; the boundaries are all but gone. The results are harder still to analyse and to hand off to the team.

An example of a **structured** script:

- Have you made purchases in our service? Yes / No
- Does our product make you feel: happy; nothing; unhappy?

An example of a **semi-structured** script:

- Have you made purchases in our service? Yes / No
- If yes, name one feature you liked.
- If no, name one feature that led you not to buy.

An example of an **unstructured** script:

- What associations does our service bring up for you?
- If you think about it more deeply, what experiences do you suppose underlie those associations?

The main downsides of the structured interview: you can't ask follow-ups, the scope is fenced in by the script, response bias is harder to spot, and respondents may simply pick from the options offered. In a semi-structured interview, other cognitive biases get in the way of objective data — the Hawthorne effect, observer bias, recall bias, and social-desirability bias.

All three variants are textbook customer development: a one-on-one, in-depth interview with people (anywhere from 20 minutes to 6 hours) who could become early adopters. To repeat: we're after not facts but the *interpretation* of facts. In the report this is usually labelled **POV** — point of view.

During the interview, ask "why?" often.

- — Why do you order food to your home?
- — Because I don't want to lug heavy bags back.
- — And what's the reason for that — why?
- — Because my wife and I agreed on it.
- — Why?
- — We don't like cooking at home, we don't like going to the shop, and we can't carry heavy bags.
- — Why?
- — Because we get home late; by then the shops are out of the products we actually want — our favourite fish salad has gone off in the summer heat — so we're left buying a pile of raw ingredients to cook, and we've no time, because we get home late.

See how doggedness digs down to the facts? The technique was devised at Toyota and is used to surface deep cause-and-effect chains. Here's Toyota's own worked example:

- — The battery's dead.
- — Why?
- — The alternator isn't working.
- — Why?
- — There's a problem with the alternator belt.
- — Why?
- — It went too long without being replaced, though it was fine to begin with.
- — Why wasn't it replaced?
- — The car went too long without a service.

A handful of questions and we've found the root cause: it isn't the alternator belt at all — it's that people need reminding about servicing. The technique can irritate a respondent, so the canonical rule is no more than five "whys."

Beyond the one-on-one interviews above, there are also *remote interviews*. This way you can poll far more people; it's the format of social surveys. Empathy with the user is naturally much weaker, the interview itself is short, and you don't control the environment.

Recording a remote *phone* interview has its own tooling. On the phone there's no shortage of apps — Cube ACR, for one. In a pinch you put the call on speaker and set a recorder beside it, though the quality will be poor and some transcription services may refuse a recording like that. Recording *from within apps* is its own creature: Skype records calls out of the box, but most messengers now detect recording attempts and cut the recorders during an audio or video call. Chinese smartphones with built-in root still haven't disabled two-way recording. (Parsec is quite good, incidentally, though it can't capture keystrokes.)

For remote *usability testing* of a mobile app, tool choice comes down to your audience. If they're technically literate, Lookback.io works well. Otherwise, the now-classic Zoom already screen-shares Android. Prototypes can be tested with marvelapp.com/user-testing. On the Western market, Validately and UserFeel are popular for unmoderated tests you don't have to attend, and new tools keep appearing, such as uxtesting.io. Maze is handy for A/B tests and quantitative work with rotation.

A video interview is an option, and it's a touch more personal than a phone call, because you can hold the respondent's gaze and nod along. But interviews like this demand the respondent download special software and have a webcam and microphone, and you still don't control the environment. If there's a repair crew working in the respondent's home, the interview collapses — unless, of course, you're studying how people feel while a repair crew is working. Internet quality can be another stumbling block. So before the interview, always test the whole rig and the setups; during it, speak slowly and clearly (bad internet = the audio drops), nod, and hold eye contact. Ideally you have a backup channel to the respondent and a text chat on standby.

From a face-to-face interview you can go on to run conversation analysis. It's used in applied research; we practised it in my university department. The essence: a meticulous record of how people communicate, non-verbal signals included — a kind of transcription of the video. It's a labour-intensive affair; a couple of minutes of a person talking to a voice assistant can take a whole week to break down.

### Reading signals beyond words

Words are only part of the picture, which is why there's an entire practice of pulling data off neuro-devices — and it's worth knowing the shape of it. There are neuro-headsets like the NeuroSky MindWave / MindSet: wireless, battery-powered (around 30 hours), able to measure *attention* and *meditation*. There are fitness trackers. Gyroscope-equipped devices can log sleep phases; phones carry SLAM, a gyroscope, an accelerometer, and a magnetometer, and can gather plenty besides. Honestly, just order yourself a TGAM board and start experimenting.

There's a whole field called GSR — galvanic skin response, the electrical resistance of the skin. When a person is over-aroused, the sweat glands activate and conductivity rises; sensors clip to the hands and feet. The catch is that the readings are slow — the skin-conductance response (SCR) can take up to 30 seconds — so it's unsuitable for games or services with fast-moving change, and it offers no clean split between positive and negative arousal.

Then the classic: eye trackers. The proper name is oculography, and it speaks directly to whether a button is noticeable. Trackers vary — there's the pricey Tobii, and the cheap-to-free eViacam, TrackEye, and WebGazer, though the free ones struggle with head-turn tracking and will happily mistake a power socket for a face. You needn't buy a dedicated device at all; a webcam plus Gazerecorder can suffice. And there are the rest of the instruments: EEG modules (brain activity), PPG (photoplethysmography from the earlobe), bio-bracelets (autonomic / electrodermal responses), prime-boxes (reaction speed). For children there are special bracelets worn on the heels. Everything they capture should flow into hubs that collect the lot and interpret it against interest, memorability, emotional engagement, and attention. Plenty of people have medical kit at home already — a blood-pressure monitor, a glucose meter, a pulse oximeter, even scales plus a thermometer — and all of it can be bent, one way or another, to the task.

The first thought is always: I'll just buy the cheapest Tobii and be done. And yes, the ₽10,000 models run happily over USB and stream coordinates over TCP/IP, or you use the proprietary USB protocol (on a Windows machine). But both modes require an SDK — either the Gaze SDK for Linux or the Stream Engine SDK — and here the saga begins. The SDK is hard to get hold of without the authors' blessing, and we can't obtain a respondent's permission to save their gaze coordinates if the developer never issued us the SDK in the first place. The Stream Engine SDK is easier to come by, but it only supports the Tobii 4c. REX is another SDK, also not bad. A Vive Pro Eye plus Tobii's SDK is a good combination. And — surprise — eye trackers treat gaze as personal data, so you have to sign a personal-data agreement with the respondent and clear the rest of the legal and technical hurdles (saving personal data to a file).

From an eye tracker you get: the number of fixations, their duration, the total fixation count, saccade speed, saccade length, pupil diameter, viewing speed, and how many fixations it took to find the right button — assuming the respondent doesn't have an astigmatism, of course. As for visualisation, there's plenty: the cheap option is gaze plots, which chart the sequence of fixations with the number and duration of each; the classic is heat maps via Tobii Ghost; and there's the inversion, fog maps.

Mobile eye-tracking, in particular, is fiddly. I recently used the Tobii mobile stand and came away ambivalent: it's hard to calibrate, and both the phone and the respondent end up in an unnatural posture. If the respondent leans forward, the signal drops, because the tracker sits just above the legs. Glasses with a built-in tracker are the more elegant solution, but a phone screen is small and the accuracy may not stretch to it, on top of a fairly low 100 Hz. I've worked with Tobii Glasses 2; on the desktop it was all fine, but on phone screens there wasn't enough accuracy to land on small, moving targets.

None of this replaces a simpler question you can answer with a plain questionnaire: is the *visual* any good? Start with a hypothesis — say, the new visual reflects the company's values better and provokes no negativity. Make two variants, show them, and ask people to complete a questionnaire. And when you show the variants, don't just play them one after another — balance the order with a Latin square. In the questionnaire, ask people to describe in their own words the impression the new interface makes, then run a Likert scale across the value attributes, and the Microsoft Desirability Toolkit with weights on the words.

And the single most important thing: record the interview. A dictaphone, a pen — these are your friends. Re-listen to your recordings; I promise you'll be amazed at how much you fumbled, or how off-target a question turned out to be. That's what lets you correct the next interview — a kind of agile for interviewing. Immediately afterwards, cluster the insights — just writing out the key points over time. If you're doing that in Excel in real time, the hotkey **Shift + Ctrl + ;** stamps the current time into a cell. Or you can right-click the sheet tab, choose *View Code*, and paste in the following:

```vba
Private Sub Worksheet_Change(ByVal Target As Range)
    Dim rInt As Range
    Dim rCell As Range
    Dim tCell As Range

    Set rInt = Intersect(Target, Range("B:B"))
    If Not rInt Is Nothing Then
        For Each rCell In rInt
            Set tCell = rCell.Offset(0, -1)
            If IsEmpty(tCell) Then
                tCell = Now
                tCell.NumberFormat = "mmm d, yyyy hh:mm"
            End If
        Next
    End If
End Sub
```

Now, the moment you create a new entry in column B, column A pulls in the current time automatically.

And for Word: go to *View → Macros → View Macros* and create a new macro with this code:

```vba
Public Sub TimeStamp()
    Selection.InsertDateTime _
        DateTimeFormat:="MM/dd/yyyy hh:mm:ss" & _
        " - ", InsertAsField:=False
End Sub
```

Run it once and it inserts the time in the format `05/28/2020 09:48:28 -`. Then head into Word's settings: *Options → Customize Ribbon → Customize…* In the window that appears, choose the category *Macros*, select your macro on the right, and assign it a hotkey.

![Assigning a hotkey to the timestamp macro in Word](https://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/photo_2020-03-08_13-09-39.jpg)

When you'd rather collect data through a form and have the results land straight in a spreadsheet — handy when there's a lot of it — there are add-ons over Google Sheets called AppSheet and Glide. You get your own admin panel with proper validation, a calendar, decent UX, and small JSON payloads: form the JSON → the client/server reads the file → the parameters are assembled on the front end.

### Turning transcription into tagged insight

Once you've recorded, you have to transcribe — and the least painful route today is OpenAI's Whisper, which runs briskly on an M2; a [Colab notebook](https://colab.research.google.com/drive/1RlABBjIfIYYBa1oAeOGDRKb7iHH2fz2P?usp=sharing) is enough. If you'd rather do it by hand, the flow is:

```bash
cd /tmp
brew install gh
gh repo clone ggerganov/whisper.cpp
cd whisper.cpp
bash ./models/download-ggml-model.sh base
bash ./models/download-ggml-model.sh medium
make
```

Pull the audio out of a video, where `f0RCGMCphUM` is the YouTube video ID:

```bash
brew install yt-dlp ffmpeg
yt-dlp -x --audio-format wav -o test.wav -- f0RCGMCphUM
```

Convert the audio to 16-bit WAV:

```bash
ffmpeg -i test.wav -ar 16000 -ac 1 -c:a pcm_s16le test_16bit.wav
```

And run the recognition:

```bash
./main -f test_16bit.wav -l ru -m models/ggml-medium.bin
```

Before Whisper existed, the go-to services in the post-Soviet space were [Zapisano](https://zapisano.org/) and [Transkribator](https://transkribator.want.ru/) (naming the second one so the first doesn't read like an ad); in English, [Temi](https://www.temi.com/) and [Trint](https://trint.com/). A large recording can be turned into text in Yandex Cloud for a small fee (Russian, English, and Turkish). Or, badly but free, run the audio through Google Translate. If you're transcribing the old-fashioned way, by hand in Excel or a spreadsheet, it's probably faster to sort the text straight into data clusters as you go.

For the tagging itself, run down the list of [CAQDAS software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer-assisted_qualitative_data_analysis_software) and pick the one that suits emotional tagging best. QDA Miner and Reframer are perfectly fine; Dedoose, less so. These days I'd look toward [Dovetail](https://dovetailapp.com/), or Aurelius / NomNom / Reframer — and don't overlook [Glean.ly](http://glean.ly/) or [Airtable](https://airtable.com/), which the standard lists tend to skip. I often drop an auto-transcription into Reframer (Optimal Workshop). To split long stretches of speech, I anchor on certain speech patterns — "So…", "What I want to say is…", "Well…" — and tools like macOS Summarize or Word's AutoSummarize (since retired) could once shrink the text dramatically for a demo.

A note on audio quality, because it starts at the source. Record well from the very beginning: strip out noise later and you may strip out information along with it. If the quality off a phone plus an external mic and Morae Observer isn't enough, get a professional recorder — a Tascam DR-40, say, or a Zoom H1n. Good recorders clean the noise in real time on their own. Decent "ears" matter too; I use Jabra UC Voice 550 MS Duo headphones and a Jabra Speak 510 speaker. Recordings you already have can be improved by simply raising the volume and running auto-correction in iMovie, or with an equalizer on the principle that hiss = highs and hum = lows.

### When the numbers have to talk

Qualitative work has a quantitative shadow, and you should know where it falls. Take a common request: many online stores, each accumulating data — revenue, unit sales per SKU, prices, prices with promotions — and the question is which products respond best to new promotions, where the products may be *new* rather than part of the current range. Academically that's causality: several X time series, several Y time series, and a test of the causal link between them. But it needs a great deal of data, and I've never once seen it work in a business setting. Better to look toward simpler models — feature importance, random forests, maybe boosting. Or step away from the heavy machinery entirely and look at the classic metrics:

- **Traffic:** CPI, ROI, CAC (customer acquisition cost).
- **Monetisation:** ARPU, ARPPU, paying share, LTV, revenue (IAP, IAS, ad), gross, cash-in, C2/C3/C4 (conversion into the 2nd/3rd/4th payment from the previous one).
- **Virality:** k-factor.
- **Activity:** DAU (often seasonal — unique users per day) / MAU / WAU / LDAU, new users, users online. Facebook reports on MAU.
- **Retention:** retention, sticky factor, lifetime.

Almost all of the above are *aggregates* — some sum, division, or product — so it's crucial to understand the metric's *distribution*. Take the number of dishes a user bought on their first order: what matters is the structure of the purchase, the share of users who buy one dish, two, three, and so on.

![The distribution behind an aggregate metric](http://your-scorpion.ru/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/Group-60.png)

In the example above you can see that the two-dish bundle wasn't there from the start, and that will skew the metric.

Cohort metrics trip people up on step one, too. Counting a one-week ARPU, for instance: if a week runs 4–10 April, do you start on the 4th or the 11th? The answer is that you count per user. Someone arrived on 4 April? Then you count seven days from the 4th. Arrived on the 8th? Same again, seven days from that date. Sum it all and divide by the cohort size, and you have the average revenue in the seven days after the key action.

Willingness to pay deserves its own note, because it's a recurring question — in free-to-play, most users won't spend real money and will only watch ads, so *who* actually pays, and on what criteria? At bottom this is a simple problem of price elasticity. If you want to dig in, study the Van Westendorp method and conjoint analysis. Conjoint — "conjoint," as everyone calls it — is a quantitative way to gather and analyse data, and it's well suited to working out which *visual* characteristics a product or service should have. You list the characteristics (colour, shape, size) and their values (yellow / red / blue — circle / square / triangle — small / medium / large); all of them must be independent. One particular combination — a small yellow square — is a single *profile*. You hand the respondent a set of images and they pick the one they like most; remove that profile, have them choose from the rest, and repeat down to the last option. It assumes the person is rational and chooses sensibly and deliberately, which isn't always true. Conjoint comes in traditional and adaptive flavours.

Say we want to design loyalty cards for a fitness club. We'd settle on attributes: visiting hours, personal training, group programmes. If we just want to play with different sets of attributes, that's the traditional conjoint above. If we need fine tuning, that's adaptive conjoint, which yields understanding at both the attribute and the level. If one of the attributes is price, our pick is Choice-Based Conjoint: the respondent can first assemble their own set of attributes, after which we deal in generated variants plus price.

If you have the resources and the time, launch an A/B test and watch the effects — and define the success criterion up front. Take the *take rate*. It's like conversion rate, but where CR is computed as, say, 200 people clicking a link, 200 / 4,500 = .044, i.e. CR = 4.4%, take rate measures the share of visitors who showed *interest* in an action without actually completing it. After a couple of months of A/B testing you might well get a result like "average order value up ₽20 ± ₽30" — no statistical significance, the result is grey. And a couple of months after that, cannibalisation, which is fine if it's over a short horizon, the one on which we should be watching average order value anyway.

### A word on the field, and on ethics

One more thread runs through everything above: how you record research artifacts in the field. The paramount thing is not to lose the information, so the simplest instruments are the ones that count. Keep everything on several devices and in the cloud, because it's easy to lose a flash drive or kill a hard disk. The artifacts themselves are the interview transcripts; where transcription is impossible — forests, mountains, no electricity — everything gets written by hand in a notebook, then scanned or transcribed later. A microphone recording is fine; a video camera, less so. In many communities even photography grates when professional gear is involved, whereas snapping something on a phone provokes less irritation. And respondents must know that you're recording or filming them, on plain ethical grounds. American and European universities have ethics committees that make everyone sign a stack of documents, which constrains the researcher (most likely the anthropologist or sociologist) — so academic freedom is often "freedom" in quotation marks.

## In closing

And the most important thing of all. A researcher must never treat their own results as the final truth; there's always a chance of false positives or false negatives. So if the team declines to accept some of a researcher's recommendations, that isn't always a bad thing — they may be saving you from a blunder. And if your research pinpoints user needs flawlessly, every single time, then in all likelihood you're studying areas that are simply too easy and too obvious.

*Perfetto!*
